Module 04 · Making It Fast, Then Deploying It · Lesson 08

Speculative decoding turns idle compute into bandwidth you did not have

working 12 min Draft-and-verify, the acceptance formula, and the MTP layer the course model ships with. · Leviathan et al., 2022

The one-minute version

  • Decode reads the weights once per token, and the compute units wait. That idle compute is a resource.
  • Speculative decoding spends it: a cheap draft guesses several tokens, and the big model verifies all of them in one weight pass.
  • Expected tokens per pass is a closed-form function of the acceptance rate α and the draft length γ.
  • Mis-drafted tokens are thrown away, so output distribution is unchanged - this is a speed lever that is not a quality lever.

1. The arithmetic of acceptance

Let α be the probability the target model agrees with a drafted token - the acceptance rate. For a draft of γ tokens, the expected number of tokens certified per verify pass is (Leviathan et al., 2022):

E[tokens per pass] = (1 − αγ+1) ÷ (1 − α) Read as: γ: how many tokens the draft guesses ahead. α: how often the target keeps a drafted token. The whole sum is a truncated geometric series: you keep the first rejection's correction plus everything before it. At α = 1 the formula degenerates to γ + 1 - a perfect draft wins everything.

Two regimes matter on desk hardware. When α is high - code completion, structured formats, any text the target finds predictable - drafting pays enormously. When the model is being genuinely creative, α falls, and every rejected token wasted draft compute that was, crucially, free: the target's weight pass happens either way. That asymmetry is the whole trick. Speculative decoding spends compute you were not using; it never spends bandwidth twice.

2. MTP: the draft built into the model

A separate draft model is one option; the other is multi-token prediction. GLM-5.3-Flash ships with an MTP draft layer trained alongside the main network, so the draft is not a guess by a stranger - it is the target's own cheap opinion of its next few tokens (model card). The vLLM serving recipe enables it with a speculative config of five draft tokens (recipe). Self-drafting removes the second model from memory - precious capacity on a desk - and typically lifts α because the draft shares the target's representations.

The key insight On a DGX Spark the compute-to-bandwidth ratio is extreme: roughly a petaFLOP of FP4 behind 273 GB/s. Every lever in Lessons 03-07 shrank bytes per token; speculation is the only lever that spends the surplus FLOPs, and it is worth the most exactly where the wall from Lesson 01 is thickest.

3. What it costs

The draft adds latency engineering: batches must wait for the draft's guesses, verification must align KV caches between draft and target, and at very high concurrency the spare compute disappears - every FLOP is already busy serving real tokens, and speculation's surplus vanishes. On a desk box serving one or two streams, surplus is abundant; on a server maxing its batch, it is not. Rejections also burn a little energy and bookkeeping. And the guarantee deserves restating: rejections are replaced by the target's own choice, so the output distribution matches plain decoding. You are buying speed with idle compute, not trading quality for it.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

The draft model guesses 5 tokens; the target rejects the second. What ships to the user this step?
Verification keeps the accepted prefix and appends the target's own choice at the first disagreement - exactly one wasted draft, no more. Output tokens always come from the target's distribution.
Why is speculative decoding worth the most on a DGX Spark rather than a saturated eight-GPU server?
The lever spends idle compute. A server at high concurrency has converted its surplus into throughput already; a desk box decoding one stream has FLOPs standing idle behind 273 GB/s.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Your workload shows an acceptance rate of α = 0.8. You serve one stream at 10 tokens per second without drafting, and the draft costs a negligible share of the step.

Find the expected tokens per pass at γ = 3 and γ = 6, the resulting per-stream rates, and which draft length you would ship.

Hint

E = (1 − αγ+1) ÷ (1 − α). Compute for γ = 3 and 6, then multiply by 10.

Show the worked solution
  1. γ = 3. E = (1 − 0.8⁴) ÷ 0.2 = (1 − 0.4096) ÷ 0.2 ≈ 2.95 tokens per pass → ≈ 30 tokens/s.
  2. γ = 6. E = (1 − 0.8⁷) ÷ 0.2 ≈ 4.40 → ≈ 44 tokens/s.
  3. Decision. Longer drafts keep helping while α stays high, but per-step draft cost grows too; measure both on the real workload and watch the marginal gain from 3 to 6 against the draft's latency.

Sanity check. E must grow with γ and approach γ + 1 as α → 1; 2.95 < 4.40 < 7, as expected.
