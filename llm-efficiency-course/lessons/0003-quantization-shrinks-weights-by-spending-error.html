Module 02 · Shrinking the Model · Lesson 03

Quantization buys smaller files by spending rounding error

working 11 min The arithmetic of low-precision weights, and where the error actually hides. · QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023)

The one-minute version

  • Quantization maps the model's continuous weights onto a small grid of representable values.
  • A scale stretches the grid over each group of weights; rounding snaps each weight to the nearest grid point.
  • Fewer bits means a coarser grid, so more error per weight - but weights as a population tolerate this remarkably well.
  • The cost is not uniform: a few outlier channels carry outsized magnitudes, and careful formats handle them with finer groups rather than more bits.

1. The scale-and-round arithmetic

Take a group of weights - say 64 adjacent values in one tensor. Find the largest magnitude among them, choose how many bits you will spend, and derive a scale that stretches your grid across the group's range:

s = max(|w|) ÷ (2b−1 − 1)   ·   wq = round(w ÷ s) × s Read as: s: the scale - one grid spacing for this group. b: bits per weight, so a 4-bit grid holds 8 negative and 7 positive steps around zero. wq: the stored-then-restored weight. Every weight snaps to the nearest grid point, and the snap is the entire cost.

The error any single weight can carry is at most half a grid spacing, s ÷ 2. Halve the bits and the grid gets roughly eight times coarser at the same range, so per-weight error grows - yet the model's answers barely move. Why? Each output sums thousands of weights, and rounding errors that are independent and roughly zero-centred partially cancel in the sum. The network's computation averages the noise away.

2. Where the error hides: outliers

The picture breaks when one weight in a group is enormous. The scale must stretch to cover the largest member, so every ordinary weight in that group lands on a coarse grid built for a giant - like pricing a shopping basket where one item costs a house. Transformer models have a few channels with exactly these outlier magnitudes, and naive per-tensor scales waste their entire grid on them.

The fix is finer grouping, not more bits. Scale per 64-weight group instead of per tensor, and a giant outlier ruins only its own group. Modern formats push this further: NF4 assumes weights follow a normal distribution and places grid points where the weights actually are (QLoRA), and 4-bit floating formats such as NVFP4 keep a floating-point layout so huge dynamic ranges survive. Lesson 04 compares them as products; the mechanism here is the same: control where the grid's resolution goes.

An honest limit: the quality cost of 4-bit is real but model-specific, and for GLM-5.3-Flash no published perplexity or benchmark comparison of its FP8 and 4-bit checkpoints existed when this course was written. The mechanism on this page says why you should expect a small, uneven degradation - concentrated in rare, outlier-heavy computations - not a uniform dumbing-down. Verify on your own workload before trusting any quantization below 4 bits.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

One weight in a group of 64 is vastly larger than the rest. What happens to the other 63 under a shared scale?
The scale must cover the group's largest magnitude, so the grid spacing grows and the ordinary weights snap to coarser points. The fix is finer groups, not more bits.
Why do independent rounding errors mostly cancel across a layer's computation?
Rounding error is bounded by half a grid spacing and scattered in both directions across weights; a layer's outputs are large sums over those weights, so the noise partially averages away rather than accumulating coherently.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A group of 8 weights has magnitudes: 0.02, 0.05, 0.11, 0.04, 3.20, 0.03, 0.08, 0.06. You quantize to a 3-bit signed grid (steps at 0, ±s, ±2s, ±3s, ±4s where s = max ÷ 4).

Find the restored values of 0.05 and 3.20, and the relative error of the small weight.

Hint

s = 3.20 ÷ 4 = 0.8. Snap 0.05 to the nearest multiple of 0.8.

Show the worked solution
  1. Scale. s = 3.20 ÷ 4 = 0.8.
  2. Restore. 3.20 snaps to 3.2 × ... the grid point 4s = 3.2 exactly. The 0.05 snaps to 0 (nearest point is 0, not 0.8).
  3. Relative error. |0.05 − 0| ÷ 0.05 = 100% for the small weight; 0% for the giant.

Sanity check. The giant survives perfectly and the ordinary weights are destroyed - exactly the outlier pathology finer grouping exists to fix.
