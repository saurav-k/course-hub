Module 03 · Steering a Frozen Model · Lesson 06

QLoRA fine-tunes a model that does not fit unfrozen

working 12 min NF4, double quantization, and paged optimizers - the three ideas that put training on a desk. · QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023)

The one-minute version

  • LoRA removed the optimizer state from the bill, but the frozen base model still sits in memory at full precision.
  • QLoRA stores that frozen base in NF4 - a 4-bit format built for weight distributions - and trains the adapters in higher precision on top.
  • Double quantization shrinks even the scales, and paged optimizers spill training spikes to disk instead of crashing.
  • The bill: dequantization overhead during compute, and adapter quality trained against a 4-bit view of the world.

1. NF4: a grid built for weights

Lesson 03 established that a 4-bit grid has 15 or so usable points and that where you put them matters. NF4 - NormalFloat4 - places those points at the quantiles of a standard normal distribution, because trained weights pile up around zero the way a bell curve does (QLoRA paper). Every grid point then does useful work on real weight values instead of reserving resolution for regions weights rarely visit. QLoRA's published result is the load-bearing claim: a 4-bit NF4 base with adapters trained on top matches 16-bit full fine-tuning quality on its benchmarks - the storage saving is nearly free.

Double quantization attacks the scales themselves. Lesson 03's groups each carry a scale, and at 32 bits per group of 64 weights that overhead is a few percent of the file. Quantizing the scales in 8-bit blocks with a second-level scale shrinks that overhead by roughly half. On a 306 GiB-class model every percentage point is GiB you get back for KV cache.

Paged optimizers close the ledger's risk line: training memory is spiky, and a long-sequence batch can transiently demand more than the average. QLoRA borrows the operating system's virtual-memory idea and lets optimizer state page to disk during spikes instead of crashing the run. On a desk machine with a large swap, that is the difference between an overnight job and an out-of-memory error at step 900.

3. What it costs

Every dequantization is compute you would not spend at 16-bit: each group's NF4 values are expanded to working precision on the fly during the forward and backward passes. Training runs slower per step than the same LoRA run over a 16-bit base. And the adapters learn against a slightly quantized view of the base - QLoRA's benchmarks say the effect is small, but your task is not their benchmark. The discipline stays the same as Lesson 05: hold out examples, measure before and after, and let the measurement price the saving.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does NF4 spend its 15 grid points at a bell curve's quantiles rather than spacing them evenly?
Lesson 03's rule: the grid's resolution is the budget, and NF4 spends it where weights actually fall. Even spacing would waste points on empty regions of the range.
A training run crashes at step 900 with out-of-memory, though average memory seemed fine. Which QLoRA idea addresses exactly this?
Average memory can hide transient spikes; paged optimizers absorb them by paging optimizer state, trading speed on those steps for not dying. The other three shrink steady-state memory, not spikes.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You will QLoRA a 70B model on one 128 GB Spark. NF4 with overhead is 0.55 bytes per weight. Your KV budget for training sequences is 10 GB; adapters and optimizer state at rank 16 over Q and V total 3 GB; activations want 6 GB; the OS holds 6 GB.

Find whether the run fits, and the largest model (at 0.55 bytes per weight) that would.

Hint

Available for weights: 128 − 10 − 3 − 6 − 6 = 103 GB. Divide by 0.55.

Show the worked solution
  1. 70B check. 70 × 0.55 = 38.5 GB for weights. 38.5 + 25 ≈ 63.5 GB total - fits with room to spare.
  2. Largest model. 103 GB ÷ 0.55 ≈ 187 billion parameters.
  3. Reading it. The course model at 0.55 bytes per weight would want ~176 GB for weights alone - it does not fit a single Spark for training; Lesson 09's serving path is a different ledger.

Sanity check. The training limit must sit far below the serving limit for the same machine, because gradients, activations, and optimizer state join the bill - 187B versus the Lesson 09 serving math is exactly that gap.
