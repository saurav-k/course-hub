Module 04 · Making It Fast, Then Deploying It · Lesson 10

One Mac holds the whole model and never crosses a cable

frontier 11 min The unified-memory route, priced against the cluster - and a decision rule to close the course. · MLX

The one-minute version

  • A 256 GB Mac Studio holds the whole NVFP4 model in one memory pool - no cable, no pipeline bubble, no second node.
  • Its bandwidth is roughly three times a Spark's, so the Lesson 01 ceiling is three times higher per stream.
  • The costs: lower raw compute for the MTP draft, a CPU-first software stack (llama.cpp, MLX), and no second machine for redundancy.
  • The verdict rule: interactive single streams favour the Mac; fleet concurrency and Blackwell-native formats favour the Sparks.

1. The same ledger, one machine

Run Lesson 09's arithmetic with no cable. Usable memory: 256 GB minus the OS's share - call it 240 GB. The NVFP4 file at ~160 GiB derived leaves ~70 GB of KV budget, nearly identical to the cluster's, because the cluster's advantage was always capacity and the Mac matches it on one board. The KV lesson transfers unchanged: tens of modest concurrent sessions, not million-token monologues.

The differences start at the wall. At roughly 800 GB/s, the Lesson 01 division for an 18B-active model at 4 bits reads 800 ÷ 9 ≈ 89 tokens per second ideal - about three times the Spark ceiling - with the same realistic fraction off. Unified memory is also simply simpler: no pipeline bubble to fill, no activation serialisation across a link, no cluster networking to configure. The software path is llama.cpp with a GGUF quant or Apple's MLX (llama.cpp, MLX) - mature, fast-moving, and CPU-first rather than Blackwell-native.

2. What the Mac gives up

Three things, priced honestly. Draft compute: MTP speculation spends FLOPs, and the Mac's FP4-class matrix throughput is modest next to GB10's petaFLOP claim - the same acceptance gain buys less. Format nativeness: GGUF integer kernels and MLX's Metal path are excellent but are not Blackwell FP4 units; the Spark runs the model's native formats on native silicon. Redundancy and growth: one machine is one failure domain, and adding capacity means buying a whole new board rather than a third 128 GB node.

An honest limit: Mac Studio bandwidth figures for the newest Ultra silicon vary by configuration and generation, and no published GLM-5.3-Flash benchmark on any Mac existed when this course was written. Treat the ~800 GB/s class figure and every rate derived from it as a planning number; the Resources file lists what would settle it.

3. The verdict rule

Close the course with the decision the whole stack was built for. If your workload is interactive streams - one or two people, or agents that think in long solo turns - the Mac's bandwidth per stream wins, and its zero-configuration single box is an operational gift. If your workload is a fleet - several agents sharing aggregate decode, or any workload that wants Blackwell-native FP4 and MTP drafting at full width - the two-Spark cluster wins on silicon even at equal memory. And if you cannot decide, remember Lesson 03: the file is the same on both. The real commitment is not the hardware; it is the format, and formats travel.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Both machines hold the same 160 GiB file with ~70 GB of KV budget. What actually separates them?
Capacity is a wash; the difference is the shape of the speed: the Mac multiplies its bandwidth per stream, the Spark spends surplus FP4 compute on drafting across a batch. Workload mix, not memory, picks the machine.
Your six-agent fleet spends most of its time in long solo thinking turns. Which desk wins, and why?
Long solo turns are exactly the bandwidth-bound regime the Mac's 800 GB/s dominates. The fleet's aggregate still exists, but if most time is spent as one-stream latency, per-stream bandwidth is the metric that pays.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Your week: half the tokens go to one developer interacting with one agent (solo streaming), half go to a six-agent fleet running in parallel. Assume MTP delivers a 2.5x acceptance gain on the Spark only, and use the lesson's ideal ceilings of 30 and 89 tokens per second with a realistic fraction of one third.

Find each machine's weekly average rate across the mix, and your call.

Hint

Mac: same rate for both halves. Spark: solo rate, and fleet rate × 2.5 from drafting. Average with 0.5 weights.

Show the worked solution
  1. Mac. Realistic ≈ 30 tok/s in both halves; average ≈ 30.
  2. Sparks. Solo ≈ 10; fleet ≈ (20 ÷ 6 per stream) × 2.5 ≈ 8 per stream, but the fleet's aggregate ≈ 20 × 2.5 = 50 tok/s. Time-weighted per-stream: (10 + 8) ÷ 2 ≈ 9.
  3. Call. For solo-weighted work, the Mac's average wins; for aggregate fleet throughput the drafted Spark pool wins. The honest answer is the mix decides - which is the lesson.

Sanity check. Any correct averaging must sit between each machine's solo and fleet rates, and neither machine can dominate both halves of the mix at once.
