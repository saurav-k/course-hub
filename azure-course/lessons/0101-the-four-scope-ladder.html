Module 01 · The lay of the land · Lesson 0101

Azure hangs everything from one directory, and a rule reaches down the tree below wherever you attach it

foundation 11 min Capability area org-tenancy, row org-hierarchy. · Management groups overview

The one-minute version

  • One Microsoft Entra tenant owns the whole estate. A subscription trusts exactly one directory, and that relationship is what makes it yours.
  • Between the tenant and a subscription sits a tree of management groups, whose only job is to be a place to attach a rule that should reach many subscriptions.
  • A subscription is the billing and quota cell. A resource group is the lifecycle container. They are different kinds of boundary and swapping them is the classic first mistake.
  • Rules flow down the tree and telemetry flows up it, so the scope you pick decides both blast radius and what you can see.
  • The tree has hard edges: 10,000 management groups per directory, six levels below the root, and one parent per node.

The tree, and the two directions things move in it

The root of the tree is a special node worth knowing by name. Its display name is Tenant root group and its identifier is the tenant identifier itself. It cannot be moved or deleted, every new subscription lands directly under it by default, and - the part that surprises people - nobody has access to it by default. A Global Administrator has to elevate themselves to User Access Administrator on it first, after which they can assign roles across the entire estate.

That elevation is the emergency brake, and it is also the single most consequential action in an Azure tenant: anything assigned at the root reaches every subscription in the directory. It is worth an alert of its own long before anyone needs it.

What each rung is actually for

ScopeWhat it isWhat a mistake here reaches
Tenant The Entra directory: users, groups, service principals, and the trust relationships Every subscription and every identity in the organisation
Management group A governance container with no resources of its own Every subscription in the branch below it, by inheritance
Subscription The billing boundary and the quota envelope Everything deployed inside it
Resource group A lifecycle container: things created and destroyed together Its members only - and deleting the group deletes them all
Resource The service instance itself Itself

Two of these rungs are easy to conflate and behave nothing alike. A subscription carries the quota: capacity limits are counted per region and per family inside it, and raising one is a request rather than a setting. A resource group carries no quota at all and carries a lifetime instead. So "we are running out of room" and "we want to tear this down in one action" are answered at different levels, and an estate that uses one subscription per application for lifecycle reasons has bought itself a quota conversation it did not need.

The reframing Management groups are not folders for tidiness. They are the only place a rule can be attached above a subscription, so the tree's shape is a governance decision made in advance of the workloads, not a filing decision made after them. Module 09 builds the standard skeleton on top of exactly this.

What one scope can carry

A second limit shapes the tree itself rather than what hangs on it. A directory supports up to 10,000 management groups and six levels of depth below the root, with every node having exactly one parent. Six levels sounds generous until an organisation tries to encode geography, business unit, environment and data classification as separate levels, at which point it has spent four of them before reaching a workload.

Moving a subscription is harder than it looks

One more rule about custom roles is worth carrying now, because it decides where data-plane permissions can live: a custom role that carries DataActions cannot be assigned at management-group scope at all. Data-plane grants therefore start at the subscription and go down, never across a whole branch in one assignment.

And a caveat about what inheritance actually evaluates. A policy may be assigned at management-group scope, but only resources at subscription or resource-group level are evaluated by it. The management groups themselves are not policy targets. That distinction is invisible in a compliance dashboard until someone writes a rule about management groups and watches it report nothing.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A subscription must move from one management group to another. Which set of permissions does Resource Manager require?
All three objects need write permission, with an exemption when one of them is the root group. The first and second options each name one of the three real checks and stop early, which is exactly how the move fails in practice. The fourth over-corrects into an elevation nobody should hold routinely.
Which statement describes what a resource group is for, as opposed to a subscription?
A group is a lifecycle container and deleting it deletes its members. The second option describes the subscription's quota envelope, the third describes the subscription and tenant relationship, and the fourth describes a management group. Each is true of a different rung, which is the confusion the question tests.
A policy is assigned at a management group and reports no compliance data for the management groups below it. Why?
A policy can be assigned at management-group scope, but the objects it evaluates are resources at subscription or resource-group level. The first option names a real 24-hour cycle that would delay data, not remove it. The second inverts inheritance. The third invents a root-specific exclusion that does not exist.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An organisation wants its management-group tree to encode four things, each as its own level: geography, then business unit, then environment, then data classification. Under the last level it wants one management group per application, and then the application's subscriptions.

Work out whether that design fits, using the documented depth limit, and say what you would drop first if it does not.

Hint

Count levels below the root, and remember that subscriptions sit under the deepest management group rather than occupying a level of the budget themselves.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write down the budget. A directory allows six levels of management-group depth below the root group.
  2. Count what the design asks for. Geography is level 1, business unit is 2, environment is 3, data classification is 4, and one group per application is 5. That is five of the six.
  3. Check the fit. It fits, with one level spare, and the subscriptions hang beneath level 5. But a single spare level is a thin margin for an organisation that will later want a regulatory sub-branch or a separate sandbox lane.
  4. Decide what to drop. Drop the level that can be expressed another way. Environment and data classification are both properties a policy can test through tags or resource properties, and application identity is what subscription vending stamps on a subscription. Geography and business unit are the two that genuinely need to differ in inherited policy, so they are the two worth spending depth on.

Sanity check. Your answer should end with fewer levels than you started with, and every level you kept should have a rule that is genuinely different at that level. A level with the same policy assignment as its parent is a folder, and folders belong in tags.
