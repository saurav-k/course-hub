Policy answers after authorisation, and a parent's deny cannot be softened below it

working 12 min Capability area org-tenancy, row org-guardrail-policy. · Azure Policy

The one-minute version

  • Policy evaluates independently of role-based access control. A fully authorised create is still refused if the resulting resource breaks a rule.
  • Its effects go beyond refusing: deny, audit, modify, deployIfNotExists and DenyAction, which blocks an operation such as deletion whatever the caller was granted.
  • It is an explicit-deny system. A child scope cannot be made more permissive than its parent - the only softeners are exclusions and exemptions.
  • Policy cannot grant anything. Granting is role-based access control's job, and the two systems never substitute for each other.
  • Compliance is evaluated on change and on a twenty-four hour cycle, so a compliance view is a recent picture rather than a live one.

Five effects, and only one of them just says no

The reframing Policy is an explicit-deny system, and inheritance runs one way. A deny assigned at a management group cannot be made permissive by anything assigned below it. The only two ways out are exclusions, which remove scopes from an assignment, and exemption objects, which are first-class resources rather than a quiet edit. That asymmetry is what makes a guardrail worth asserting at the top of the tree: nobody below can argue with it in configuration.

What deploying a missing thing actually requires

When evaluation happens

Two clocks run. A resource is evaluated when it changes, which is what makes a deny effect feel immediate, and the whole estate is re-evaluated on a twenty-four hour compliance cycle, which is what populates the compliance view. So a resource created outside a rule's reach and later brought into scope shows as compliant until the cycle catches it, and a compliance dashboard is best read as a recent picture rather than a live one.

Policy carries object limits that shape a design: 200 assignments per scope, 500 definitions per scope, 200 initiatives per scope, 1,000 policies per initiative, 1,000 exemptions per scope, and 20 parameters per definition. An estate that assigns one policy per rule at one scope meets the assignment limit long before it runs out of rules, which is the practical argument for grouping rules into initiatives rather than assigning them individually.

Two things policy is often asked to do and cannot

One further absence belongs here. There is no dedicated stream of policy denials: a refusal appears in the control-plane record as a failed operation naming the assignment that blocked it, which module 10 comes back to. So "show me everything policy blocked this week" is a query over that record rather than a report the engine produces.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A deny policy is assigned at a management group. A team assigns a permissive policy on their own subscription. What happens?
Policy is an explicit-deny system and inheritance is one-way. The first and third options import a precedence model that does not exist here. The fourth confuses the evaluation cycle, which decides when compliance is reported, with the enforcement, which is immediate on change.
A governance team holds Contributor and cannot make a new policy take effect. What is missing?
Contributor can trigger remediation but cannot create or update definitions and assignments, and wiring the managed identity needs User Access Administrator. The third option states the opposite of what Contributor can do. The first and second invent structural requirements.
Which of these is outside what Azure Policy can express?
Category-level filtering inside a diagnostic setting is outside the engine. The other three are the deny, modify and deployIfNotExists effects respectively, and all three are documented capabilities.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A platform team must deliver four rules across an estate: resources may only be created in two named regions; every resource must carry a cost-centre tag; every resource that can emit diagnostics must send them somewhere; and one production resource group must be impossible to delete. They plan one policy assignment per rule at each of forty subscriptions, and the team holds Contributor across the estate.

Assess the plan: choose an effect for each rule, say what is wrong with the assignment strategy, and name the permission problem.

Hint

Pick the least disruptive effect that actually achieves each rule, then count assignments against the per-scope limit, then ask who can create an assignment at all.

Show the worked solution
  1. Choose the effect for regions. This is a refusal about a property of the request, so deny. There is nothing to fix up - a resource in the wrong region should not be created.
  2. Choose the effect for the tag. Modify, not deny. The rule is satisfiable by adding the tag, and refusing a deployment for a missing label makes a governance team unpopular for no gain in compliance.
  3. Choose the effect for diagnostics. deployIfNotExists. Refusing a resource for lacking a diagnostic setting would block work, and auditing it would leave the estate uninstrumented. Deploying the missing setting is exactly the case this effect exists for.
  4. Choose the effect for the protected resource group. DenyAction, which blocks the operation regardless of what the caller was granted. Note that lesson 0801's deny assignments are a different mechanism that reaches the same outcome from the authorisation side.
  5. Fix the assignment strategy. Four assignments times forty subscriptions is 160 assignments to maintain, and every future rule multiplies by forty again. Assign at a management group instead, so the rules inherit downward, and group them into an initiative so that one assignment carries all four. That also keeps the estate away from the 200-assignments-per-scope limit.
  6. Name the permission problem. Contributor cannot create or update definitions or assignments, and the modify and deployIfNotExists rules act through a managed identity whose permissions must be granted by a User Access Administrator. So the team can operate this once it exists and cannot introduce it, and that is a conversation to have before the work starts.

Sanity check. You should have used four different effects, and your final assignment count should be one rather than 160. If two rules ended up with deny, look again at the tagging rule - a rule that can be satisfied automatically should be.
