Choosing a table plan is choosing whether you pay to store or to read

working 12 min Capability area observability, row log-store, with service-specific-log from audit-telemetry. · Azure Monitor Logs cost

The one-minute version

  • Three table plans are mixable within one workspace: Analytics, where interactive queries are not charged by scan; Basic, cheaper to ingest and charged per gigabyte scanned; and Auxiliary, cheapest to ingest and queryable across the whole retention.
  • The default is thirty-day analytics retention, and thirty-one days are bundled with the ingestion price - so lowering retention below thirty-one saves nothing at all.
  • Three table families ride free at ninety days: usage, the exported control-plane record, and the application telemetry tables.
  • Retention extends to 730 days interactive and to a twelve-year total, read back through search jobs or a temporary restore.
  • A few streams dwarf everything else. Full Kubernetes audit on a large cluster is the classic bill shock, and the documented mitigation is a narrower category on a cheaper plan.

Three plans, and what each one trades

The retention ladder

The reframing A restore is billed by capacity and time, with minimums. Bringing long-term data back into a hot cache carries a minimum size and a minimum duration - two terabytes and twelve hours - so restoring a small slice of old data is priced as if it were a large one. That makes a search job the ordinary tool for looking into the past, and a restore something you do when you need to work with the data repeatedly.

The levers, in the order worth pulling them

Two constraints bite when the levers are pulled. A table plan change is rate-limited to once every seven days on the structured firewall tables, so plan experiments are slow rather than instant. And some features require the Analytics plan - the firewall's own policy analytics among them - so moving a table to Basic can silently disable a capability somebody else depends on. Check what reads a table before you change how it is billed.

Two more things that meter

Continuous data export bills per exported byte, so the durable path from lesson 1000 has a running cost proportional to volume rather than a flat one. And a workspace with a security product enabled bills security meters on top of the ingestion meters, which is the argument for keeping operations and security data in separate workspaces - unless the combined volume buys a better commitment tier, in which case the arithmetic can go the other way. Rows marked as non-billable never charge, and the free-riding tables above are the main population of those.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A team lowers a workspace table's retention from thirty days to seven to save money. What happens?
Thirty-one days are bundled, so anything below that is a pure loss of history for no saving. The first option assumes retention and ingestion are priced separately throughout. The second describes the effect of a plan change rather than a retention change. The third invents a floor that is not the issue.
A Kubernetes cluster's audit stream is the largest line on the bill. What is the documented first move?
Narrowing the category removes the read verbs that make the full audit stream so large, and it costs nothing. The second buys the volume rather than reducing it. The third saves nothing below thirty-one days. The fourth moves the cost rather than changing it.
Which table belongs on the Basic plan?
Basic trades cheaper ingestion for scan-priced queries, which suits high volume that is rarely read. The first would pay a scan charge continuously. The second has nothing to save. The fourth is about total retention, which is a different dial from the plan.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A workspace has grown until it is the largest single line on an estate's bill. Its contents are: full Kubernetes audit from three large clusters, firewall logs in the structured tables, application telemetry, the exported control-plane record, and a security product enabled on the same workspace. Retention is set to fourteen days everywhere on the grounds of cost, and a compliance rule requires the control-plane record to be kept for seven years. Somebody proposes buying a commitment tier.

Work the levers: say what is wrong with the retention setting, name the changes you would make in order, and say how the seven-year requirement is met.

Hint

Start with the setting that is costing money and saving none. Then sort the streams by how often anybody actually reads them, and only reach for the commitment tier once the volume is what it is going to be.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix the retention setting first. Fourteen days saves nothing, because thirty-one days come with the ingestion price. The estate is paying for thirty-one days of retention and keeping fourteen, so raising it to thirty-one is free and restores two weeks of history.
  2. Check what is already free. The exported control-plane record and the application telemetry tables ride free at ninety days, so neither is contributing to the problem in the way the team probably assumes.
  3. Narrow the largest stream. Full Kubernetes audit records every verb, including reads. Switching to the admin-only category removes that bulk at no cost in the audit questions most estates actually ask, and it is the documented mitigation.
  4. Move the remaining audit volume to a cheaper plan. An audit table read only during investigations is a Basic-plan table. Note the constraint before doing it: a plan change on the structured firewall tables is limited to once every seven days, and some firewall features require the Analytics plan - so check what reads those tables first.
  5. Separate the security product. A workspace with the security product enabled bills its meters on top. Splitting operations and security data stops the operational volume being metered twice, unless the combined volume would buy a better commitment tier - which is arithmetic to do after the reductions, not before.
  6. Only now consider a commitment tier. Commitments start at 100 gigabytes a day. Buying one before the reductions locks in the volume you were trying to remove.
  7. Meet the seven-year requirement. Seven years is beyond the 730-day interactive ceiling, so it is total retention on that table, read back with a search job or a temporary restore. Remember the restore minimums - two terabytes and twelve hours - which make a search job the right tool for an occasional lookup.

Sanity check. Your first action should increase a retention number rather than decrease one, and the commitment tier should be last rather than first. If the seven-year requirement ended up as an interactive retention setting, it exceeds the ceiling by nearly four times.
