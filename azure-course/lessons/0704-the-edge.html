Four ways in, and the scope of each one decides the design

working 12 min Capability area networking-lb-edge, rows l4-load-balancer, l7-load-balancer, global-front-door, ddos-protection and gateway-load-balancer. · Azure Front Door

The one-minute version

  • Traffic Manager steers in DNS, so its failover speed is bounded by the profile's time to live and by what clients have already cached.
  • Front Door is global anycast ingress with caching, a rules engine, a web application firewall and origin health steering - and it is the current answer for content delivery.
  • Application Gateway v2 is the regional layer-seven proxy: listeners, rules, backend pools and probes, autoscaling, zone-redundant, with an optional firewall of its own.
  • Load Balancer is layer four and pass-through, so it preserves the client address. Its Basic tier has been retired, leaving the Standard and Gateway forms.
  • DDoS Network Protection is enabled on the virtual network, not on an address or an interface - and a platform layer of protection is always on and free underneath it.

The four scopes

Why DNS steering fails over more slowly than anycast

The reframing Azure CDN is a transition story, not a choice. The legacy content-delivery products were built on partner networks, one of which shut down, and Microsoft directs content-delivery needs to Front Door. Front Door itself has a classic tier with an announced retirement, so "Front Door" in a current design means the Standard or Premium surface. Its Private Link origin support is the detail that matters most for the previous lesson: the public edge can reach a backend that has no public exposure at all.

Inserting an appliance without re-addressing anything

DDoS Network Protection is enabled on the virtual network, not on a public address or an interface, and a free always-on platform layer sits underneath it protecting the infrastructure regardless. The paid plan is what adds policies tuned to your traffic, its own telemetry, and cost credits for the resources consumed during an attack. A design that "enabled DDoS protection on the public IP" has configured nothing, and the mistake survives review because the free layer means nothing visibly fails.

The apex problem, and the record that solves it

Azure DNS public zones are authoritative hosting for a domain, and their alias records are what let the root of a domain point at a Traffic Manager profile, a Front Door endpoint or another Azure resource - the case ordinary aliasing cannot express. Two limits belong beside them: the default record-set quota per zone is published in the vendor's limits table rather than on the overview page, and there is no per-query logging for public zones, so query visibility has to come from a resolver you control, which is the subject of module 10.

Which pairing does the corpus describe as a common shape rather than a choice between rivals?
The global product finds the region and the regional layer-seven product delivers inside it, which is why the two are frequently paired. The first is possible but is not the documented pairing. The third has the scopes inverted. The fourth is not a shape the corpus describes.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A retailer serves customers on two continents. Requirements: static assets should be served near the customer; requests must be routed to a regional deployment and, inside the region, split by path between a storefront and an API; the origins must not be reachable from the public internet; a third-party inspection appliance must sit in the path for a regulated subset of traffic; and the site must survive losing one region. Somebody proposes Traffic Manager plus one Application Gateway per region, and nothing else.

Rebuild it: place each requirement at a scope, say what the proposal cannot do, and name what changes about the origins.

Hint

Take the requirements one at a time and ask, for each, whether it is decided before the connection exists, at the edge, inside the region, or at the instance. Two of the five cannot be answered by anything in the proposal.

Show the worked solution
  1. Place the caching requirement. Serving assets near the customer is caching at a global edge, which is Front Door. The proposal has nothing at that scope, so this requirement is unmet.
  2. Place the path split. Splitting between a storefront and an API by path is regional layer-seven routing, which the proposed Application Gateway does. This one the proposal meets.
  3. Place the origin exposure requirement. Origins that must not be publicly reachable is what Front Door's Private Link origin support is for. Under the proposal the gateways have to be publicly reachable for Traffic Manager to send clients to them, so this requirement is not merely unmet - the proposal actively conflicts with it.
  4. Place the appliance. A third-party appliance in the path without re-addressing the application is the Gateway form of the load balancer, chained at the public endpoint. Nothing in the proposal covers it.
  5. Place the regional survival requirement. Both a DNS profile and a global anycast front door can steer away from a failed region, but they differ in speed: the profile is bounded by its time to live, and the anycast product is not. If seconds matter, the requirement lands on Front Door.
  6. State the rebuilt shape and what changed. Front Door globally, with caching, health steering and Private Link origins, and an Application Gateway per region behind it doing the path split. The Gateway load balancer chains the inspection appliance into the regulated path. The origins change from publicly reachable gateways to backends reached over Private Link, which is the single largest difference from the proposal.

Sanity check. Your rebuilt design should have exactly one global component and one regional layer-seven component per region. If Traffic Manager survived in your answer, ask what it is deciding that the anycast layer is not - on this set of requirements the honest answer is nothing.
