A budget tells you, and never stops you

working 11 min Capability area org-tenancy, rows cost-management, commitment-discounts and cost-advisory. · Cost Management and Billing

The one-minute version

  • Budgets alert. They never stop spend. Stopping requires something you build on top of the alert, using policy or automation.
  • Cost data has two views, amortized and actual, and which one is being read changes what a month appears to have cost when a commitment was bought up front.
  • A reservation commits to a specific resource type in a region. Its discount only lands while a matching resource is running, so the flexibility setting and the scope decide how much of it you actually get.
  • A savings plan commits to an hourly compute spend that flexes across eligible services and regions - the same trade made the other way round.
  • Azure Advisor is the recommendation surface, scored against the Well-Architected pillars, and it is a separate product from the one that holds the billing data.

What the cost product actually gives you

What happens when a budget is breached

The reframing Two commitment shapes, and they trade the same thing in opposite directions. A reservation commits to a specific resource type in a region: the deepest discount, and it only pays off while something matching is actually running. A savings plan commits to an hourly compute spend that flexes across eligible services and regions: less specific, and far more forgiving of an estate that changes shape. Both come in one-year and three-year terms, paid up front or monthly.

Why a reservation can be bought and not received

Azure Advisor sits beside all of this as the recommendation layer, scored against the Well-Architected pillars and covering reliability, security, performance and operational excellence as well as cost. Its cost recommendations name idle and under-used resources and suggest commitment purchases. Recommendations can be filtered by subscription, postponed, dismissed and read through the interface for reporting - and it is a separate product from the one holding the billing data, which is why the two disagree about what is interesting.

Two caveats belong on any cost design. Some cost features are not available at management-group scope under the Microsoft Customer Agreement, so a reporting model that assumes every view rolls up the hierarchy should be checked against the scopes actually supported. And this course quotes no prices at all: the shapes above are stable and the numbers are not, so the vendor's own pricing and cost pages are the authority for any figure.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A subscription passes its budget on the fifteenth of the month. What does the platform do?
A budget is a threshold with a notification, and stopping requires policy or automation you wrote. The first and second describe controls the budget does not have. The fourth invents a billing behaviour that does not exist.
A reservation was bought and the saving is smaller than expected. What should be checked?
A reservation's discount only lands on matching, in-scope usage, so those two settings and the running resources are the place to look. The second option changes how cost is presented rather than how much discount landed. The third and fourth change the rate and the payment shape, not whether the discount applied.
What does a savings plan commit to?
The commitment is an hourly compute spend with flexibility across eligible services and regions. The first describes a reservation, which is the other half of this pair. The third and fourth invent commitment shapes that neither product offers.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A company has a steady base of virtual machines that has run unchanged for two years, plus a platform team that is midway through moving several workloads from machines to containers and from one region to another over the next year. Finance wants predictable spend, has set budgets on every subscription, and has been told that budgets will "keep teams within their allocation". Somebody proposes three-year reservations for the whole compute estate.

Advise them: say what the budgets actually give them, choose a commitment shape for each half of the estate, and name the alert you would arm on day one.

Hint

Split the estate by how confident you are that the same resource will still be running next year. That single question chooses between the two commitment shapes, and it also tells you what the budget cannot do.

Show the worked solution
  1. Correct the budget expectation. A budget raises an alert when a threshold is crossed and stops nothing. Keeping teams within an allocation needs something built on the alert - a policy that denies further creation, or automation that acts - and none of that exists yet.
  2. Split the estate. One half has run unchanged for two years, so the same resource type in the same region is very likely to still be running. The other half is being deliberately changed in both service shape and region over the next year.
  3. Choose for the stable half. Reservations. The commitment is to a specific resource type in a region, which is exactly what this half is, and it is the deeper discount of the two.
  4. Choose for the changing half. A savings plan. Committing to an hourly compute spend that flexes across eligible services and regions survives a workload moving from machines to containers and from one region to another, and a reservation would not - the resources it was matched against will stop existing.
  5. Reject the original proposal. Three-year reservations across the whole estate commits the changing half to matching resources it is actively planning to retire. The commitment is paid for regardless and simply goes unused.
  6. Arm the alert. Reservation utilisation alerts, on the day of purchase. The failure this design is exposed to is a commitment that quietly stops matching, and nothing else in the product complains about it.
  7. Note the term choice. Both shapes offer one-year and three-year terms. The stable half can defend three years; the changing half should not be asked to predict three years of an estate it is redesigning.

Sanity check. Your answer should use both commitment shapes rather than one, split by confidence in the resource still existing. If a single shape covered everything, the migration half has been assumed to stay still, which is the one thing it is guaranteed not to do.
