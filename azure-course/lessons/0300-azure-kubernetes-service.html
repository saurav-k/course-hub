AKS runs the control plane for free and leaves the nodes where you can see them

working 10 min Capability area containers, row managed-kubernetes, with kubernetes-node-autoscaler beside it. · Azure Kubernetes Service

The one-minute version

  • Azure runs the Kubernetes control plane and you keep the node pools. The control plane is free; a paid uptime service level agreement is the optional part.
  • Two tiers exist and they differ in how much is decided for you: Automatic ships opinionated defaults including monitoring, and Standard leaves the choices open.
  • A node pool is where the compute decisions from module 02 reappear: sizes, availability zones, Spot capacity and GPU families are all node-pool properties.
  • Just-in-time node provisioning is available as node autoprovisioning, which is Karpenter-based and is a mode of the cluster rather than a separate product to install.
  • The cost that surprises people is not the cluster. It is audit logging, whose volume on a busy cluster dwarfs every other stream.

The line Azure draws

Two tiers, and what they decide for you

TierWhat it is
AutomaticAn opinionated cluster: defaults are chosen for you, monitoring among them. Managed Prometheus metric collection is on by default here.
StandardThe open form: you choose the add-ons, the monitoring and the node-pool layout yourself.

The tier is a statement about who makes the decisions rather than about capability, and it is worth choosing consciously. A team that wants Kubernetes because it wants control will find Automatic's defaults arriving as surprises, and a team that wants Kubernetes because everything ships as a container will find Standard's blank slate expensive.

The reframing The control plane being free changes what a cluster costs to think about. On AKS the meaningful bill is the node pools plus the telemetry, not the cluster object, which is why the sizing conversation for a cluster is the same conversation as for a scale set and the observability conversation is the one people forget to have.

Nodes that appear because a pod needs them

The cost cliff nobody budgets for

AKS emits its control-plane logs as resource-log categories, and they are not equal in volume. The categories include kube-audit, kube-audit-admin, kube-apiserver, kube-controller-manager, kube-scheduler, cluster-autoscaler, guard and the container-storage controllers. Of these, kube-audit is the one that dominates a bill on a busy cluster.

Two facts make this worse than it looks. The audit policy is fixed by AKS, so you cannot turn the verbosity down - the only lever is which category you route. And kube-audit captures every verb, while kube-audit-admin excludes the read-shaped ones. The documented mitigation on the AKS monitoring page is to route kube-audit-admin instead and to hold the audit table on the Basic log plan. Decide this when the cluster is created, because the alternative is discovering it on an invoice.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A team wants just-in-time nodes sized from pending pods on AKS. What do they install?
Node autoprovisioning is Karpenter-based and built into the cluster as a mode, so there is nothing separate to run. The first option describes how the same engine is consumed elsewhere. The third and fourth describe fixed-pool and fleet mechanisms that do not size a node from a pending pod.
Which statement about AKS costs matches how the service is packaged?
The control plane is free, an uptime service level agreement is the paid option, and the real spend is node pools and telemetry. The first option inverts the packaging. The second is the specific mistake this page warns about: audit volume is the classic surprise. The third bills a free thing.
Why can you not simply reduce AKS audit-log verbosity to control its volume?
AKS fixes the audit policy, so the design choice is which category to route - kube-audit-admin excludes the read-shaped verbs - and which log plan holds it. The second and third options put the policy in places it does not live, and the fourth overstates the constraint into a claim about every managed Kubernetes service.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A platform team is creating its first production AKS cluster. It will run a mix of ordinary services, a nightly model-training job that needs accelerators, and a large batch tier that tolerates interruption. Compliance requires that administrative changes to the cluster be auditable for a year.

Sketch the cluster: how many node pools and why, which tier you would choose and what it commits you to, and how you would satisfy the audit requirement without the volume that usually accompanies it.

Hint

Node pools differ by the properties of their machines, so count the distinct machine requirements first. Then read the audit requirement carefully: it asks about administrative changes, not about every read.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the distinct machine requirements. Ordinary services want general-purpose sizes; training wants GPU sizes; the batch tier wants interruptible capacity. That is three node pools, because size family and capacity type are pool properties rather than pod properties.
  2. Place the discount. Spot capacity is available to node pools, and the batch tier is the workload that tolerates losing members - the same reasoning as the fleet page in module 02. The services pool stays on ordinary capacity.
  3. Choose the tier deliberately. Automatic brings opinionated defaults including monitoring, which suits a team that wants the platform decided; Standard leaves the add-ons and layout open, which suits a team that has opinions about them. A first production cluster with three differently shaped pools is a design with opinions, so Standard is the honest choice - and it makes the monitoring decision explicit rather than inherited.
  4. Satisfy the audit requirement cheaply. Route kube-audit-admin rather than kube-audit: it excludes the read-shaped verbs, which are the bulk of the volume and are not what the requirement asked for. Hold the audit table on the Basic log plan, and take the one-year retention from the log workspace's retention settings rather than from the ingest volume.
  5. Note what you cannot tune. The audit policy itself is fixed by AKS, so if kube-audit-admin still proves too coarse there is no verbosity dial to reach for - the remaining levers are the log plan and the retention.

Sanity check. Your answer should have three pools and exactly one of them on Spot. If the GPU pool ended up on Spot to save money on training, check whether a nightly job that must complete can survive losing its accelerators mid-run.
