Archive is not a cheaper tier, it is a tier you cannot read from

working 10 min Capability area storage, row archive-storage. · Access tiers overview

The one-minute version

  • Blob Storage has four access tiers - hot, cool, cold and archive - and the tier is a property of the blob rather than of the account.
  • Lifecycle rules move blobs between tiers automatically, driven by last-modified or last-accessed time, so ageing is configuration rather than a job you write.
  • Archive is different in kind. Data in it must be rehydrated before it can be read, and rehydration to hot or cool takes up to fifteen hours.
  • A high-priority rehydration is faster and is billed extra, which makes recovery speed a purchase decision made during an incident.
  • Beside the tiers sits a stack of protections - soft delete, versioning, point-in-time restore, immutability, change feed and object replication - and they answer different failures.

Four tiers and the rules that move between them

What the wait actually looks like

The tier is set per blob, so an "archive account" does not exist and a lifecycle rule that matches the wrong prefix archives exactly the blobs it matched and nothing warns you. Test a lifecycle rule against a copy of the naming convention before it runs against the real container, because the cost of getting it wrong is not data loss - it is fifteen hours per mistake.

The protections, and which failure each one answers

Immutable storage is the one with a governance flavour rather than an operational one. A legal hold or a time-based retention policy makes data write-once and read-many, so it cannot be modified or deleted while the policy stands - including by the people who would otherwise be allowed to.

The reframing Tiering and protection pull in opposite directions and both are configured on the same data. Cheaper tiers make data slower to reach; protections make data harder to lose. A retention design has to state which of the two the data is in for - because "archived for seven years" and "recoverable within the hour" cannot both be true of the same copy.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A recovery plan states a four-hour recovery time objective and names archived blobs as the source. What is wrong?
Standard-priority rehydration can take up to fifteen hours, which cannot meet a four-hour objective; high priority is faster and costs more. The first option inverts the per-blob granularity. The second invents a data-loss behaviour. The fourth would make archive a one-way trip, which it is not.
Which protection answers "an application overwrote good data with bad data"?
Versioning keeps prior versions, which is exactly the overwrite case. Object replication would faithfully copy the bad data. A retention policy prevents the overwrite rather than recovering from one, and would have to have been in place first. Change feed tells you what happened without giving the content back.
What drives a lifecycle rule's decision to move a blob to a colder tier?
Lifecycle rules act on last-modified or last-accessed time. The first invents a size threshold. The third confuses redundancy with tiering, which are independent account and blob settings. The fourth is a plausible-sounding metric that lifecycle rules do not use.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A finance system writes statement documents that must be kept for seven years and must not be alterable. Auditors read a handful of them a few times a year, at short notice - a working day is acceptable. Separately, an operational copy of the last ninety days must be recoverable within an hour if the application corrupts it.

Design the storage: name the tier and the protections for each requirement, and say why the two requirements cannot be met by one copy of the data.

Hint

Write down the read-time requirement for each of the two, and check each against the rehydration figure before you choose a tier. Then ask which protection makes data unalterable rather than merely recoverable.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the seven-year requirement. Auditors accept a working day, and standard rehydration is up to fifteen hours, so the archive tier fits - just. Set the tier by a lifecycle rule that moves statements to archive once they are past the operational window.
  2. Make it unalterable. Recoverability is not the requirement here; immutability is. Apply a time-based retention policy so the blobs are write-once and read-many for the retention period, which prevents modification and deletion even by principals who would otherwise be permitted.
  3. Take the ninety-day requirement. One hour is far inside the rehydration window, so this copy cannot be in archive at all. Keep it in hot or cool, where it is immediately readable.
  4. Choose its protection. The named failure is the application corrupting data, so versioning covers an overwrite and point-in-time restore covers a broader corruption of a container. Soft delete covers the deletion case beside them.
  5. Say why one copy cannot do both. The tier is a property of the blob. A blob in archive cannot be read within an hour, and a blob in hot is not being stored at archive economics. The two requirements differ on the axis the tier sets, so they are two copies with two lifecycles - which is the design, not a compromise.

Sanity check. Your answer should contain two copies with different tiers and different protections, and the immutability policy should sit on the seven-year copy rather than on the operational one. If immutability landed on the ninety-day copy, the application would be unable to write its own corrections.
