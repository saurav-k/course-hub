Three services, similar names, opposite designs

working 12 min Capability area integration-messaging, rows message-queue, pub-sub and event-bus. · Azure Service Bus

The one-minute version

  • Service Bus is the broker: queues and topics with sessions for ordering, dead-lettering, transactions and duplicate detection.
  • Event Grid is a router: it pushes discrete events to handlers with filtering and retries, and dead-letters to storage on failure.
  • Event Hubs is the stream ingester from lesson 0603, where the message rate is the reason the service exists.
  • The discriminator to carry: who pulls, and whether volume is the point. Facts that are pushed, work that is pulled, telemetry that is streamed.
  • Storage queues remain as the small, cheap primitive with an older feature set. New designs pick the broker unless the case is genuinely trivial.

The discriminator, drawn once

What the broker gives you that nothing else does

The reframing Topics are the same broker with filtered subscriptions. A publisher writes once, and each subscription applies its own filter expressed in an SQL-like language to decide what it receives. That is publish-subscribe with the broker's guarantees intact - so fan-out does not have to mean giving up dead-lettering and ordering. Subscription counts and filter complexity are bounded per namespace, so a design fanning out very widely should check those limits before committing.

The router's own shape

Two smaller members complete the family. Storage queues are the older, minimal primitive - cheap and simple, with a feature set from an earlier era - and the corpus is direct that new designs choose the broker unless the case is genuinely trivial. And IoT Hub is the device-facing broker: per-device identity, device twins holding state, and two-way communication including cloud-to-device messages and direct methods, which is what separates it from a one-way telemetry pipe.

Tier choice on the broker is a networking decision as well as a capacity one. The premium namespace is what brings isolated capacity, virtual network integration and customer-managed keys - so a requirement from lesson 0703 or lesson 0804 lands on the tier rather than on a separate feature. A design that specifies "Service Bus, private" and picks the standard tier has specified two things that do not go together.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An order must be processed exactly once by one of several workers, and retried if a worker crashes. Which service?
Peek-lock is exactly this requirement: a crashed consumer has not completed, so the work returns. The first pushes a work item through a fact router. The second is for volume rather than work distribution. The fourth is the minimal primitive the corpus steers new designs away from.
What is the practical discriminator between the router and the broker?
Push against pull is the property that decides the design. The second invents a format constraint. The third invents a locality constraint. The fourth describes a request-response pattern that neither product is.
A design says "Service Bus, reachable only from our network". What does that require?
Network integration, isolated capacity and customer-managed keys are premium-tier properties. The first and second attach networking to messaging constructs that have nothing to do with it. The third swaps the product to solve a tier question.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A photo service has four flows. A user uploads an image and a thumbnail must be generated. Three internal teams each want to know when a new user registers, and each cares about a different subset. Device sensors in the company's warehouses report readings several thousand times a second. And a nightly billing run must process each account exactly once, in order per account, with failures kept for inspection rather than lost. A proposal uses one Service Bus queue for everything.

Route each flow: name the service and the feature that decides it, and say what the proposal loses on the flow it fits worst.

Hint

Ask of each flow whether it is a fact, a unit of work, or telemetry at a rate. Two of the four are facts and two are not - and one of the facts has a shortcut most designs miss.

Show the worked solution
  1. The thumbnail flow. A blob being written is a platform lifecycle event, and the router's system topics emit exactly that. So this is a subscription rather than an upload handler that also enqueues a message - which removes a component from the design entirely.
  2. The registration flow. Three interested parties, each wanting a different subset, is filtered publish-subscribe. The broker's topics give each subscription its own filter while keeping dead-lettering and ordering, so this is a topic with three subscriptions rather than three queues the producer has to know about.
  3. The sensor flow. Several thousand readings a second is a rate requirement, which is the stream ingester from lesson 0603. If the sensors are managed devices with identities and state, the device-facing broker is the front door and it routes onward.
  4. The billing flow. A unit of work, processed exactly once, in order per account, with failures retained. That is a queue with sessions keyed on the account, peek-lock so a crash returns the work, and dead-lettering so failures are inspectable.
  5. Say what the proposal loses. The sensor flow is where a single queue fails worst. A broker queue is not designed for that rate, and pushing telemetry through it costs the throughput model, the archival capture and the protocol endpoint that make the ingester the right answer.
  6. Note the second loss. The thumbnail flow through a queue means writing and operating a producer that the platform would have supplied for free through a system topic - work that exists only because the wrong product was chosen.
  7. State the final shape. A system topic subscription for thumbnails, a broker topic with three filtered subscriptions for registrations, the stream ingester for sensors, and a session-enabled broker queue for billing. Four flows, three products, and one of them used twice in two different shapes.

Sanity check. Your answer should use the broker twice in two different shapes and should remove at least one component the proposal implied. If all four flows landed on one product, the push-against-pull question has not been asked.
