When meaning became geometry

Neural networks turned words from symbols that are either identical or unrelated into positions in a space where closeness means similarity, and that single change is what made everything after it possible.

Where this lesson starts

Before the machine could read left the field counting word sequences and running out of room. This chapter explains what replaced counting. It assumes only that you know a language model tries to predict the next word.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Before the machine could read For fifty years the only two ways to make a machine handle language were to write the rules by hand or to count how often words follow each other, and counting won.

The one tight idea

Neural networks turned words from symbols that are either identical or unrelated into positions in a space where closeness means similarity, and that single change is what made everything after it possible.

2. What a neural network actually does

A neural network is a stack of two operations repeated, plus a way of correcting itself. That is the whole thing, and it is worth spending a paragraph on because every model this course describes sits on it.

The first operation is a weighted sum. Take a list of numbers coming in. Multiply each one by its own number, called a weight, and add the results together. Do that many times over with different sets of weights and you get a new list of numbers coming out. That is all a "layer" is: a table of weights that turns one list of numbers into another list of numbers.

The second operation is a bend. Pass each number in the new list through a simple rule such as "if it is negative, make it zero". Without that bend, stacking two layers would be pointless, because a weighted sum of a weighted sum is just another weighted sum, and the stack would collapse into a single layer no matter how tall you built it. The bend is what lets depth buy anything.

Multiply, bend, multiply, bend. Stack that a few dozen times and you have a network.

Training is the correction. Show the network an example, look at what it produced, and compare that to what you wanted. Reduce the disagreement to a single number, called the loss. Then work out, for every weight in every layer, whether nudging it up or down would make that number smaller, and nudge it a little in that direction. Repeat with the next example, and the next, billions of times. The procedure that works out which way to nudge each weight is called backpropagation, and the small step in that direction is called gradient descent. Nobody tells the network what any individual weight should be. The weights are the residue of a very long argument with the data.

3. The field stalled twice, for two different reasons

The idea is old. A neuron as a logic gate was described by Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts in 1943, and Frank Rosenblatt built a learning version, the perceptron, in 1958. The New York Times reported that year that the Navy expected it eventually to walk, talk, see, write and be conscious of its existence, which tells you the hype cycle is as old as the field.

The first stall was an argument. In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert showed in their book Perceptrons that a single layer of the kind Rosenblatt built cannot compute exclusive-or, which is the rule "one or the other, but not both". Draw the four cases as four dots on a page and you cannot separate the true ones from the false ones with a single straight line. That is a real limitation and it was correctly stated. Funding and interest collapsed for about fifteen years. The answer, once it arrived, was to stack layers, which needed a workable way to assign blame across a stack: backpropagation, made widely known by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams in Nature in 1986.

The second stall was not an argument but an obstacle in the arithmetic. When you push the correction signal backwards through a deep stack, each layer multiplies it by something. Multiply a number smaller than one by itself twenty times and it is effectively zero, so the early layers of a deep network receive no useful instruction at all. Yoshua Bengio, Patrice Simard and Paolo Frasconi proved this in 1994, and it is called the vanishing gradient problem. It hit hardest on networks that read sequences, because a sequence of thirty words is a stack thirty deep by construction. The workaround for sequences, published by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber in 1997, is the long short-term memory network, which gives the memory its own protected lane through the network so the signal has a path that does not get multiplied away.

4. In 2012 the hardware changed, not the idea

By 2011 the neural approach was known to be better in principle and worse in practice. Two things were missing, and neither was an idea.

The first was data at a useful scale, which arrived as ImageNet in 2009: millions of photographs, labelled through Amazon Mechanical Turk against a pre-existing hierarchy of concepts. The second was arithmetic at a useful speed, which arrived as the programmable graphics card. CUDA 1.0 shipped in June 2007 and made it possible to write ordinary code for a graphics processor.

It is worth being precise about what a graphics card actually gives you, because this gets hand-waved constantly. A graphics processor is not faster than a general-purpose processor. It is wider. Training a neural network is dominated by weighted sums, which are thousands of independent multiply-and-add operations with no dependencies between them. A processor of that era executed a handful of operations at a time, very cleverly, with branch prediction and large caches. A graphics card executed thousands at a time, stupidly. For a job with no dependencies in it, stupid and wide wins.

The comparison that makes this concrete is two papers a year apart. In December 2011 a Google team trained a network with a billion connections using 1,000 machines and 16,000 processor cores for three days. In 2013 a Stanford team trained networks at the same billion-parameter scale using three machines with graphics cards. Same scale, three orders of magnitude less hardware.

The proof landed in October 2012, when a network called AlexNet won the ImageNet classification challenge. Its top-five error rate is the argument:

Two years of careful hand-engineered computer vision moved that number by 2.4 points. One neural network moved it by roughly ten. The AlexNet paper also quotes a different figure, 15.3% against a second-best 26.2%, from the track that allowed extra training data, so always check which track a number comes from before comparing it with another one. The hardware line in the paper is the one worth remembering: the network took between five and six days to train on two consumer graphics cards. Not a supercomputer.

And 2012 was not one lucky result. In November of the same year, four separate laboratories jointly reported that deep networks had displaced the previous standard method in speech recognition. The same answer arrived in several places at once.

5. A word as a position rather than a symbol

Before the machine could read ended on a specific ceiling: a model that counts word sequences stores every word as a separate table entry, so in its arithmetic dog and cat are exactly as unrelated as dog and Tuesday, and a sensible sentence it has never seen gets a probability of zero.

The fix is to stop storing a word as a name and start storing it as a position.

Give every word a list of a few hundred numbers. Read that list as coordinates, the way two numbers give you a point on a page, except with three hundred numbers instead of two. Every word is now a point in a space, and two words can be near each other or far apart. Nobody chooses those coordinates by hand. They are weights like any other, learned by the loop in figure 1 from one very cheap signal: the company a word keeps. Words that turn up in the same kinds of sentence get pulled towards the same region.

Yoshua Bengio, Rejean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent and Christian Jauvin set this out in 2003, and their statement of why it works is the whole idea in one sentence: a sequence of words that has never been seen before gets a high probability if it is made of words that are similar to words forming an already seen sentence. Their method was expensive, so almost nobody had these word positions at scale. In January 2013 Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeff Dean published a much cheaper way to learn them, known as word2vec, and reported that it took less than a day to learn good word vectors from 1.6 billion words. Suddenly everyone had them.

The famous result attached to this is that the relationship between king and queen turns out to point in roughly the same direction as the relationship between man and woman, so that stepping from king by the amount that separates man from woman lands you near queen. Two details usually get lost, and both are worth keeping. That result is not in the word2vec paper: it was reported by Tomas Mikolov, Wen-tau Yih and Geoffrey Zweig at a conference in June 2013, out of Microsoft Research rather than Google. And the vectors they analysed came from a recurrent network language model, not from word2vec. The analogy is older than the tool it is usually credited to, which is a cheap and useful lesson in how folk history compresses.

Be careful about what to call this. The network does not understand the words. What it has is a space whose geometry puts words that behave alike near each other, and that turns out to be enormously useful. Anything more than that is a claim the evidence does not support.

6. What this bought, and what it did not

Representing a word as a position rather than a symbol dissolves the ceiling that stopped counting. Similar words are now literally close together, so a model that has seen "the cat sat on the mat" is already partway to handling "the dog sat on the rug", without ever having seen it. Generalisation stops being something you hope for and becomes a property of the representation.

What it does not solve is sentences. A word vector is one word. Real tasks are about whole sentences, sentences vary in length, and word order matters: "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" are made of the same words. Getting from a bag of word positions to a machine that reads one sentence and writes another is the next problem, and the fix that made it work also created the constraint that the rest of this course spends its time escaping. The bottleneck, and the fix called attention is that story.

7. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: What a neural network actually is A network is a stack of two operations, multiply by a table of numbers and then bend the result, and training is nudging those numbers until a measured error gets smaller.
  • Deep dive: Backpropagation, and the two winters The field stalled twice for two different reasons: the first was an argument about what a single layer could represent, and the second was an obstacle in the arithmetic of training deep stacks.
  • Deep dive: Sequences, memory, and the LSTM A recurrent network is one small network applied again and again with a carried-over memory, and the LSTM is that memory given a protected lane and gates that decide what enters it.
  • Deep dive: 2012: the year the hardware caught up The ideas that won in 2012 were roughly twenty-five years old, and what actually changed was a large labelled dataset and a graphics card that could do the arithmetic in parallel.
  • Deep dive: Meaning as a direction in space Give every word a position in a few hundred dimensions, learn those positions only from the company each word keeps, and similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space.

Retrieval practice

Why does a network need a bend between its layers?
A weighted sum of a weighted sum is another weighted sum, so depth buys nothing at all until something non-linear sits between the layers.
What actually changed in 2012?
The ideas behind AlexNet were roughly twenty-five years old; ImageNet and the programmable graphics card are what made them practical.
In what sense is a graphics card better for this work?
Weighted sums are thousands of independent multiply-and-adds, so width beats cleverness; a graphics card is wide rather than fast.
Why does a position generalise where a table entry cannot?
Similar words end up close together, so a sentence the model has never seen inherits probability from the sentences it has seen.
