Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint

The first scaling recipe held the wrong thing fixed, so the models built on it were far larger than they needed to be and far less trained than they should have been, and once that was corrected the scarce resource became text.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson is a correction of Predicting the future with a straight line, and the two are kept adjacent in every route so that the claim and its correction land together. It assumes you can read a log-log plot.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Predicting the future with a straight line Model loss falls as a power law in size, data and compute, which means a straight line on a log-log plot, which means you can forecast the result of a run you have not paid for.

The one tight idea

The first scaling recipe held the wrong thing fixed, so the models built on it were far larger than they needed to be and far less trained than they should have been, and once that was corrected the scarce resource became text.

2. The instruction the whole field followed

In January 2020, Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models gave the field a rule for turning a compute budget into a model. Predicting the future with a straight line covers it in full; the one sentence that matters here is its instruction about allocation: "As the computational budget C increases, it should be spent primarily on larger models, without dramatic increases in training time or dataset size."

The field did exactly that. Model sizes went from single-digit billions to hundreds of billions, and training sets stayed almost still, clustered around 300 billion tokens because that is roughly what the earlier work had used.

On 29 March 2022, twenty-two authors at DeepMind, led by Jordan Hoffmann, Sebastian Borgeaud and Arthur Mensch, published Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models and said the rule was wrong. Not the power laws, which held up. The split between size and data, which was badly off, and had been for two years, at a cost measured in very large numbers.

3. What the first study held fixed

The flaw is a piece of experimental design, and it is worth understanding precisely, because it is the single most useful cautionary tale in this part of the course.

Both studies do the same basic thing: train a lot of models of different sizes on different amounts of data, and see which combination reaches the lowest loss for a given amount of compute. The difference is in one setting that was left alone.

Training a neural network uses a learning-rate schedule: the size of the steps taken during training, planned in advance to start large and decay towards nothing as the run approaches its end. The decay is not optional decoration. A run that ends with its steps properly decayed lands at a meaningfully lower loss than the same run read at the same point with the steps still large.

The first study set one schedule, planned for a long run, and then read the loss at earlier points along that single curve to stand in for what a shorter run would have achieved. DeepMind names this directly: "the authors use a fixed number of training tokens and learning rate schedule for all models; this prevents them from modelling the impact of these hyperparameters on the loss".

And then they spell out the consequence: for a schedule that decays over 130 billion tokens, "the intermediate loss estimates" are "overestimates of the loss of a model trained with a schedule length matching" the shorter run. Reading loss halfway along a long run is not the same as finishing a short one. It is worse, systematically.

So every data point for "a model trained on not very much data" was pessimistic. Data looked less valuable than it is. And the fitted rule tilted the budget towards parameters, which is exactly what DeepMind concludes: it "eventually contributes to the conclusion that model size should increase faster than training data size as compute budget increases".

4. The corrected rule

The corrected finding is one sentence in the abstract: "for compute-optimal training, the model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled equally: for every doubling of model size the number of training tokens should also be doubled".

DeepMind reached that three separate ways, which is what makes it convincing rather than merely contrary. The three approaches agree with each other and disagree with the first study.

The paper also states the practical difference in plain terms. Under the old rule, ten times the compute means a model five and a half times bigger and less than twice the data. Under the corrected rule, ten times the compute means a bit over three times both.

The number practitioners actually remember from this paper is a rule of thumb that falls out of its projection table: about twenty training tokens per parameter. It is consistent across the range the table covers, from 8 billion tokens for a 400-million model up to 1.5 trillion for a 67-billion one. Quote it with the assumption attached, though, because it is the output of a fit under a stated setup and not a constant of nature.

5. The demonstration

An argument about experimental design is easy to wave away, so the paper settles it by building the model its own rule predicts and racing it.

DeepMind had already trained Gopher: 280 billion parameters on 300 billion tokens. They took the identical compute budget and spent it the corrected way instead, on Chinchilla: 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens. A quarter of the size, more than four times the data, the same bill.

Chinchilla "uniformly and significantly outperforms Gopher (280B), GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B)". On the MMLU benchmark it reached 67.5 per cent, "greater than a 7% improvement over Gopher".

Note what that last comparison means. A 70-billion model beat a 530-billion one. Nothing about the smaller model's architecture was better; it had simply been fed properly.

7. Why the constraint moved to text

Now do the arithmetic that the table forces on you.

Under the old rule, growing a model was a hardware problem: buy more machines, and the data requirement barely moved. Under the corrected rule, doubling the model doubles the text, and the projections run into the trillions almost immediately. A 520-billion model wants 11 trillion tokens.

Text at that scale is not something you can buy more of. The public web is a fixed quantity and it is already being scraped, so the binding constraint on the whole enterprise stopped being compute and became text. Where the data actually came from shows how carefully the earlier corpora had been curated, and that kind of care becomes very difficult when the requirement grows a hundredfold.

Three things follow, and between them they describe most of what the field did next.

Data became a research programme. Deduplication, quality filtering, mixing ratios and multiple passes over the same text all became things worth publishing about, because they were now the scarce resource rather than an unglamorous preprocessing step.

Small models trained for a very long time became attractive. If a 70-billion model beats a 530-billion one, and is also four times cheaper to serve every time somebody uses it, then the smaller model wins twice. That reasoning is why the notable open-weight families of 2023 are collections of models in the single-digit to tens of billions of parameters, trained on trillions of tokens, rather than one enormous model.

And getting more out of each parameter became urgent. If parameters are no longer the thing you can simply buy more of, then arranging them better matters again, which is where the architectural work of the following years goes. Sparsity moves to attention is one branch of that.

The corrected number is also a fit

Twenty tokens per parameter is not a constant. It comes out of one paper's projections, under its assumptions about how compute is counted and its choice of architecture and data, and its own three methods give exponents from 0.46 to 0.50 rather than a single value. Later work adjusts the picture further, particularly once the cost of serving a model, rather than training it, is counted too. Give the assumption whenever you give the number, and note that this whole analysis optimises for training cost alone.

Retrieval practice

What did the 2020 study hold fixed that it should have varied?
Reading loss partway along a long decay is systematically worse than finishing a short run properly, and the penalty falls only on the data side.
What is the corrected rule for spending a compute budget?
Three independent methods put the split near half and half, against the earlier study's split of roughly three quarters to one quarter.
What did the Chinchilla demonstration actually show?
Same compute, same lab, same family: only the split between parameters and tokens differed, which is what makes the comparison decisive.
Why did the binding constraint move from compute to text?
Under the old rule the data requirement barely moved with size; under the new one it grows just as fast, and text is not a thing you can order more of.
