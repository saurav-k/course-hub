Mixture of experts, part one: the router

Replace one large feed-forward network with many smaller ones and a small chooser that picks a couple per token, and what a model knows stops being the same number as what it costs to run.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson opens the central mechanism of the efficiency story. It assumes you know that a Transformer block contains a feed-forward network applied to each position, from The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked Delete the recurrence and keep only attention, and every position in a sentence can be computed at the same time with a constant number of operations between any two words.

The one tight idea

Replace one large feed-forward network with many smaller ones and a small chooser that picks a couple per token, and what a model knows stops being the same number as what it costs to run.

2. The weld this breaks

Through most of this course, one number has stood for two different things. Model size measures what a model knows, because knowledge lives in weights. Model size also measures what a model costs to run, because every weight is multiplied by something for every token that passes through. Those two meanings are welded together, and the whole scaling argument depends on the weld: a bigger model is better, and a bigger model is dearer, and there is no third option.

Mixture of experts is the third option. Its idea, in one sentence, is to make most of the model's weights available to a token without making them run for that token. If it works, the two meanings of "size" come apart, and from then on you have to quote two numbers instead of one.

Whether that is a good bargain depends on where a model's weights actually are, so start there.

3. Where the experts sit

A Transformer block does two things in turn, described in The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked. First, self-attention lets every position look at every other position and gather a blend of what it finds. Second, a small feed-forward network processes each position on its own, with the same weights used at every position. Attention is where positions meet. The feed-forward network is where each position thinks privately about what it just collected.

Those two halves are very different in weight count. In the original architecture the feed-forward network widens each position's vector by a factor of four and brings it back down, which is two large matrices per block, and that is where most of the parameters live. Attention's projections are comparatively small.

So the target is obvious once it is stated: leave attention alone and attack the feed-forward network, because that is where the weights are. Replace the one large feed-forward network in a block with many smaller ones, and route each token to only a few of them. Each of the small networks is called an expert, the choosing is called routing, and using only a few is called sparse activation.

The word "expert" is unfortunate and worth defusing straight away. Nobody assigns an expert a subject. There is no French expert and no chemistry expert. An expert is just a feed-forward network with its own weights, and whatever it ends up specialising in is a by-product of which tokens happened to be sent to it during training.

4. The router is a small matrix and a sort

The router is much less clever than the name suggests, and the fact that it is so plain is the interesting part.

For each token, at each block that has experts, the router takes the token's current vector and multiplies it by one learned matrix holding one row per expert. That produces one score per expert, usually called the token-to-expert affinity, which answers a single question: how well does this expert match this token. The router then takes the highest few scores, and only the experts behind those scores are run. Their outputs are added together, each weighted by its own score, and the sum is added back into the token's vector. That is the entire mechanism.

One detail is worth separating carefully, because beginners conflate it and everything in Mixture of experts, part two: why it is hard depends on the distinction. The affinity score does two jobs. It is a selector, deciding which experts are used at all, and it is a weight, deciding how much each chosen expert's answer counts in the sum. Those are separable, and prising them apart is exactly what the best-known fix for the balancing problem does.

It is worth knowing how little the router costs, because a reader who has just been told that a small learned component decides everything will reasonably suspect it of being expensive. It is one matrix with one row per expert, each row as wide as the token's vector. Take a real configuration: a model whose vectors are 7,168 numbers wide and which holds 256 routed experts per block needs about 1.8 million numbers for that block's router, against a model whose total is 671 billion. The router is a rounding error in both storage and arithmetic, and all of the saving comes from the experts it declines to run.

Two further points follow from the mechanism as stated. The choice is made per token, not per sentence or per request, so the word "the" in one position and the word "the" in another may be sent to different experts depending on what each has gathered from its neighbours. And the choice is made independently at every block, so a token that used experts 3 and 17 in one layer may use 88 and 204 in the next. A model does not pick a path through itself; it picks again at every step.

5. Two numbers that used to be one

Once a block only runs a few of its experts, the question "how big is this model" has two different answers, and they are worth defining exactly because the rest of this course keeps them apart.

Total parameters is every weight in the file. It is what you must have resident in memory before the model can answer anything at all, because the router might ask for any expert on any token.

Active parameters is the weights actually multiplied by something to produce one token. It is what sets the arithmetic per token, and therefore the latency and a large part of the running cost.

The anchor for this course is Mixtral 8x7B, released by Mistral in December 2023, because its own paper states both numbers plainly: each layer holds eight feed-forward blocks, a router selects two of them per token, and so "each token has access to 47B parameters, but only uses 13B active parameters during inference". Those two numbers are quoted, not derived, and that matters. You cannot get one from the other by arithmetic, because attention and the embedding tables are shared and are counted in both, so a reader who multiplies or divides will get a wrong answer. When this course gives a pair of these numbers, both halves come from the model's own paper or card.

The honest catch belongs here rather than in a footnote. Sparsity buys knowledge with memory rather than with compute. A model whose experts total many hundreds of billions of parameters still needs all of them resident somewhere, even though a given token touches a small fraction. That is why the largest open-weight models are simultaneously free to download and impossible for one person to serve, and it is a direct continuation of the memory story in Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs: sparsity moved the binding constraint further away from arithmetic and further towards memory.

6. From an LSTM experiment to every frontier open model

The idea is older than the Transformer. In January 2017, five months before the Transformer paper, Noam Shazeer and colleagues published Outrageously Large Neural Networks, which put a sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts layer with up to thousands of sub-networks between stacked LSTM layers and reported greater than a thousandfold increase in model capacity with only minor losses in computational efficiency. Everything in section 4 above is already in that paper, including the router, the top-k selection and the weighted sum.

It then sat mostly unused for four years, for reasons taken up in Mixture of experts, part two: why it is hard. Switch Transformer, from Google in January 2021, is the paper that made it practical at scale, and its central simplification was to route each token to exactly one expert rather than several. Its own abstract names the reasons adoption had been blocked: complexity, communication costs and training instability. Its largest model held 1,571 billion parameters across 2,048 experts.

The idea arrived in the open-weight world in December 2023 with Mixtral, and from 2024 onward the direction of travel is easy to read off the model cards.

Read the expert column downwards and it climbs from 8 to 896. Read the total column and it climbs from 47 billion to 2.8 trillion. Then read the active column on its own, because it is the one that does not behave: 13 billion in December 2023, 13 billion in April 2026, and roughly a hundred billion at the very top. The industry found it could keep growing what a model knows almost without limit while what it computes per token barely moved. That is the whole point of the idea, stated as a measurement rather than as a claim.

What this lesson has deliberately not done is explain why the router works at all rather than sending everything to its favourite expert. It does not, left to itself. That is the debt Mixture of experts, part two: why it is hard collects.

Retrieval practice

Which part of a Transformer block do the experts replace?
Attention is comparatively small in weight count, so replacing it would save little; the feed-forward half is the target precisely because that is where the parameters are.
What does the router actually produce for one token?
It is one small learned matrix producing an affinity score per expert, and the choice is remade for every token at every block rather than fixed for a request.
Why must total and active parameters both be quoted rather than one computed?
Only the expert weights are sparsely used, and everything shared sits in both figures, which is why this course quotes both halves from the model's own document.
Reading the table of real models, which column moved least between 2023 and 2026?
Totals climbed from 47 billion to 2.8 trillion while active counts stayed in the tens of billions, which is the measured version of the claim that sparsity separates knowing from computing.
