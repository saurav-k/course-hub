GPT-2 and the bet on scale

Make the decoder much bigger and train it on much more text, and the fine-tuning step starts to become unnecessary: you describe the task in the prompt instead.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

The fork: GPT-1 and BERT leaves two branches and a habit of fine-tuning a pretrained model for every task. This lesson is the first serious argument that the fine-tuning step could be dropped altogether.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The fork: GPT-1 and BERT Apply the same pretraining recipe to the two halves of the Transformer and you get two incompatible models, one that understands and one that writes, and for eighteen months the understanding one wins.
  • Tokens: how text becomes numbers A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

The one tight idea

Make the decoder much bigger and train it on much more text, and the fine-tuning step starts to become unnecessary: you describe the task in the prompt instead.

2. The habit GPT-2 was arguing with

By the end of 2018 the field had a recipe and everybody was following it. Train one model on a large pile of unlabelled text so that it learns the shape of the language, then take that model and train it further on a small labelled dataset for whichever task you actually want. ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later covers where the recipe came from, and The fork: GPT-1 and BERT covers the two rival ways of applying it.

The second step of that recipe is called fine-tuning, and it has a fixed cost every time. You need labelled examples for the task, which somebody has to write. You need to run gradient updates, which changes the weights. And at the end you have a model that does one thing, so the next task starts the whole process again. A hundred tasks means a hundred datasets and a hundred models.

On 14 February 2019 six authors at OpenAI - Alec Radford, Jeffrey Wu, Rewon Child, David Luan, Dario Amodei and Ilya Sutskever - published a paper arguing that the second step might be unnecessary. Its title says the whole thesis: Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners. The claim is that a model trained only to predict the next piece of text, if it is large enough and has read enough, will already do a task you describe to it, with no labelled examples and no gradient updates at all.

3. Four sizes, and the counts that are wrong in the paper

The paper trains four models rather than one, which is the part that makes it an argument about scale rather than a model release. Everything about them is identical except depth and width, so the comparison between them isolates size.

There is a trap here, and it is worth taking seriously because it is a lesson about sources as much as about models. The paper's Table 2 names its four sizes 117M, 345M, 762M and 1542M. Every checkpoint OpenAI actually released is named 124M, 355M, 774M and 1558M. Both sets are in wide circulation, including in this hub before they were corrected. OpenAI settled it in the repository README, in a footnote: "Note that our original parameter counts were wrong due to an error (in our previous blog posts and paper). Thus you may have seen small referred to as 117M and medium referred to as 345M."

The published architecture is enough to check this without trusting either party. Counting embeddings, attention projections, feed-forward layers and normalisations for the layer counts and widths the paper gives, with its stated vocabulary of 50,257 and context of 1,024, lands on the released names to the nearest million in all four cases. The released names are correct and the paper's are wrong.

The interesting thing that arithmetic reveals is not the correction. It is where the parameters go.

Two other changes from the previous model matter later. Normalisation moved to the input of each sub-block rather than sitting after it, and a further normalisation was added at the end of the stack. That is the switch from post-normalisation to pre-normalisation, and it is what made much deeper stacks trainable. Everything built since uses it. The context window also doubled, from 512 tokens to 1,024.

4. Describing a task instead of training for one

The mechanism of the claim is simpler than it sounds, and it depends entirely on the fact that a language model is a text continuer. If a model is good at guessing what comes next, then arranging the input so that the thing you want is what comes next turns the model into a tool for that task, without touching it.

Summarisation is the cleanest example, and the paper is candid about how crude the trick is. To make the model summarise an article, they append the characters TL;DR: to the article and let it continue. Nothing was trained. The string is doing all of the work, because on the open web that string is followed by a summary often enough for the pattern to have been learned.

Translation is done the same way, by writing a few lines of the form english sentence = french sentence and then a final english sentence =, and reading off the continuation. Question answering is done by seeding the input with a couple of question-and-answer pairs so the model infers the expected length and style of an answer.

This setting has a name that recurs through the rest of the course: zero-shot, meaning the model is given no worked examples of the task and no gradient update, only a description or a cue. It is worth being precise about that, because the same paper's translation and question-answering setups do put a few example pairs in the input, which is a different thing and gets its own name in GPT-3, and learning without learning.

5. The measured results, reported as what they were

It matters a great deal to this course that GPT-2 was not, in 2019, a good general-purpose assistant, and that its own paper says so. The abstract's headline is that the largest model "achieves state of the art results on 7 out of 8 tested language modeling datasets in a zero-shot setting", which is a claim about predicting text, not about performing tasks. On the tasks themselves the results split sharply.

Read down that column and the honest summary is: a real signal on every task, and a competitive result on almost none. The paper does not oversell it, and a footnote gives the comparison a human scale: Alec Radford records that he answered 17 of 100 of those trivia questions correctly himself, then adds "He actually only got 14 right but he should have gotten those other 3".

One number is worth pausing on because it is the argument in miniature. Removing the TL;DR: cue drops summarisation performance by 6.4 points on the aggregate metric. A few characters of English, and nothing else, are worth that much.

6. The bet, stated as a bet

So why does a paper of mostly mediocre task numbers matter? Because of one sentence about how those numbers move with size: "The capacity of the language model is essential to the success of zero-shot task transfer and increasing it improves performance in a log-linear fashion across tasks."

Every one of the four sizes was measured, and on task after task the bigger model was better in a smooth, regular way. Nothing flattened out. And in a second observation that gets dropped in retellings, the paper says in both its abstract and its results that the largest model was still improving when they stopped: "All models still underfit WebText and held-out perplexity has as of yet improved given more training time." GPT-2 was not finished. It was abandoned mid-climb.

Put those two together and you have a wager rather than a result. If the curve is smooth and the biggest model has not converged, then the mediocre translation score is not a ceiling, it is a point on a line that has not stopped rising. The bet is that the line keeps going, and that somewhere further along it the described task starts working properly.

That bet is what the next three years of the field consist of. It gets a measurement in Predicting the future with a straight line, which turns "the curve keeps going" into a fitted forecast, and it gets its payoff in GPT-3, and learning without learning, which spends a hundred times the compute to find out. It also leaves two debts that this lesson does not pay: where all that text came from, which Where the data actually came from collects, and what a lab should do with a model it thinks might be dangerous, which The model they would not release collects.

What this lesson does not claim

The four parameter counts used here are the corrected ones. The paper's own 117M, 345M, 762M and 1542M are still widely quoted, and were quoted in this hub until they were fixed, so expect to meet them. Note also what GPT-2 never disclosed: no training compute, no hardware, no wall-clock time, no token count and no cost appear anywhere in the paper. Any figure you see for those is somebody's estimate, not OpenAI's statement.

8. Sources to read first

  • Radford, Wu, Child, Luan, Amodei and Sutskever, Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners, OpenAI, 14 February 2019. The abstract for the seven-of-eight claim and the log-linear sentence; Table 2 and section 2.3 for the sizes and the normalisation change; sections 3.6 to 3.8 for summarisation, translation and question answering, including the 6.4-point drop and the 5, 11.5 and 4.1 per cent figures.
  • The openai/gpt-2 repository README. The footnote correcting the parameter counts, and the released checkpoint names.
  • The fork: GPT-1 and BERT, in this course, for the two branches of the pretraining recipe that GPT-2 is choosing between.

Both sources were opened and read before this lesson was written. The GPT-2 paper has no arXiv version, so it is read from the copy OpenAI hosts. The parameter totals in the table were re-derived from the paper's own architecture rather than quoted, which is what settles the disagreement between the paper and the checkpoint names.

Retrieval practice

What did GPT-2 argue could be dropped from the standard recipe?
Pretraining stayed and grew; it was the per-task training run, with its labels and its gradient updates, that the paper argued was avoidable.
How does appending TL;DR: to an article make a model summarise it?
The model only ever continues text, and on the open web that string is followed by a summary often enough to have been learned.
Why is a paper of mostly uncompetitive task scores important here?
A smooth rise across four sizes, on a model the authors say still underfits its data, turns a weak result into a wager that the line continues.
Which parameter counts should you use for GPT-2, and why?
OpenAI corrected itself in the repository, and the architecture the paper prints is enough for anyone to check which set is right.
