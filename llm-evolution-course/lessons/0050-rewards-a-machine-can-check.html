Deep dive · The reasoning era · Lesson 0050

Rewards a machine can check

Replace the human rater with a program that can check the answer, and the ceiling on training data moves from how fast people can label to how fast you can generate problems.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Teaching a model to answer describes a reward model that imitates human taste. This lesson is about what happens when the reward does not need a human at all. It assumes you know that three-stage recipe.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Teaching a model to answer A model trained to continue text is not trying to be helpful, so you add a stage whose only job is preference: show it demonstrations, learn a stand-in for human taste, and let it practise against that on a short leash.
  • Thinking is just more tokens A reasoning model does not think in any new sense: it writes a region of working tokens before it answers, which buys both more computation per question and an external place to keep intermediate results.

The one tight idea

Replace the human rater with a program that can check the answer, and the ceiling on training data moves from how fast people can label to how fast you can generate problems.

2. Where the human sat, and why that was a ceiling

The recipe that turned a text continuer into an assistant, described in Teaching a model to answer, has a person at its centre. Sample two answers to the same prompt, ask a human which is better, collect many such comparisons, and fit a model that predicts what the human would have said. That fitted model is the reward model, and the assistant is then trained to score well against it.

It works, and it has two problems that get worse the harder the question is.

The first is throughput. Every comparison costs a person's attention, so the amount of training signal available is bounded by how many people you can hire and how fast they can read. That is a hard ceiling and no amount of hardware moves it.

The second is competence, and it is the more serious one. A rater can reliably tell you which of two answers is politer, clearer or better organised. A rater cannot reliably tell you which of two long mathematical arguments contains a subtle error, and neither can a reward model fitted to that rater's judgements. On exactly the questions where you most want a reliable signal, the signal is a fitted approximation of a person's guess.

The move this lesson is about is to notice that for a large class of problems, the signal does not have to come from a person at all. If the question is "what is the value of x", a script can compare the answer with the known one. If the question is "write a function that does this", a compiler can build it and a test suite can run it. The grader is a program, it is right rather than approximately right, and it costs almost nothing per judgement.

The name for training this way was coined in Tulu 3, published by the Allen Institute for AI in November 2024: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.

3. Two rules, quoted in full

The clearest published statement of the recipe is the DeepSeek-R1 report of 22 January 2025, which trained a model this way starting from a base model with no supervised demonstration stage at all. Its entire reward function is two rules, and this course quotes them rather than paraphrasing because the paraphrases in circulation are all slightly grander than the original.

The first is the accuracy reward: the accuracy reward model evaluates whether the response is correct. For example, in the case of maths problems with deterministic results, the model is required to provide the final answer in a specified format, such as within a box, enabling reliable rule-based verification of correctness. Similarly, for programming problems, a compiler can be used to generate feedback based on predefined test cases.

The second is the format reward: in addition to the accuracy reward model, the authors employ a format reward model that enforces the model to put its thinking process between <think> and </think> tags. That region of working tokens is what Thinking is just more tokens describes.

That is all of it. Is the answer right, and is it laid out in the required shape. No human raters and no learned reward model, and the report says why not: they do not apply an outcome or process neural reward model, because such a model may suffer from reward hacking in large-scale reinforcement learning, retraining it needs additional resources, and it complicates the whole pipeline.

Read that as an engineering judgement rather than as a principle. A learned reward model is a thing that can be gamed and has to be maintained. A script that checks a boxed number is neither.

4. One more thing was deleted at the same time

A second simplification arrived alongside the first and is often confused with it, so it is worth one paragraph of separation.

The standard reinforcement learning algorithm used for these models needs a second network, called a critic, whose job is to estimate how good a typical answer to this question would be, so that an individual answer can be judged as better or worse than typical. That critic is usually about the same size as the model being trained, which doubles the memory and adds another thing that can go wrong.

Group relative policy optimization, introduced in DeepSeekMath in February 2024, removes it. For each question, sample a group of answers rather than one, grade them all, and use the group's own average as the standard to be judged against. Answers better than their group's average get pushed up and answers worse than it get pushed down. You do not need a learned estimate of how good a typical answer to this question is when you have just generated a batch of answers to it and can measure it. The DeepSeek-R1 report gives the motivation in one clause: in order to save the training costs of reinforcement learning.

The two ideas fit together neatly, which is why they arrived together. Sampling a whole group per question is only affordable if grading a sample is nearly free, and grading is nearly free exactly when the grader is a program. The papers course derives the objective. Post-training after PPO: three things deleted is where this course covers the wider run of deletions this belongs to.

5. What this lifts, and what it does not

The ceiling moves, and it is worth being precise about which ceiling.

Under human preference training, the limit is how much labelling you can buy. Under verifiable rewards, the limit is how many problems with checkable answers you can produce, and producing them is itself a machine task: generate a maths problem together with its solution, generate a programming task together with its tests. A ceiling set by human labour was replaced by a ceiling set by computation, and that substitution is the reason this became the dominant training method for reasoning within a year of being demonstrated.

What it does not lift is anything the grader cannot see. This is the structural consequence and it deserves to be stated as one rather than filed as a caveat. A program can check a numeric answer, a compiled function against its tests, a formal proof step, a parseable output format, a unit conversion. A program cannot check whether a memo is tactful, whether an explanation is pitched correctly for its reader, whether advice is wise, or whether an argument is honest. Training that improves only what a checker can grade will improve only what a checker can grade.

That is why the models of this era are extraordinary at competition mathematics and merely good at writing a difficult letter. The asymmetry is not an accident of what the labs cared about. It is the shape of the training method showing through, and a reader who understands this one point can predict the strengths and weaknesses of a reasoning model without reading its benchmark table.

6. What the authors reported seeing

The part of this story that circulated fastest is also the part that most needs careful attribution, so this section reports what the authors reported rather than asserting it.

The model was rewarded only for producing correct answers in the right format. It was never told to be long, never told to check its work, never given examples of backtracking. The report states that during training the average response length grew on its own, and describes the model learning to solve reasoning tasks with more thinking time without being instructed to. It also documents the appearance, over the course of training, of behaviours the authors did not train for: revisiting earlier steps, trying a second approach after abandoning a first, and pausing to re-examine a result.

The report includes a transcript of the model interrupting its own derivation with the words "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here", and the authors' framing of the whole result is worth quoting because it is the claim they actually make: rather than explicitly teaching the model how to solve a problem, they simply provide it with the right incentives, and it autonomously develops advanced problem-solving strategies.

The measured result alongside those observations is that the model's first-attempt score on the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination questions rose from 15.6 per cent to 71.0 per cent over training, reaching 86.7 per cent when sixty-four attempts were taken and the most common answer kept.

Two qualifications belong immediately next to that, both from the same report. The model trained this way, with no demonstration stage at all, had poor readability and mixed languages within a single answer, which is why the released model added a small amount of ordinary supervised training before the reinforcement stage. And the same report has a section on what did not work, in which two of the mechanisms the field most expected to be necessary, a model that scores individual reasoning steps and a tree search over possible continuations, are reported as attempts that failed. What did not work, and why that matters is where this course takes those up, because published negative results are rare enough to be worth a lesson of their own.

7. The shape this left behind

Three consequences follow, and together they describe most of what happened next.

Reasoning became reproducible. A recipe consisting of a base model, a pile of problems with known answers, a grader script and a group-sampling training loop is something a well-funded group can run, and it needs no proprietary dataset of human judgements. How reproducible became clear immediately: an independent report published on 22 January 2025, the same day, described reaching the same class of result without tree search, learned value functions or step-level scorers, and a paper published nine days after that got a comparable effect on competition mathematics by fine-tuning an existing model on one thousand curated examples.

The bottleneck moved to problem generation. If the limit is how many checkable problems you have, then generating them, and generating hard ones, becomes the valuable activity. That is where a good deal of the effort went afterwards, along with extending the idea from problems with numeric answers to whole environments where a program can check whether a task was completed, which is the bridge to Why agents are a systems problem.

The unverifiable half became the open question. Nothing here says how to improve judgement, taste or honesty, and the method's own logic says it will not. That is not a small remainder: most of what people actually ask these models to do falls on the right-hand side of the figure above. It is one of the questions this course leaves open rather than answers.

9. Sources to read first

  • DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning, submitted 22 January 2025, read as arXiv version 1. Section 2.2.2 carries both reward rules and the refusal to use a neural reward model, all quoted above; section 2.2.1 states the motivation for group relative policy optimization; section 2.2.4 reports the growth in response length, the self-evolved behaviours, the transcript quoted above and the rise from 15.6 to 71.0 per cent on AIME 2024; the abstract records the readability and language-mixing problems.
  • Shao et al., DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models, submitted 5 February 2024. The abstract introduces group relative policy optimization as a variant of PPO that improves mathematical reasoning while reducing its memory use.
  • Lambert et al., Tulu 3: Pushing Frontiers in Open Language Model Post-Training, submitted 22 November 2024. The abstract is where the name reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards is introduced.
  • Kimi Team, Kimi k1.5: Scaling Reinforcement Learning with LLMs, submitted 22 January 2025. The independent, same-day report whose abstract states it establishes a simple effective framework without relying on Monte Carlo tree search, value functions or process reward models.
  • Muennighoff et al., s1: Simple test-time scaling, submitted 31 January 2025. One thousand curated questions and a supervised fine-tune, nine days later.
  • Ouyang et al., Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback, submitted 4 March 2022. The human-preference recipe this lesson is contrasted against, in its own words.

Every one of these was opened and checked against what this lesson claims before it was written into the prose.

Retrieval practice

What exactly replaces the human rater in this training method?
The published reward function is two rules: is the final answer correct, and was the working placed inside the required tags.
Which ceiling does a checkable reward actually lift?
Producing a maths problem with its solution or a task with its tests is machine work, so the limit stops being how fast people can read.
Why are these models much stronger at competition maths than at difficult writing?
Training that improves what a checker can grade improves only what a checker can grade, so the asymmetry is the training method showing through.
How should the reported self-correcting behaviour be described?
Nothing in the two reward rules asks for length, checking or backtracking, and the authors report those behaviours emerging over training rather than being taught.
