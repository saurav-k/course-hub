Sequences, memory, and the LSTM

A recurrent network is one small network applied again and again with a carried-over memory, and the LSTM is that memory given a protected lane and gates that decide what enters it.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Text arrives one word at a time and the meaning of a word depends on what came before it. This lesson is about the first architecture that handled that, and about the specific way it failed on long inputs.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Backpropagation, and the two winters The field stalled twice for two different reasons: the first was an argument about what a single layer could represent, and the second was an obstacle in the arithmetic of training deep stacks.

The one tight idea

A recurrent network is one small network applied again and again with a carried-over memory, and the LSTM is that memory given a protected lane and gates that decide what enters it.

2. What this lesson must cover

To write. Full prose, complete sentences, 1,400 to 2,000 words. Every bullet below is a beat the finished lesson owes the reader, in this order.

  • Unrolling: the same weights applied at every step, with a hidden state passed along.
  • Why the vanishing-gradient problem is worse over time steps than over layers.
  • The LSTM's answer: a cell state that is added to rather than multiplied through, plus gates.
  • What a gate is, in plain terms: a learned number between zero and one that decides how much passes.
  • That the LSTM was published in 1997 and did not dominate until the 2010s, and why that gap matters to the story.
Do not overclaim

Do not say the LSTM solved long-range memory. It extended the reach considerably and the reach was still the binding constraint, which is why the next two lessons exist.
