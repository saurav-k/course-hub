Sequences, memory, and the LSTM

A recurrent network is one small network applied again and again with a carried-over memory, and the LSTM is that memory given a protected lane and gates that decide what enters it.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Text arrives one word at a time and the meaning of a word depends on what came before it. This lesson is about the first architecture that handled that, and about the specific way it failed on long inputs.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Backpropagation, and the two winters The field stalled twice for two different reasons: the first was an argument about what a single layer could represent, and the second was an obstacle in the arithmetic of training deep stacks.

The one tight idea

A recurrent network is one small network applied again and again with a carried-over memory, and the LSTM is that memory given a protected lane and gates that decide what enters it.

2. What changes when the input arrives one piece at a time

A network that takes a fixed list of numbers and produces another fixed list is fine for a photograph, where everything is present at once and there is a fixed number of pixels. Text is not like that. Sentences vary in length, they arrive in order, and the meaning of a word depends on what came before it. Bank means one thing after "the river" and another after "the money in the". Nothing about a fixed-size input handles that.

The first widely read answer is Jeffrey Elman's "Finding Structure in Time", published in Cognitive Science in March 1990. Elman added a second group of units to an ordinary network and called them context units. Their only job is to hold a copy of what the hidden layer computed on the previous step and feed it back in alongside the next input. The network therefore sees, at every step, the current thing and a summary of everything before it.

The experiment worth carrying away is the one Elman calls the letters-in-words simulation. He generated two hundred sentences from a small vocabulary, concatenated them into a stream of 1,270 words, broke those into their 4,963 individual letters, and fed the letters in one at a time. There were no spaces. There were no breaks between words and no breaks between sentences. The only task was to predict the next letter.

Plot the error against position and something appears that nobody put there. The error spikes at the first letter of each word and falls as the word continues, because once you have seen the first two letters of a word the rest is predictable and the letter after the word's end is not. Word boundaries are visible in the error curve, and the network was never told that words exist. Elman is careful about what this is: the information is not categorical but graded, so the boundaries are relative rather than crisp, and he points at the y in they as a case where the reading is genuinely ambiguous. It is still the earliest clean demonstration in this course of the pattern everything after it relies on. Structure in language falls out of the attempt to predict it.

3. Unrolling: one small network, applied again and again

Diagrams of recurrent networks are usually drawn with an arrow looping from a box back into itself, which is compact and teaches almost nothing. The honest picture is the unrolled one.

Draw the same box once per word, left to right, and straighten the loop into a horizontal arrow running between the copies. The word goes in at the bottom of each copy. The horizontal arrow carries the hidden state, which is a list of numbers summarising everything read so far. Each copy takes the previous summary and the current word and produces a new summary.

Two things about that picture are load-bearing. Every copy is the same box, with the same weights: there is one small network, applied repeatedly, not a chain of different ones. And the hidden state is the only route by which anything from step one can reach step thirty. It is a fixed-size list of numbers, so as the sentence gets longer it does not get bigger; it simply has to hold more.

4. Depth you did not choose

Training this thing means running the backward sweep along that horizontal chain, from the end of the sentence back to the beginning. Blame at step thirty has to travel back through twenty-nine hops to reach step one, and every hop multiplies it by something. Backpropagation, and the two winters sets out what that multiplication does: a factor a little below one, applied twenty times, leaves less than a millionth of the original signal, and a factor a little above one blows it up instead.

The reason this hurts a sequence model more than a deep stack is that the depth is not a design decision. If a network of twenty layers will not train, you can build one of six. If a thirty-word sentence will not train, you cannot build a shorter sentence. Sequence length sets the number of multiplications, the world sets the sequence length, and the dependencies that matter most are usually the long ones. "The keys to the cabinet are on the table" needs the plural at word two to reach the verb at word seven, across five words that say nothing about it.

Sepp Hochreiter and Jurgen Schmidhuber's 1997 paper adds a second problem alongside the arithmetic, and it is easy to miss. The same weights that decide what a step contributes also decide what it lets through from the past. So a unit that has stored something useful is constantly being pulled in two directions: keep the stored thing safe, and react to what is arriving now. The paper calls these input and output weight conflicts, and notes they exist for short lags too but become severe over long ones. Even if the arithmetic were kind, one set of weights doing two jobs would be a bad design.

5. The protected lane

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber published "Long Short-Term Memory" in Neural Computation in November 1997, and the central move is architectural rather than mathematical.

Give the memory its own lane.

Instead of one hidden state that is transformed at every step, an LSTM carries a second list of numbers called the cell state, and arranges for it to travel from step to step essentially untouched. Things are added to it and things are removed from it, but it is not passed through a weight matrix and a bend at every step the way the hidden state is. The paper's own name for the resulting property is the constant error carousel: a route along which the error signal flows backwards without being scaled at every hop, because the forward route did not scale it either.

That is the entire invention. The abstract states what it bought in one number: by keeping error flow constant along that route, an LSTM can bridge minimal time lags in excess of a thousand discrete time steps, against a handful for the networks that came before.

6. What a gate is

A gate sounds like a switch, and thinking of it as a switch is the main way people go wrong.

A gate is a number between zero and one, produced by the network itself from the current input and the current summary, and used by multiplying. A gate value of 1 lets a quantity through unchanged. A value of 0 blocks it entirely. A value of 0.3 lets thirty per cent of it through. There is one such number per position in the list, so a gate is really a list of taps rather than one, and each is set independently at every step.

Crucially, nobody writes the rule that sets them. The gate values are computed by a small weighted sum like everything else, and the weights that produce them are trained by the same loop as every other weight, from the same loss. When people say an LSTM "learns to remember", this is the concrete content of the claim: the model discovered weights that produce a gate near 1 for the things worth keeping and near 0 for the things worth dropping, because doing so predicted the next word better.

7. The forget gate arrived three years late

Almost every explanation of the LSTM, including the figures above, describes three gates: input, output and forget. The 1997 paper has two.

Search the original for the word forget and it does not occur. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber introduce a multiplicative input gate, to protect the stored memory from irrelevant inputs, and a multiplicative output gate, to protect the rest of the network from memory that is not currently relevant. The cell state itself just keeps accumulating. That is fine for a task that ends, and a problem for a stream that does not, because a memory that can only be added to eventually saturates. The forget gate was added by Felix Gers, Schmidhuber and Fred Cummins in "Learning to Forget: Continual Prediction with LSTM", in Neural Computation in October 2000.

It is a small correction and it is worth making for two reasons. The thing everyone calls the LSTM is a 1997 design plus a 2000 amendment, and the amendment is the gate that most explanations put first. And the reason for the amendment is a real lesson about the difference between a benchmark and a deployment: the original was evaluated on tasks that stop, and the gap only showed up when somebody ran it on a stream that does not.

8. Seventeen years between the paper and the moment

The LSTM was published in 1997 and did not become the default way to handle text until the middle of the 2010s. That gap is not a footnote, it is the point of this section.

The visible turn came in stages. In 2010 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues showed a recurrent network language model cutting perplexity by around half against a strong n-gram baseline, and word error rate by around eighteen per cent on a speech task. In August 2013 Alex Graves published "Generating Sequences With Recurrent Neural Networks", in which LSTMs generate text and handwriting one character at a time, and it is the paper that made a lot of people believe. By September 2014 an LSTM was translating between languages end to end.

Nothing about the architecture changed in between. What changed was that there was finally enough text to train on and hardware that could do the arithmetic fast enough for the training to finish, which is the subject of 2012: the year the hardware caught up. A course that tells this history as a sequence of ideas will make 1997 to 2014 look like a mystery. It is not a mystery. A correct idea can sit unused for seventeen years because the machines are not there, and that keeps happening in this story.

9. What it did not fix

Be careful about the claim here, because the popular version overstates it. The LSTM did not solve long-range memory. It extended the reach, considerably, from a handful of steps to a distance where useful work became possible.

The constraint that remained is the one figure 1 is drawn to make visible. The state is a fixed-size list of numbers. Whatever the model has understood about a sentence has to fit in it, and a longer sentence gets no more room than a short one. For predicting the next word that is tolerable, because most of what you need is recent. For reading an entire sentence in one language and then writing the whole of it in another, it is not, and that is precisely the task the field turned to next. Sequence to sequence, and the eight thousand numbers is where that fixed-size list becomes the binding constraint of the field, with a number attached to it.

Retrieval practice

In an unrolled recurrent network, what are the repeated boxes?
There is a single small network applied again and again, and the horizontal arrow between the drawings is the running summary it carries forward.
Why does the vanishing gradient bite harder over time steps than over layers?
You can build a shallower stack, but you cannot build a shorter sentence, so the number of multiplications is decided by the data.
What is the central idea of the LSTM?
Because nothing multiplies the cell state on the way forward, nothing multiplies the error signal on the way back, which is what lets the reach extend.
What is a gate, mechanically?
Gate values are computed by ordinary weighted sums whose weights are trained by the same loss, so "learning to remember" means those weights settled somewhere useful.
