What is still open, and what would change it

The one tight idea

The interesting questions in this field are not predictions but open problems, and each one can be stated with what would count as an answer, so that a future reader can tell whether it has been resolved.

2. This is a register, not a forecast

The last page of a history of a field that is still moving has two options. It can predict, which is entertaining and ages into embarrassment. Or it can state the questions that are actually open, in a form a future reader can test. This course takes the second option, and the form is fixed.

Every question below carries three things: the question, what would count as an answer, and the date it was opened. The middle one is the part that does the work. A question with no stated resolution condition can be argued about for ever, and a question with one can be checked by someone who was not here.

The rule that governs this page is the Open zone rule, and it has one clause worth knowing before you read further. A question is retired with its answer and the date it was answered, and is never silently deleted. A page that only shows the questions still open would tell a future reader nothing about how good this course's questions were. Keeping the resolved ones, with what turned out to be true, is the only way to find that out.

3. Evaluation

The question. What would a benchmark that does not saturate and cannot be contaminated actually look like?

Two failures have retired most of the suites this field grew up on. Saturation: frontier scores on the older suites cluster in the nineties, so they no longer rank anything. Contamination: no laboratory discloses the composition of its training data, so nobody can demonstrate that the test items were absent from it, and the absence of proof of contamination is not evidence of its absence.

Two structural responses exist, and they attack different halves. One raises the ceiling: an expert-written closed-ended examination across dozens of subjects, with 2,500 questions whose answers are unambiguous and easily checkable but cannot be quickly looked up. The other removes the possibility of contamination by dating: continuously collecting new problems from programming contests as they are published, so that problems appearing after any given model's cutoff can be isolated. The second idea is the more interesting one, because it converts trust into arithmetic on dates.

What would count as an answer. A benchmark that still separates frontier systems three years after its publication, whose contamination argument does not require trusting an undisclosed training set, and whose rankings are stable when the harness around the model changes. No suite in wide use in 2026 satisfies all three.

4. Interpretability

The question. What would count as knowing why a model produced a particular answer?

The obvious candidate does not qualify, and the evidence for that is older than the reasoning models. In May 2023, Turpin and colleagues showed that a model's stated step-by-step reasoning can systematically misrepresent the real reason for its answer: reorder the options in a prompt so that the answer is always in the same position, and models follow the bias while producing explanations that never mention it, rationalising incorrect answers at length. Two months later, Lanham and colleagues intervened directly on the reasoning, inserting mistakes and paraphrasing, and found models varied enormously in how much they actually conditioned on it, with the uncomfortable finding that larger and more capable models produced less faithful reasoning on most tasks they studied.

So the main visible artefact of a reasoning model is a scratchpad that demonstrably helps it and is not an account you can audit. The situation got harder rather than easier in 2026, because at the top of the range the raw working is no longer returned to the caller at all, only a summary, which Where we are now records with the vendor documentation.

What would count as an answer. An explanation that predicts behaviour under intervention: change the stated reason, and the answer changes in the way the explanation says it should; suppress the stated reason, and the answer changes accordingly. Anything weaker is a plausible narrative, and this field has ample evidence that models produce those readily.

KNOWN as a problem, unanswered as a question, opened 21 August 2026.

5. Cost

The question. Does the price of a unit of capability keep falling, and what would stop it?

It fell steeply for four years, and the reasons are mechanical rather than mysterious: computing only a fraction of a large model per token, which Mixture of experts: the router covers; storing weights in fewer bits; training small models on the outputs of large ones; and the serving improvements in Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs.

There is a measurement problem sitting underneath the question, and it is new. Once deliberation is a dial, the price of a token stops being the price of an answer. A model at half the price per token that thinks for four times as long is more expensive per question, and the vendors' own launch material now says as much. Any claim that inference got cheaper needs to say cheaper per what.

What would count as an answer. A published series measuring the cost of reaching a fixed level of capability on an unsaturated benchmark, taken at intervals over several years, with the effort setting held constant or reported. Nothing of that shape currently exists in public. Candidate causes of a stop, each independently plausible: hardware supply, energy, and the returns on the capital commitments that Where we are now lists as contested.

6. Reliability

The question. Do the capability curve and the reliability curve converge, and what would make them?

What a system can do on one attempt keeps improving. How often it does the same thing on every attempt improves much more slowly, and 2026 measurements report that the strongest models carry the highest catastrophic failure rates, because attempting an ambitious multi-step strategy is exactly what produces a long chain with no way back. Why agents are a systems problem is that evidence in full, with the measurements dated.

What would count as an answer. Either direction resolves it. Convergence: a frontier release where success on four attempts out of four rises as fast as success on one, on the same long-horizon suite, sustained across two model generations rather than one. Or the opposite: a further two generations in which catastrophic failure rates continue to rise with capability, which would make the finding structural rather than a stage.

INFERRED, opened 21 August 2026.

7. Data

The question. What happens when the readily available human-written text has been used?

This one has an actual forecast attached, which is unusual. Villalobos and colleagues estimated the total stock of public human-generated text and the growth in demand for it, and projected that models would be trained on datasets roughly the size of that stock somewhere between 2026 and 2032, or slightly earlier if they are overtrained. Their own suggested continuations are the three things the field is in fact now doing: generating data, transferring from data-rich domains, and improving how much a model learns per token.

What can be checked from outside is thin. Token counts are published, including by open-weight releases that state training sets in the tens of trillions of tokens. Composition never is, so the honest statement is that nobody outside a frontier laboratory knows how much of any current training set is human-written. Where the data actually came from tells the earlier part of that story, when it was still checkable, and Post-training after PPO: three things deleted covers what is known about training on generated text.

What would count as an answer. A published training-set composition from any frontier laboratory, which would settle it directly. Failing that, a measured plateau in capability gained per token of training data that shows up across several laboratories at once, which is the shape the constraint would take if it were biting.

INFERRED, opened 21 August 2026.

8. The next binding constraint

This course can be read as a sequence of eras, each defined by the one thing that was holding everything else back. Hand-written rules, until statistics replaced them. Meaning, until words became directions in space. Reach, until attention removed the fixed-size bottleneck between two sequences. Scale, until the recipe and the hardware allowed it. Usefulness, until a model was trained to answer rather than to continue. Cost, until only a fraction of a model had to run per token. And thinking, until computation could be spent at the moment the question was asked.

Each of those was visible only in retrospect. Nobody in 2014 said that the fixed-size vector between an encoder and a decoder was the binding constraint on the field; they said translation quality dropped off on long sentences.

The question. What is the eighth?

The honest answer is that a reader inside an era cannot see its constraint, which is why this page does not choose. What it can do is name the candidates with the argument for each, so that a future reader can see which one it turned out to be and whether it was on the list. Reliability, because capability is no longer what stops a system being deployed. Verification, because training against checkable rewards only improves what a checker can grade, which Rewards a machine can check sets out. Energy and capital, because the build-out is now large enough to have its own economics. Trust and governance, because a model can now be switched off by an authority outside the company that made it. And data, for the reasons in section 7.

9. Where this leaves the reader

You have reached the end of a story that runs from a program in the 1960s with no model of meaning at all to systems that spend minutes of computation on a question and then act on the answer. The useful thing to take from it is not a list of models. It is the habit this last page is made of: when you meet a claim about this field, ask what would count as evidence for it, ask whether that evidence exists, and label the claim honestly when it does not.

Every deep dive in this course names the papers underneath it, and the papers course next door carries the mathematics and the runnable code that this one deliberately left out. The chronicle is where anything that has happened since this page was written will be, with a date and a label attached.
