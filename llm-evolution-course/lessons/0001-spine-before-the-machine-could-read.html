What you need first

The one tight idea

For fifty years the only two ways to make a machine handle language were to write the rules by hand or to count how often words follow each other, and counting won.

2. What this lesson must cover

To write. Full prose, complete sentences, 1,400 to 2,000 words. Every bullet below is a beat the finished lesson owes the reader, in this order.

  • ELIZA in 1966 and why a program with no model of meaning still convinced people.
  • The rule-writing era and the ceiling it hit: every sentence needs another rule.
  • Shannon's insight that language is predictable, and that predictability can be measured.
  • The n-gram language model as the first thing that scaled, and the curse of dimensionality that stopped it.
  • Statistical machine translation as the proof that counting beat rule-writing on a real task.
  • The one sentence to leave the reader with: the field stopped trying to write down meaning and started measuring it.
Do not overclaim

Do not say ELIZA was an early chatbot in the modern sense. It matched patterns and reflected them back. The interesting fact is what people did with it, not what it did.

4. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: Machines that talk without understanding You can produce convincing language with pattern matching and no model of meaning at all, and people will supply the understanding themselves.
  • Deep dive: The statistical turn: guessing the next word Language is predictable, predictability can be measured, and a machine that counts what usually follows what beats a machine that reasons about grammar.
  • Deep dive: Translation without a linguist Model the source language and the translation separately, learn both by counting a parallel corpus, and search for the target sentence that scores best, with no grammar written by anyone.
