Before the machine could read

For fifty years the only two ways to make a machine handle language were to write the rules by hand or to count how often words follow each other, and counting won.

Where this lesson starts

This is the first chapter of the spine. It assumes nothing at all: no maths, no programming, no prior chapter. It asks one question - before anyone trained a neural network on text, what did a language machine actually do?

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

This lesson has no prerequisites in this course. It assumes only curiosity, and it states its own starting point above.

The one tight idea

For fifty years the only two ways to make a machine handle language were to write the rules by hand or to count how often words follow each other, and counting won.

2. A program with no meaning in it

In January 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum described a program called ELIZA in Communications of the ACM. It ran at MIT, it held conversations in English, and it contained no model of the world. There was no dictionary of concepts inside it and no representation of what any word meant.

ELIZA worked in three mechanical steps. First it scanned your sentence for a keyword. Then it matched the sentence against a decomposition rule, which is a pattern that cuts a sentence into numbered pieces. The rule written (0 YOU 0 ME) means "anything, then the word you, then anything, then the word me", and it splits the sentence into four chunks. Finally it poured those chunks into a reassembly rule, which is a fixed frame with numbered slots. The frame (WHAT MAKES YOU THINK I 3 YOU) takes the third chunk and drops it in.

Type "It seems that you hate me" and ELIZA answers "What makes you think I hate you". Nothing in that exchange is a meaning. The word hate was copied from one position in your sentence to another position in the program's sentence, and the program could not have told you what it was copying.

It would be wrong to call ELIZA an early chatbot in the modern sense. It matched patterns and reflected them back, and it did so with a few hundred lines of rules written by one person. The interesting fact about ELIZA is not what it did. It is what people did with it. Weizenbaum's own observation, in the paper, is that his users sustained the illusion themselves: they attributed to the machine background knowledge, insight and reasoning that were entirely their own contribution. Half a century before anyone trained a language model, the first thing the field learned about talking machines was that a listener will supply the understanding if you give them somewhere to put it.

3. The rules never finished

ELIZA was a demonstration, but the approach behind it was the serious plan for machine language for about thirty years. You write the rules down. A person who knows the language and knows the task sits and enumerates what the program should do, case by case.

Machine translation was where that plan was tried at industrial scale, and where it failed publicly. A demonstration at Georgetown University in 1954, run with IBM, translated a set of Russian sentences into English and produced enormous press excitement. Twelve years later the ALPAC report concluded that machine translation was not delivering, and United States funding was cut sharply. The professional body of the field, then called the Association for Machine Translation and Computational Linguistics, dropped the words "Machine Translation" from its name.

The reason hand-written rules lose is not that the people writing them were careless. It is that language has a very long tail. A rule covers the sentences its author thought of. Real text keeps producing sentences nobody thought of, and each one needs another rule, and the rules interact, so the thousandth rule is far more expensive to add than the tenth. There is no point at which the list is finished.

There was also an argument in the way of the alternative. In 1956 and 1957 Noam Chomsky argued that a finite-state process that only knows which words tend to follow which other words, while it might be a useful engineering heuristic, could not be a complete model of human grammar. He was making a claim about linguistics rather than about engineering, and he was largely right about linguistics. The effect on engineering was that counting words looked disreputable for roughly thirty years.

4. Shannon had already measured the alternative

The case for counting was made before the argument against it, in two papers by Claude Shannon at Bell Labs.

The first, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" in 1948, is the founding paper of information theory, and buried in it is a demonstration that matters here. Shannon built approximations of English by sampling: pick letters at random and you get gibberish; pick each letter according to how often it follows the previous letter and you get pronounceable nonsense; do the same with whole words and you get text that reads like a sentence you have half heard. Nothing in that procedure knows any grammar. It only knows what usually follows what.

The second, "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" in 1951, turned that into a measurement. Shannon had people guess the next letter of a text they could not see, letter by letter, and counted how often they were right. That gave a number for how predictable English is, and therefore for how much of any English sentence is genuinely new information and how much the reader could have supplied. It is the direct ancestor of perplexity, which is still the number people quote when they say a language model got better.

The lesson those two papers carry into this course is a single sentence. Language is predictable, and predictability is something you can measure rather than argue about.

5. Counting, and the ceiling it hit

Making that operational took another twenty-five years and a different community. From about 1975, Fred Jelinek's group at IBM and James Baker at Carnegie Mellon independently made counting work for speech recognition, where a machine has to decide which words a noisy signal probably contains. The IBM group is where the terms language model and perplexity come from.

The thing they built is called an n-gram model, and it is exactly as simple as it sounds. Take a large pile of text. Count how often each run of two words occurs, then each run of three, and so on. To guess what follows "the cat sat on the", look up what usually followed those words in the pile and pick accordingly. No theory of grammar is involved. The only ingredient is text, which is why this was the first approach to language that got better when you fed it more of something you could simply go and collect.

The most famous line of the era belongs to Jelinek, and the wording is genuinely disputed. It circulates as "every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recognizer goes up". Jelinek himself recalled saying something closer to "anytime a linguist leaves the group the recognition rate goes up", and in 2004 he gave a talk titled "Some of My Best Friends Are Linguists" specifically to push back on the reputation the line had given him. The quip is worth knowing, and so is the fact that nobody can quite agree on it.

Counting also has a ceiling, and the ceiling is sharp. An n-gram model treats every word as an atom with no relationship to any other word. In the model's arithmetic, dog and cat are exactly as unrelated as dog and Tuesday: they are three different table entries and nothing more. So a perfectly sensible five-word run that happens never to have occurred in your pile of text gets a probability of zero, and there is no principled way for the model to notice that it has seen an almost identical run with one word swapped for a near-synonym. You can add more text, but the number of possible word runs grows far faster than any pile of text you can collect, so the gaps never close.

6. Translation with no grammar in it

The proof that counting beat rule-writing on a hard, real task came from the same IBM group, in 1990. Peter Brown, John Cocke, Stephen and Vincent Della Pietra, Fred Jelinek, John Lafferty, Robert Mercer and Paul Roossin published "A Statistical Approach to Machine Translation", and its structure is worth carrying with you because the shape recurs.

They split translation into two separate questions, each answerable by counting. Given a French sentence, and looking for the English sentence it came from, ask: does this candidate sound like English, and does this candidate look like something that would produce that French? The first question is answered by a language model built from English text alone. The second is answered by a model of how words in one language line up against words in the other, learned from a parallel corpus, which is simply the same documents in both languages. Their source was the Canadian parliamentary record, which is published in English and French because Canada requires it. Then you search for the English sentence that scores best on both questions at once.

The 1993 follow-up worked out how to estimate all those numbers from the parallel text without anyone labelling anything. Between them, the two papers translate between two languages, and no linguist wrote a single rule in either of them. The rules were not written down. They were estimated.

7. What the field learned, and what it still owed

By the early 2000s the argument was settled in practice. The way to make a machine handle language was not to write down what language means. It was to measure how language behaves, on as much text as you could find.

That leaves this chapter with one unpaid debt, and it is the problem When meaning became geometry opens with. Counting works, and counting cannot generalise, because a model that stores words as separate table entries has no way to know that two words are similar. Somebody had to find a representation of a word that carries its resemblance to other words inside it. When they did, in a line of work running from a 2003 paper by Yoshua Bengio and colleagues to the word-vector tools of 2013, the whole shape of the field changed, and When meaning became geometry is that story.

8. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: Machines that talk without understanding You can produce convincing language with pattern matching and no model of meaning at all, and people will supply the understanding themselves.
  • Deep dive: The statistical turn: guessing the next word Language is predictable, predictability can be measured, and a machine that counts what usually follows what beats a machine that reasons about grammar.
  • Deep dive: Translation without a linguist Model the source language and the translation separately, learn both by counting a parallel corpus, and search for the target sentence that scores best, with no grammar written by anyone.

Retrieval practice

What actually produced ELIZA's convincing replies?
A keyword, a decomposition rule and a reassembly rule sent your own words back inside a fixed frame, and nothing on that path represented a meaning.
Why did hand-written language rules stop scaling?
Language has a very long tail, so the list of rules never reaches an end and each new rule is harder to add than the last.
What did Shannon's guessing experiments establish?
Putting a number on how often a reader can guess the next symbol is what turned "language is predictable" from an opinion into a measurement.
What is the ceiling an n-gram language model runs into?
Words are separate table entries, so a sensible run that never occurred gets probability zero even when a near-identical run was seen.
