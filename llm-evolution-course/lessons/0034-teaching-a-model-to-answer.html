Teaching a model to answer

A model trained to continue text is not trying to be helpful, so you add a stage whose only job is preference: show it demonstrations, learn a stand-in for human taste, and let it practise against that on a short leash.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Pretraining produces a model that predicts text extremely well. This lesson is about the gap between predicting text and answering a question, and it picks up the preference thread named in Two papers, one day.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • GPT-3, and learning without learning A model whose weights never change gets better at a task when you put a few worked examples in the prompt, which is a kind of learning that leaves no trace in the model.
  • Two papers, one day On 12 June 2017 two unrelated papers were posted, one that made models fast and one that made them listen to people, and the rest of this course is those two threads converging.

The one tight idea

A model trained to continue text is not trying to be helpful, so you add a stage whose only job is preference: show it demonstrations, learn a stand-in for human taste, and let it practise against that on a short leash.

2. A very good model, giving a useless answer

Pretraining produces a model that predicts text extremely well. It does not produce a model that is trying to help you, and those are different things in a way that is easy to miss.

Type a question into a purely pretrained model and a plausible continuation is more questions, because on the open web a question is very often followed by another question in a list. That is not a malfunction. The model was asked what text usually comes next, it answered correctly, and what usually comes next is not an answer.

The paper that eventually fixed this opens with the diagnosis in one line: "Making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent. For example, large language models can generate outputs that are untruthful, toxic, or simply not helpful to the user. In other words, these models are not aligned with their users."

Notice that scale is explicitly ruled out as the cure. GPT-3, and learning without learning covers a model a hundred times larger than its predecessor, and it has exactly this problem.

3. Why you cannot simply train it properly

The obvious response is to train it on helpfulness the way it was trained on text. That runs straight into a wall.

Ordinary training needs a loss: a number saying how wrong each output was. For next-token prediction that number is free, because the real next token is right there in the data. For "be helpful" there is no right answer anywhere to compare against, because helpfulness is not a string.

This is the same problem that Two papers, one day names in its second thread. On 12 June 2017 Paul Christiano, Jan Leike, Tom Brown, Miljan Martic, Shane Legg and Dario Amodei published Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences, which states it about robots rather than text: "suppose that we wanted to use reinforcement learning to train a robot to clean a table or scramble an egg. It's not clear how to construct a suitable reward function".

Their answer is the move that everything below is built on. Do not write the reward function. Learn one. Show a person two short clips of the agent, ask which is better, and fit a model to those choices.

One design decision from that paper survives into every later system, and the reason it was made is worth carrying. The authors state it twice. In the introduction: "We ask the human to compare short video clips of the agent's behavior, rather than to supply an absolute numerical score. We found comparisons to be easier for humans to provide in some domains, while being equally useful for learning human preferences." And later, more directly: "we found it much easier for humans to provide consistent comparisons than consistent absolute scores". People are unreliable at scoring and reliable at comparing. That is why every system in this lesson asks for rankings rather than ratings.

4. Three stages, with their data

The recipe that assembled all of this for a general instruction-following model is Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback, published by twenty authors at OpenAI on 4 March 2022, and known by the name of the models it produced, InstructGPT. It has three stages, and each one exists because the stage before it does not scale.

Stage one: demonstrations. Hire people, give them real prompts, and have them write the answer they wish the model had given. Now you have pairs of prompt and good answer, and you can train in the ordinary way. This is called supervised fine-tuning, and it works. It also does not scale: writing a good answer is slow, and each prompt teaches you about exactly one answer. The dataset was about 13,000 prompts.

Stage two: comparisons, and a machine that has taste. Instead of asking people to write answers, show them several answers the model has already produced and ask which is best. Ranking is far faster than writing and far more reliable, for the reason established in 2017. Then train a second network, the reward model, whose only job is to look at a prompt and an answer and output one number saying how much a person would like it. That is the conversion that makes everything else possible: helpfulness was not a number, and now it is one. The dataset was about 33,000 prompts, with labellers ranking between four and nine responses each.

Stage three: practice against the judge. Now that a score exists it can be optimised. The model writes an answer, the reward model scores it, and the model is adjusted to score higher next time. That is reinforcement learning, and the specific algorithm used here is PPO. This stage used about 31,000 prompts and no human labels at all, because the judging is now done by the reward model.

The total human effort behind this is worth stating plainly, because it is much smaller than people assume. About forty contractors, hired through Upwork and ScaleAI and selected on a screening test, produced the data that shaped how this family of models answers. The paper says so directly.

5. The leash

Stage three has an obvious failure mode, and it was identified in the first paper that ever tried this on a language model.

The reward model is a model of human taste, not human taste, and it was trained on samples from the original model, so it is only reliable about text that looks like what that model produced. Let the policy chase the score without restraint and it will find the blind spots, because finding the maximum of a function is exactly what optimisation does.

Daniel Ziegler and colleagues at OpenAI ran that experiment in September 2019 - on GPT-2 at 774 million parameters, one month after that checkpoint was publicly released - and published what came out when the restraint was removed. The samples, which reach a mean score corresponding to 99.97 per cent positive sentiment according to the scoring model, read like this: "These These These sound flowed instantly easily easily easily easily!"

A perfect score, and gibberish. The paper's own caption says that "without regularization towards natural language, the results are gibberish even if we include an entropy bonus".

The fix is a penalty for drifting away from where the model started. In prose, and this is the one formula this lesson allows itself: the score the model actually chases is the reward model's number minus a term that grows the further the model's behaviour has moved from the stage-one model's behaviour. Moving is still allowed. It just has to be worth paying for.

Ziegler's paper explains why this is not merely a tidiness measure: the penalty "prevents the policy from moving too far from the range where r is valid". It is not restraining the model for its own sake. It is keeping the model inside the region where its own scoring function still means something.

6. Two asymmetries worth remembering

The judge is smaller than the thing it judges. The reward model in InstructGPT has 6 billion parameters and scores a 175-billion model. The paper explains that this "saves a lot of compute", and adds something more interesting: "we found that 175B RM training could be unstable and thus was less suitable to be used as the value function during RL". The big judge did not work.

The alignment stage is nearly free next to pretraining. The paper puts three numbers side by side: the supervised stage cost 4.9 petaflop-days, the reinforcement stage cost 60, and pretraining the underlying model had cost 3,640. The whole alignment procedure is under two per cent of the pretraining bill.

Now the result those two facts frame. In human evaluations, "outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters". Read that beside the cost figures and the conclusion is the one OpenAI drew itself: at that moment, "increasing investments in alignment of existing language models is more cost-effective than training larger models". After two years in which the answer to every question was a bigger model, here is a measurement saying a hundredfold size advantage loses to a few tens of thousands of human judgements.

One genuine complication, which should be reported rather than smoothed over. The InstructGPT paper coins the term alignment tax for the performance drops it observed on standard benchmarks after this training, and describes an extra ingredient - mixing ordinary pretraining gradients back in during stage three - that mostly removes them. Three weeks later Anthropic published a paper whose abstract says the opposite: "We find this alignment training improves performance on almost all NLP evaluations". Read further, though, and the two accounts partly reconcile. Anthropic reports that smaller models "experience severe 'alignment taxes'", while their 13-billion and 52-billion models did better on zero-shot evaluations and the same on few-shot ones after the same training. So the tax is real and appears to depend on size, and where exactly it turns into a benefit is not settled by either paper.

7. What the word means here, and what came next

Be precise about the vocabulary, because this word travels badly. In this lesson, alignment means one specific thing: following instructions, and matching the preferences of the people hired to rank the answers. The reward model learned the taste of about forty contractors working to a written brief. That is a real and useful achievement, it is not a solution to any broader question about machine values, and the paper does not claim it is.

The technique now has a name, reinforcement learning from human feedback, usually shortened to RLHF, and it arrived through four papers over five years.

Notice the last row's date. The recipe that made these models answerable was finished nine months before the product that made them famous, and it was published. A text box changes everything is what happened when the same technique was applied to conversations instead of single instructions and put behind a free web page.

This is also not the end of the method. Every stage described here has since been replaced or removed by somebody: the reward model, the reinforcement stage, and the human writing the demonstrations have each been done away with in some later system. Post-training after PPO: three things deleted is that story. For the equations behind stages two and three, the papers course lesson on InstructGPT writes them out.

One word, one meaning, in this lesson

Alignment here is instruction-following plus agreement with a specific set of labellers, hired by one company and working to one brief. It is not a claim about values, safety in any broad sense, or truthfulness beyond what those labellers rewarded. Keeping the word this narrow is what makes it possible to say anything measurable with it.

Retrieval practice

Why does a well-trained language model give an unhelpful answer?
On the open web a question is often followed by more questions, so the model is behaving exactly as trained and is useless anyway.
Why do these systems ask people to rank answers rather than score them?
That was an experimental finding in the 2017 preference-learning paper, and every system since has been built on it.
What goes wrong without a penalty for drifting from the start point?
The judge was only ever trained on text from the original model, so outside that neighbourhood its score stops measuring anything real.
What does the 1.3-billion against 175-billion result establish?
Labellers preferred the small aligned model's outputs, and the whole alignment stage cost under two per cent of the pretraining bill.
