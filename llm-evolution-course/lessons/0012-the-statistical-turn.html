The statistical turn: guessing the next word

Language is predictable, predictability can be measured, and a machine that counts what usually follows what beats a machine that reasons about grammar.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Hand-written rules, the subject of Machines that talk without understanding, ran out of authors. This lesson introduces the idea that replaced them: do not describe language, measure it. It assumes only that you can read a table of counts.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Machines that talk without understanding You can produce convincing language with pattern matching and no model of meaning at all, and people will supply the understanding themselves.

The one tight idea

Language is predictable, predictability can be measured, and a machine that counts what usually follows what beats a machine that reasons about grammar.

2. Shannon built English out of nothing but counts

The case for counting was made in 1948, in the paper that founded information theory. Claude Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" is about telephone lines and how much can be sent down them, and buried in its third section is a demonstration that matters more to this course than anything else in it.

Shannon builds a ladder of approximations to English and prints the output of each rung. He assumes a 27-symbol alphabet, the twenty-six letters and a space. At the bottom rung, letters are picked independently and with equal probability, and the result is XFOML RXKHRJFFJUJ ZLPWCFWKCYJ FFJEYVKCQSGHYD. Give the letters their real English frequencies and it becomes OCRO HLI RGWR NMIELWIS EU LL NBNESEBY A TH EEI, which is still nonsense but has begun to look like nonsense written in English. Pick each letter according to the letter before it and you get ON IE ANTSOUTINYS ARE T INCTORE ST BE S DEAMY ACHIN D ILONASIVE, with real words appearing by accident. One rung further, each letter conditioned on the two before it, and you get IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS GROCID PONDENOME.

Then he switches from letters to whole words. Words drawn independently with their real frequencies give REPRESENTING AND SPEEDILY IS AN GOOD APT OR COME CAN DIFFERENT NATURAL HERE HE THE A IN CAME THE TO OF TO EXPERT GRAY COME TO FURNISHES THE LINE MESSAGE HAD BE THESE. Each word conditioned on the word before it gives THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD FOR THE LETTERS THAT THE TIME OF WHO EVER TOLD THE PROBLEM FOR AN UNEXPECTED. Shannon points at the run "attack on an English writer that the character of this" and observes that it is not at all unreasonable.

Two things about that ladder are worth holding on to. The first is that nothing on any rung knows any grammar. The only ingredient is how often one thing follows another. The second is how Shannon actually produced the samples, which he explains and which is charming: he opened a book at a random page, read until he found the current letter, and wrote down the letter after it, then turned to another page and repeated. A statistical language model in 1948 was a person with a novel and a pencil.

3. How predictable is English, exactly?

Three years later Shannon turned that observation into a measurement, in "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English", published in January 1951.

The question is how much of an English text is genuinely new information and how much the reader could have supplied. The unit is the bit, and the quantity is how many bits per letter you would need to send the text down a wire if you encoded it as efficiently as possible. If all twenty-six letters were equally likely you would need 4.70 bits each. Using their real frequencies brings that to 4.14. Conditioning on the previous letter brings it to 3.56, and on the previous two to about 3.3. Counting whole words rather than letters gets you to 2.62 bits per letter, because a word is a cohesive lump with strong internal statistics.

Table lookups run out at that point, so Shannon built a different instrument: a person. He picked a passage the subject had not read, and asked them to guess the first letter. If they were right they were told so and moved on; if they were wrong they were told the correct letter and moved on. In one run of 129 letters, 89 of them, 69 per cent, were guessed correctly on the first attempt. Errors clustered at the starts of words, where the line of thought can branch.

The sharper version asks the subject to keep guessing until they get it right, and records how many guesses that took. Over 102 symbols, the subject was right first time 79 times, second time 8 times, third time 3 times, fourth and fifth twice each, and needed more than five guesses only 8 times. That column of guess counts is itself a recoverable encoding of the original text, provided you have an identical twin of the guesser to run it backwards, and Shannon works out what it implies. Taking long-range structure into account, up to a hundred letters back, the entropy of ordinary literary English comes out at something of the order of one bit per letter, with a redundancy of roughly seventy-five per cent.

Three quarters of an English text is, in this precise sense, not telling you anything you could not have worked out. The claim that language is predictable stopped being an opinion in January 1951 and became a number.

4. The n-gram model, said plainly

Making that operational took twenty-five years and a different community. From about 1975 Fred Jelinek's group at the IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center, and James Baker at Carnegie Mellon, independently made counting work for speech recognition, where the machine must decide which words a noisy signal probably contains. The IBM group is where the terms language model and perplexity come from, and Jelinek used "language model" in the modern broad sense from the start, to mean the whole set of influences on which word comes next.

The thing they built is called an n-gram model and it is as simple as it sounds. Take a large pile of text. Count how often each run of two words occurs, then each run of three. To guess what follows "the cat sat on the", look up which words followed that run, or a shorter version of it, in the pile, and use those proportions as your probabilities. The whole model is a table of counts, divided through.

The mathematics underneath is older than the computer. In 1913 Andrei Markov classified twenty thousand letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin as vowel or consonant and computed the probability that a letter would be a vowel given the one or two before it. That is a bigram and a trigram model, worked by hand, on a poem.

5. Perplexity, and why lower is better

If a model assigns probabilities to the next word, you can score it on text it has never seen: run through the text, and at each position ask what probability the model gave to the word that actually turned up. A good model gave high probabilities, a bad one gave low ones. Perplexity packages that into one number, and the useful way to read it is as an effective number of choices. A perplexity of 100 means the model was, on average across the text, about as uncertain as somebody choosing uniformly between a hundred options. A perplexity of 20 means it had narrowed the field to about twenty. Lower is better, and the number is a direct descendant of Shannon's bits per letter.

This is the point at which language modelling became an engineering discipline rather than a debate. Two models can be compared on the same held-out text, the comparison produces a number, and the number moves when you change something. Almost every claim in the rest of this course is ultimately a claim that some number of this kind went down.

6. Most word runs have never happened

Counting has a ceiling, and it is sharp.

The problem is not that text is scarce, it is that word runs are astronomically more numerous than text. With a vocabulary of a hundred thousand words, the number of possible ten-word sequences is one followed by fifty zeros. No pile of text you can collect makes a dent in that. So the overwhelming majority of perfectly ordinary word runs have a count of zero in your table, which means a probability of zero, which means the model declares a sensible English sentence impossible.

Worse, the model has no way to notice that it has already seen an almost identical run. An n-gram model treats every word as an atom with no relationship to any other word. In its arithmetic dog and cat are exactly as unrelated as dog and Tuesday, because they are three different rows in a table and a table has no distances in it. Having seen "the cat sat on the mat" ten thousand times tells the model nothing at all about "the dog sat on the rug".

The patch that kept counting alive for twenty years is called smoothing, and its logic is to take a little probability away from everything you did see and hand it to everything you did not. The crudest version adds one to every count before dividing, which is Laplace's law of succession from 1812 pressed into service. Better versions discount cleverly and interpolate: when the five-word run has no count, fall back on the four-word run, and then the three, mixing the estimates together with weights that are themselves fitted to held-out text. By the turn of the century the standard was a method called modified interpolated Kneser-Ney, and it was very good.

Smoothing stops the model from saying "impossible" about things that are merely unseen. It does not give the model any notion that two words resemble each other, so it treats the ceiling rather than removing it.

7. The quip about firing linguists

The statistical turn has a famous one-line summary, and it belongs to Jelinek. It circulates as "every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recognizer goes up", and it gave him a reputation he spent years disowning.

In May 2004 Jelinek was awarded the Antonio Zampolli prize and gave an invited talk at the LREC conference titled "Some of my Best Friends are Linguists", specifically to take the reputation apart. His second slide is headed "The Quote", and his own wording is "Whenever I fire a linguist our system performance improves". He attributes it to his own talk "Applying Information Theoretic Methods: Evaluation of Grammar Quality", at a workshop on the evaluation of natural-language systems in Wayne, Pennsylvania, in December 1988. So the speaker is settled and the occasion is settled, and the wording that circulates is not the wording he gives. Treat any single version you meet, including the one above, as a paraphrase.

What the talk does next is more useful than the quip. Under the heading "When Linguists Left the Group", Jelinek sets out one task three ways. The differences are the entire argument of this lesson, in three rows.

Jelinek's own illustration, from his 2004 LREC talk: one speech-recognition task, three ways of building the same model
ModelWhere the numbers came fromAccuracy
1Expert-written pronunciations, statistics estimated by experts35%
2Expert-written pronunciations, statistics estimated automatically from data75%
3Spelling used in place of pronunciations, statistics estimated automatically43%

Read rows one and two together and the quip looks justified: keeping the experts' pronunciations but letting data set the numbers more than doubles accuracy. Read rows two and three together and it collapses: throw the experts' pronunciations away as well and accuracy falls by more than half. Jelinek's own reading, on the same slides, is that he and his colleagues always hoped linguistics would eventually let them strike gold, and that the quip accentuated a particular situation in the 1970s and 1980s rather than stating a principle. The thing that was being replaced was not linguistic knowledge. It was the practice of setting numbers by intuition when data was available to set them instead.

8. What it owes the next step

By the early 1990s the argument was settled in practice, and it left two debts.

The first is a proof obligation: counting had won on speech, but speech is a task where the answer is already a sentence of the language. Winning on translation, where the machine has to produce a sentence in a language it was not given, would settle it. Translation without a linguist is the moment that happened, in 1990, from the same group at IBM.

The second is the ceiling in section 6. Somebody had to find a way of representing a word that carries its resemblance to other words inside it, so that a model which has seen one sentence is already partway to handling another. Meaning as a direction in space is that story, and it took until 2003 to state and until 2013 to make cheap.

Retrieval practice

What did Shannon's ladder of approximations to English demonstrate?
Every rung of the ladder is a count of what follows what, and by the last rung the output reads almost like a sentence.
A model scores perplexity 20 rather than 100 on the same held-out text. What does that mean?
Perplexity reads as an effective number of choices, so a lower number means the model narrowed the field further at each position.
What does smoothing fix in an n-gram model, and what does it leave alone?
Shaving probability off seen runs stops the model calling unseen sentences impossible, but it still has no notion that two words resemble one another.
In Jelinek's own three-model illustration, what actually made the difference?
Keeping the experts' pronunciations but fitting the statistics to data took accuracy from 35% to 75%, while discarding the experts' pronunciations dropped it to 43%.
