Reading an eval table like a scientist

A benchmark number is a measurement made by someone with a stake in it, and six questions about how it was produced usually explain most of the gap between two competing claims.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.
Where this lesson starts

The disclosure collapse describes a record that closed. This lesson is the practical skill that follows from that. It is a method lesson, it can be read at any point, and it is the most transferable page in the course.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The disclosure collapse Once models became commercially valuable the technical reports stopped containing technique, so from 2023 onward a reader has to sort every claim into what is known, what is inferred, what is marketing and what is unverifiable.

The one tight idea

A benchmark number is a measurement made by someone with a stake in it, and six questions about how it was produced usually explain most of the gap between two competing claims.

2. A benchmark score is a measurement, and measurements have apparatus

A row in an evaluation table looks like a property of a model, the way a mass is a property of an object. It is not. It is the output of a procedure, and the procedure has a dozen settings, and whoever ran it chose all of them.

This is not a claim that laboratories cheat. Mostly they do not need to. Two teams can run what they both sincerely call "MMLU" on the same weights and get numbers thirty percent apart, with nobody misbehaving, because they made different reasonable choices about what counts as the model's answer. Section 4 works through exactly that case, with both sides published.

So the useful question in front of a table is never "is this true". It is what procedure produced this, and would the same procedure have produced the comparison number next to it. The disclosure collapse gives the reason this matters more after 2023 than before it: the architecture and the training data stopped being disclosed, so the eval table became the only quantitative thing a frontier laboratory publishes about its own model, and it is published by the seller.

3. Six questions to ask a number

These are in rough order of how often the answer turns out to explain the gap.

  1. Which harness? The evaluation code. Not the dataset - the software that feeds the dataset to the model and decides whether the reply was right.
  2. What scaffold? What surrounded the model. Was it allowed several attempts, a tool, a retry after a failing test, a longer thinking budget? A scaffolded score measures the system, not the model.
  3. Which prompt? The exact wording, the number of examples, the system message. On some benchmarks this moves the result more than a model generation does.
  4. Compared with what baseline? Which competitor, which version of it, and evaluated by whom under which of the settings above.
  5. Who ran it, and on exactly which weights? A vendor running a competitor is not the same evidence as a vendor running itself, and "the model" is not always the model the public can obtain.
  6. Could the test items be in the training data? Benchmarks are published on the internet. Training corpora are scraped from the internet. Nobody has ever fully solved this.

4. One model, one dataset, three different scores

In June 2023 the team maintaining Hugging Face's Open LLM Leaderboard published an investigation into a complaint they had received. Their leaderboard reported a much lower MMLU score for LLaMA than the LLaMA paper did, and people wanted to know which was wrong.

The answer was neither. There were three widely used implementations of MMLU in circulation, and they disagree about a single question: what counts as the model's answer to a multiple-choice question?

  • The original implementation from the benchmark's authors compares the model's probability for the four letters A, B, C and D and takes the highest.
  • Stanford's HELM asks the model to generate text and checks whether the text it generated is the right letter.
  • The evaluation harness the leaderboard used at the time compared the summed log-probabilities of the four full answer strings, letter and text together.

All three are defensible. All three were labelled "MMLU". Here is what they produce on the same downloaded weights.

Two things in that table are worth sitting with.

The first is the size of the swing. The same 65-billion-parameter model scores 0.637 and 0.488, a relative difference of about thirty percent, and nothing about the model changed. The investigation makes the practical point vividly: if you had reproduced LLaMA perfectly, evaluated it with the harness, and compared your 0.488 against the published 0.637, you would have concluded that you had ruined your own training run.

The second is the bold row. Under the original and HELM implementations, llama-65b beats falcon-40b comfortably. Under the harness, falcon-40b wins. The implementation changed the ranking, not just the scores. Anyone drawing a conclusion about which model was better in mid-2023 was, without knowing it, drawing a conclusion about which evaluation code they had run.

The investigation's own summary is the sentence to keep: evaluations are "strongly tied to their implementations - down to minute details such as prompts and tokenization", and "the mere indication of 'MMLU results' gives you little to no information about how you can compare these numbers to others".

5. Contamination, and the only clean way to test for it

Benchmarks are published so that people can use them. Training corpora are scraped from where things are published. So test questions leak into training data, and a model that has memorised an answer scores like a model that can work it out.

This is not a hypothetical failure and it is not new. The GPT-3 paper of 2020 says outright that a bug in its own filtering left benchmark overlaps in the training set and that retraining was too expensive, so the authors measured the effect instead of removing it. That is an unusually honest disclosure, and after 2023 nobody at the frontier discloses their corpus at all, so the same check cannot be run from outside.

Which leaves one clean method: build a new test the model cannot have seen, matched to the old one, and compare.

That is what Hugh Zhang and colleagues did in May 2024. They commissioned a thousand fresh grade-school maths problems, called the set GSM1k, and matched it to the widely used GSM8K on the properties that ought to make two maths tests equally hard: human solve rate, number of steps in the solution, and the magnitude of the answers. Then they ran leading open and closed models on both.

Their findings are worth quoting carefully in all three parts, because the middle one is usually dropped:

  • Accuracy dropped by up to 8 percent on the fresh set, and several model families showed systematic overfitting across almost all of their sizes.
  • The drop correlated with a direct measure of memorisation - how likely a model was to generate a GSM8K example verbatim - at a Spearman r-squared of 0.36.
  • And yet "many models, especially those on the frontier, show minimal signs of overfitting, and all models broadly demonstrate generalization to novel math problems guaranteed to not be in their training data".

So contamination is real, measurable, and unevenly distributed. It is not a reason to disbelieve every score, and it is a reason to distrust any score on a benchmark that has been public for years. The structural answer the field has moved toward is benchmarks that refresh - drawn from problems published after a model's training cutoff - because a test that did not exist when the model was trained cannot have been in the training data.

6. Saturation, and what a live disagreement looks like

A benchmark also dies the other way. Once the models it was built to separate all score above ninety, the differences between them fall inside the noise of the measurement, and the benchmark stops ordering anything. Several of the benchmarks that defined the 2020 to 2023 period - MMLU, HumanEval, the original GSM8K - are in that state by 2026: still quoted, no longer informative, because a two-point gap near the ceiling says more about the harness than about the models.

Their replacements are harder, and being harder makes them contested. Here is a case from July 2026, with both sides on the record.

Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 was reported at 80 percent on SWE-bench Pro, a benchmark of realistic software-engineering tasks. OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol was reported at 64.6 percent on the same benchmark. The day before the GPT-5.6 launch, OpenAI published an audit of SWE-bench Pro concluding that roughly 30 percent of its tasks are broken and advising model developers to examine results on it carefully.

The temptation is to decide who is right. Resist it, because the honest reading is that both statements can be true at once.

  • A benchmark assembled at speed from real repositories genuinely can contain a large fraction of broken tasks, and an audit finding so is a public service.
  • And an audit of an unfavourable benchmark, published by an interested party on the eve of its own launch, is also a competitive act, whatever its findings.

Neither observation cancels the other. What you can conclude is narrow and still useful: the 80-against-64.6 comparison should not be quoted as a fact about the two models until somebody with no stake re-runs it on the tasks that survive the audit. Sitting in that state - knowing precisely what you do not know - is the skill this lesson is trying to teach.

One more failure mode belongs here, because it answers the fifth question rather than the first. In April 2025 Meta submitted a variant of Llama 4 to LMArena, a site that ranks models by head-to-head human votes. The variant was not the model the public could download. LMArena said afterwards that this breached its expectations and changed its policy. The lesson is not about that episode - it is that "which model, exactly" is a real question with a real answer, and the answer is not always the name on the chart.

7. What a trustworthy evaluation report looks like

Turn all of the above around and you get a checklist. A report you can act on tells you:

  • The harness, by name and version. A repository and a commit, not "we evaluated on MMLU".
  • The full prompt. Including the number of examples and the system message, ideally as a file.
  • The scaffold, or its absence. Attempts allowed, tools available, thinking budget, retry policy.
  • Which weights. A model identifier the reader can obtain, not an internal name.
  • Baselines run the same way. Competitors evaluated under identical settings, or the differences stated.
  • A contamination check. Either a fresh or post-cutoff test set, or a stated overlap analysis.
  • Variance. More than one seed, and an interval rather than a single decimal.

Very few launch announcements meet that bar, which is precisely why The disclosure collapse files a self-reported eval table under marketing rather than under known. It is not an accusation. It is a description of what a number without its apparatus can support.

The habit is short enough to carry around: when someone shows you a benchmark table, ask what would have to be true for the comparison to be fair, and then ask whether they told you. Most of the time the second answer is no, and that is the finding.

9. Sources to read first

  • Fourrier, Habib, Launay and Wolf, What's going on with the Open LLM Leaderboard?, Hugging Face, 23 June 2023. The three implementations, the score table reproduced in section 4, the ranking flip, and the two quoted conclusions. It also lists the exact commit hashes of all three implementations, which is itself a model of how to report an evaluation.
  • Zhang et al., A Careful Examination of Large Language Model Performance on Grade School Arithmetic, submitted 1 May 2024. GSM1k, the matching criteria, the 8 percent figure, the Spearman correlation, and the finding that frontier models showed little overfitting.
  • Brown et al., Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, submitted 28 May 2020. Section 2.2 contains the admitted contamination-filtering bug quoted in section 5.
  • Simon Willison, The new GPT-5.6 family: Luna, Terra, Sol, 9 July 2026. The 80 against 64.6 comparison on SWE-bench Pro, the timing of the audit, and links to both parties' publications.

Retrieval practice

Why can the same weights score 0.637 and 0.488 on the same dataset?
One compares the probability of the four letters, another checks generated text, and a third compares whole answer strings.
What made the fresh grade-school maths set a clean test for contamination?
Matching the difficulty of the old set is what makes the drop attributable to memorisation rather than to the new questions being harder.
Why does a benchmark stop being useful once scores cluster above ninety?
A two-point difference near the ceiling tells you about the apparatus rather than about the models it was meant to rank.
What is the right conclusion when a vendor audits a benchmark it scores badly on?
Broken tasks and strategic timing are independent facts, and holding both leaves you with a smaller claim that is actually supported.
