Reading an eval table like a scientist

A benchmark number is a measurement made by someone with a stake in it, and six questions about how it was produced usually explain most of the gap between two competing claims.

Where this lesson starts

The disclosure collapse describes a record that closed. This lesson is the practical skill that follows from that. It is a method lesson, it can be read at any point, and it is the most transferable page in the course.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The disclosure collapse Once models became commercially valuable the technical reports stopped containing technique, so from 2023 onward a reader has to sort every claim into what is known, what is inferred, what is marketing and what is unverifiable.

The one tight idea

A benchmark number is a measurement made by someone with a stake in it, and six questions about how it was produced usually explain most of the gap between two competing claims.

2. What this lesson must cover

To write. Full prose, complete sentences, 1,400 to 2,000 words. Every bullet below is a beat the finished lesson owes the reader, in this order.

  • The six questions: which harness, what scaffold, which prompt, what baseline, who ran it, and how likely is contamination.
  • Saturation: what it means when everyone scores above ninety and why the benchmark stops being informative.
  • Contamination: how test items reach training data and why it is hard to rule out.
  • A worked example using two competing published results for the same benchmark, holding both possibilities open.
  • What a trustworthy evaluation report looks like, as a checklist the reader can apply.
Do not overclaim

Pick a worked example whose two sides you can both link. If you cannot link both, use a different example.
