The model they would not release

The first time a lab withheld a trained model on the grounds that releasing it might be harmful, it set the terms of an argument the field is still having.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson is about a decision rather than a mechanism. It assumes you know what the model in question could do, from GPT-2 and the bet on scale and Where the data actually came from.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • GPT-2 and the bet on scale Make the decoder much bigger and train it on much more text, and the fine-tuning step starts to become unnecessary: you describe the task in the prompt instead.
  • Where the data actually came from The dataset behind GPT-2 was scraped by its authors and filtered by a proxy for human approval, it was not a public crawl, and that filtering idea outlived the model that introduced it.

The one tight idea

The first time a lab withheld a trained model on the grounds that releasing it might be harmful, it set the terms of an argument the field is still having.

2. A new kind of announcement

On 14 February 2019 OpenAI published a paper and a blog post about GPT-2, a language model described in GPT-2 and the bet on scale. Four sizes had been trained. The announcement released the smallest and withheld the other three.

Withholding a model was not, at that point, a thing that machine learning labs did. The field's normal practice was a paper, then code, then weights, as fast as the authors could manage, because publishing was how you claimed a result and reproducibility was how the result was checked. A lab saying we built this and we are not giving it to you, because of what you might do with it was new.

This lesson is about that decision. It is not about whether the decision was right, and it will not tell you. It is about what was actually done, what was actually argued on each side, and why the argument has recurred in every year since.

3. What was released, and when

The withholding was not permanent. It was staged: a bigger model every few months, with a public write-up each time, over roughly nine months. OpenAI recorded the schedule itself, in an appendix to a report on the whole exercise.

Two details in that timeline matter more than the dates. The first is that the smallest model, which is fully capable of showing what the architecture does, went out on day one. The second is that by the final stage the largest model had already been reproduced by other people, which the next section returns to.

4. The argument they made

The reasoning was published at the time, under the heading Release Strategy, and it is short enough to quote and worth reading as written rather than as remembered.

"Due to concerns about large language models being used to generate deceptive, biased, or abusive language at scale, we are only releasing a much smaller version of GPT-2 along with sampling code. We are not releasing the dataset, training code, or GPT-2 model weights."

Then, immediately, an unusual amount of hedging for a corporate announcement:

"This decision, as well as our discussion of it, is an experiment: while we are not sure that it is the right decision today, we believe that the AI community will eventually need to tackle the issue of publication norms in a thoughtful way in certain research areas."

And an admission that the measure was porous:

"We are aware that some researchers have the technical capacity to reproduce and open source our results. We believe our release strategy limits the initial set of organizations who may choose to do this, and gives the AI community more time to have a discussion about the implications of such systems."

Read those three together and the claim being made is narrower than the one usually attributed to it. It is not this model is too dangerous to exist. It is we do not know, this is an experiment in publication norms, and the value we are buying is time. The word the post uses for the whole thing is "experiment", twice.

5. The argument against

The criticism arrived immediately and came in three distinguishable forms, which are worth separating because they are not the same objection.

That the risk was overstated. A model that scores 5 BLEU on translation and answers 4.1 per cent of trivia questions is not obviously a propaganda engine, and its own paper reports those numbers. On this view the harm was speculative and the cost to research was immediate.

That withholding functioned as promotion. An announcement whose headline is that the thing is too dangerous to release is an extraordinarily effective announcement, and the coverage that followed was larger than any comparable model had received. This objection does not require anyone to have acted in bad faith. It only observes that the incentive existed and was not disclosed.

That it did not work, and cost reproducibility. A determined actor can retrain the model; a researcher who wants to check the paper's numbers cannot, without weights. So the argument goes, withholding filters out exactly the people you did not need to stop.

OpenAI's own report supplies the strongest evidence for that last point, which is to its credit. It records that students working independently at the Technical University of Munich and at Brown University replicated GPT-2, one group choosing to publish and one choosing not to. The published one came with a community reconstruction of the training corpus, built from the recipe in the paper, and that reconstruction went on to be used by a great many other people.

And there is the sentence in the final post that concedes how the ground had shifted underneath: OpenAI continued the staged plan "in order to provide the community with a test case of a full staged release process", by which point larger language models had been released elsewhere. Nine months of withholding ended with the withheld model no longer being the largest one available.

6. What the evidence actually showed

Because OpenAI treated it as an experiment, it also published findings, and those findings are more interesting than either side's summary of them.

Humans did find the output convincing, and size mattered less than expected. A survey run with researchers at Cornell scored credibility on a ten-point scale. The 355M model scored 6.07, the 774M model 6.72, and the largest 6.91. The report's own reading is that the jump from the medium model to the large one was bigger than the jump from the large one to the largest, "indicating that the 1.5B model does not have a significantly higher capability for misuse than the 774M model". The thing being withheld was, on this measure, barely more dangerous than the thing already released.

Fine-tuning for misuse worked. Researchers at the Middlebury Institute's Center on Terrorism, Extremism, and Counterterrorism fine-tuned the model on texts from four extremist ideologies and were able to produce synthetic material in each of those voices. That is a real demonstration, and it cuts against the "risk was overstated" position.

Detection was possible and not good enough. OpenAI trained a classifier that detects text from the largest model with about 95 per cent accuracy, and released it. Five per cent is a very large error rate when the thing being classified is published at internet scale.

Notice that these three findings do not line up on one side. One weakens the case for withholding the largest model specifically, one strengthens the case that misuse is real, and one shows that the mitigation everybody hoped for is partial. That is what an honest experiment usually looks like.

7. What it changed

Whatever you think of the decision, a set of practices dates from it.

Staged release became a recognised option rather than an eccentricity: OpenAI's report notes that researchers at the Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence and the University of Washington took an incremental approach with their own generation model, and that Hugging Face declined to release some internal models. Model cards, which GPT-2 got in November 2019, became routine. So did pre-release work with outside partners, of the kind the Cornell and Middlebury studies were.

The counter-current dates from it too. The reconstruction of the training corpus, built because the original was withheld, became a foundation of the open-dataset effort, and the argument that a capability should be public precisely because only a public one can be checked has been made continuously since. The leak that started an ecosystem picks that thread up at the point where it stops being an argument and becomes an industry.

And the decision itself scaled up. Fifteen months later the same lab released its next model through a paid interface rather than as a download, and gave three reasons, one of which was a direct descendant of this one: it is "inherently safer to release them via an API and broaden access over time, rather than release an open source model where access cannot be adjusted if it turns out to have harmful applications". GPT-3, and learning without learning covers that turn. The disclosure collapse covers where it ends up.

8. How to hold this

The useful stance is not a verdict. It is a habit: when you meet this argument again - and in this course you will meet it at open weights, at model cards, at safety evaluations and at every frontier release - name which of the two columns in Figure 2 a given claim belongs to, and ask what evidence would settle it.

The reason to refuse a verdict here is not politeness. It is that the two sides are arguing about different quantities. One is about a harm that has not happened and may not; the other is about a check that cannot be run today. Nothing in the 2019 record measures the first, which is precisely why the debate could not end and has not.

Do not score this one

You will find confident claims in both directions: that the staged release was vindicated by the extremism fine-tuning result, and that it was discredited because the model was reproduced within months. Both cite real evidence and neither settles it, because "what would have happened otherwise" was never observable. Report what was argued, what was done, and what followed.
