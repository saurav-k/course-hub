The fork: GPT-1 and BERT

Apply the same pretraining recipe to the two halves of the Transformer and you get two incompatible models, one that understands and one that writes, and for eighteen months the understanding one wins.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later establishes pretrain-then-specialise. This lesson is about the architectural fork that recipe ran into, and why the branch that looked like the loser in 2018 is the one everything runs on now.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later Train one model on a mountain of unlabelled text, then adapt it cheaply to each task, and the field's economics invert: the expensive step happens once instead of once per task.

The one tight idea

Apply the same pretraining recipe to the two halves of the Transformer and you get two incompatible models, one that understands and one that writes, and for eighteen months the understanding one wins.

2. Same block, one rule apart

The architecture published in June 2017, described in The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked, is a translation system with two halves. The encoder reads the source sentence; the decoder writes the translation. Both halves are stacks of the same kind of block.

The blocks differ in exactly one rule, and it is a rule about what each position is allowed to see.

In the encoder, every position may attend to every position, in both directions. The representation of word three is informed by words one, two, four and five, all at once.

In the decoder, a position may attend only to itself and to the positions before it. Word three sees words one and two, and nothing to its right. This is not an architectural difference so much as a mask: the scores for the forbidden positions are set to a value that makes their weight zero before the blend is taken.

The reason for the restriction in the original paper is mundane. The decoder is writing the translation one word at a time, and at the moment it writes word three, words four and five do not exist yet, so it had better not have been trained to rely on them.

The BERT paper names both halves by these rules in a footnote, and the naming is exact: the bidirectional version is often called a Transformer encoder, and the left-context-only version is called a Transformer decoder, since it can be used for text generation.

So the fork is this. Take the pretraining recipe, and apply it to one half or the other. Keep the encoder and you get a model in which every word is informed by its whole sentence, which is ideal for judging text and structurally unable to write any. Keep the decoder and you get a model that can continue text indefinitely, at the cost of every word being informed only by what came before it.

3. GPT-1, June 2018

Alec Radford, Karthik Narasimhan, Tim Salimans and Ilya Sutskever at OpenAI published Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training on 11 June 2018. There is no arXiv version; the paper was released on OpenAI's own site alongside the code.

Its recipe is one sentence, and the name of the entire era comes from it: generative pre-training of a language model on a diverse corpus of unlabelled text, followed by discriminative fine-tuning on each specific task.

The model is a twelve-layer decoder-only Transformer with masked self-attention, 768 numbers per position and twelve attention heads. It was trained on BooksCorpus, over 7,000 unpublished books, chosen over the alternative corpora because, as ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later describes, it preserved whole documents rather than shuffling sentences. It improved the state of the art on nine of the twelve datasets it was measured on.

The paper never states a parameter count, which is worth knowing because a specific number is often quoted for it. The commonly cited figure comes from the following year's GPT-2 paper, which says its smallest model is equivalent to the original GPT, and OpenAI later corrected that model's own count. The honest description is about 117 to 124 million parameters, with that history attached.

The part of GPT-1 that gets forgotten is the part that matters most for what follows. It did not build a different architecture for each of its twelve tasks. It reformatted the input. For deciding whether one sentence implies another, it joins the two with a separator token and reads the result as a single stream. For choosing between answers, it runs the model once per candidate answer. The paper calls these task-aware input transformations requiring minimal changes to the model architecture.

That is the ancestor of prompting. In June 2018, the idea that you specify a task by arranging the text you feed the model is already present, in a paper about fine-tuning, with a small model, before anybody had discovered that a large enough model does not need the fine-tuning step at all.

4. BERT, October 2018

Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee and Kristina Toutanova at Google posted BERT on 11 October 2018, four months later, taking the other branch.

Its argument against the decoder branch is stated plainly. It is reasonable to believe, they wrote, that a deep bidirectional model is strictly more powerful than either a left-to-right model or the shallow concatenation of a left-to-right and a right-to-left model. The first half of that is aimed at GPT and the second half at ELMo, which had stitched two one-directional models together rather than training a genuinely two-directional one.

Then they name the obstacle, and it is a good one. You cannot simply train a bidirectional language model, because standard language models can only be trained left to right or right to left: bidirectional conditioning would let each word indirectly see itself, and predicting a word you can already see is not a task.

Masked language modelling

Their answer is to change the objective rather than the architecture. Take the input and hide 15 percent of its tokens at random, replacing them with a placeholder. Ask the model to predict the hidden ones, using everything on both sides of each hole. Nothing can see itself, because the thing to be predicted is not in the input.

The paper notes, pleasantly, that this is a task educational researchers have used since 1953 under the name Cloze. The objective is sixty-five years older than the model.

A second objective, predicting whether one passage follows another, was included and later work showed it was largely unnecessary.

The comparison was deliberately head to head. BERT-base is twelve layers, 768 numbers per position, twelve heads, 110 million parameters, and the paper says outright that this size was chosen to match GPT for comparison purposes. BERT-large is twenty-four layers and 340 million parameters. It was pretrained on BooksCorpus, the corpus GPT-1 had used, which BERT's paper puts at 800 million words, plus 2,500 million words of English Wikipedia.

It set new state of the art results on eleven tasks, pushed the GLUE benchmark score to 80.5, and reached 93.2 on the SQuAD reading comprehension test. At matched size, on the benchmarks everyone was scored on, it beat GPT-1.

5. Why a bidirectional model cannot write

This is the structural fact that the leaderboards of 2018 hid, so it is worth stating carefully.

Generating text means producing one token, then producing the next one given everything so far, and repeating. That requires a model whose training matches that situation: it must have been trained to predict a position from its left context only, because at generation time the right context does not exist.

An encoder trained by masking has never been in that situation. It has always been shown a complete passage with holes in it, and it fills the holes using both sides. Ask it to continue a sentence and there is no right side, which is an input unlike anything it was trained on. You can coax filling one hole at a time out of it, and people have, but it is slow, it is not what the model was optimised for, and it produces poor text.

There is a second, quieter cost. The masked objective only produces a learning signal at the hidden positions, which is 15 percent of them. The next-word objective produces one at every position. Per token of text consumed, the decoder branch extracts more supervision, and that difference compounds enormously once the amount of text becomes the thing you are scaling.

6. Why the leaderboard favoured understanding, for about eighteen months

From late 2018 through most of 2019 essentially everyone in language research built on BERT, and each of the four reasons was rational at the time.

It won the comparison. At matched size, on GLUE and SQuAD, BERT beat GPT-1.

The benchmarks measured what it was good at. This is the deepest of the four reasons. The tasks the field scored itself on were classification and extraction: is this sentence positive, does this passage entail that one, which span of this paragraph answers this question. Every one of those hands the model a complete text and asks for a judgement, which is exactly the shape an encoder is built for. Open-ended generation was not on any leaderboard, because nobody had an agreed way to score it. A field measures what it can measure, and then optimises for the measurement.

The weights were released, immediately. Google published BERT's code and trained checkpoints, so anyone could use it without doing the expensive step. Four months later, when OpenAI's next model arrived, that lab declined to release its weights, which is the subject of The model they would not release.

It was cheap to use. Fine-tuning BERT on a task took a single graphics card and an afternoon, so a graduate student could produce a state-of-the-art classifier. The evidence for how much that mattered is dated: on 29 October 2018, eighteen days after the BERT preprint appeared, a small company called Hugging Face created a repository named pytorch-pretrained-BERT, described as a version of Google's pretrained BERT model for a different framework. That repository became the library the entire open-model ecosystem is built around. It started as a port of one released checkpoint, which is the most concrete available argument about what releasing weights actually buys.

7. Why the other branch won in the end

Four things eventually favoured the decoder branch, and only one of them is about quality.

The architecture and the task became the same thing. An encoder-decoder maps one sequence to a different sequence, which is what translation is. Language modelling maps a sequence to itself shifted by one position. There is no second sequence, so the encoder has nothing to encode, and its cross-attention pathway has nothing to point at. Deleting it is not a simplification for its own sake; it is removing a part with no job.

One objective covers everything, if the input carries the task. GPT-1 already reformatted its input per task rather than adding an architecture per task. Push that idea further and the task description itself can be written into the text, at which point there is no fine-tuning stage, no task head, and no separate loss: there is one model doing one thing. An encoder cannot follow that path, because it has no way to produce a continuation. GPT-2 and the bet on scale is where that argument is made.

It has a use for scale. A model whose only job is predicting the next token gets better at that job as it gets larger and reads more, without anything else in the recipe changing. That is what makes an enormous training run a coherent thing to buy, and it is why the arguments in Predicting the future with a straight line apply to this branch and not neatly to the other.

It is cheaper to serve. When a decoder-only model generates its next token, it can reuse every key and value it already computed for the tokens before it, because those positions never see anything to their right and so never change. That reuse is what makes generation affordable, and it is the subject of Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs. For a product charged by the token, this is not a footnote.

BERT did not lose

Encoder models are still the right tool for classification, retrieval and producing embeddings, and they are far cheaper than a generative model at all three. They are running in production today, in search and recommendation systems, in enormous numbers. What happened is not that the encoder branch was refuted; it is that the interesting problem moved to open-ended generation, and the encoder branch could not follow it there. When the question was properly tested in 2022, on models of the same size trained on the same data, decoder-only with a next-token objective came out strongest for the setting where you describe a task in the input and expect an answer. The same study found that a model with unrestricted visibility over its input, trained with a masked objective and then fine-tuned on many tasks, performed best overall. Decoder-only won the regime that turned into a product. It did not win every regime.

8. What to take from the fork

The general lesson here is worth more than the specific history, because it recurs.

In 2018 two groups applied one recipe to two halves of one architecture and got different answers, and for eighteen months the field built almost entirely on the branch that turned out not to be the one that scaled. Nobody was careless. The comparison was run at matched size, honestly, and the losing branch really did score lower on the benchmarks that existed.

What the benchmarks could not measure was the thing that turned out to matter: an objective that produces a signal at every position, that has a use for arbitrary amounts of text and compute, and that can be run forward to produce something a person can read. The measurement was fine. The measurement was answering a different question.

Reading an eval table like a scientist is the lesson that generalises this, and it is worth reading next to this one.

Retrieval practice

What single difference separates the two halves of the Transformer?
It is a mask rather than an architecture: the forbidden positions get a weight of zero, and every other part of the block is identical.
Why does masking make bidirectional training possible at all?
Predicting a word you can already see is not a task, so the word is taken out of the input before the model is asked for it.
Why did the benchmarks of 2018 favour the encoder branch?
Classification and extraction hand the model a whole passage and ask a question about it; open-ended generation was on no leaderboard because nobody could score it.
What is the correct thing to say about BERT today?
Encoders are still the cheap and correct tool for classification and retrieval; the interesting problem moved to generation and they could not follow it there.
