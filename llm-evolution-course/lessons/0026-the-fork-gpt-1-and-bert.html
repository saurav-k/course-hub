The fork: GPT-1 and BERT

Apply the same pretraining recipe to the two halves of the Transformer and you get two incompatible models, one that understands and one that writes, and for eighteen months the understanding one wins.

Where this lesson starts

ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later establishes pretrain-then-specialise. This lesson is about the architectural fork that recipe ran into, and why the branch that looked like the loser in 2018 is the one everything runs on now.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later Train one model on a mountain of unlabelled text, then adapt it cheaply to each task, and the field's economics invert: the expensive step happens once instead of once per task.

The one tight idea

Apply the same pretraining recipe to the two halves of the Transformer and you get two incompatible models, one that understands and one that writes, and for eighteen months the understanding one wins.

2. What this lesson must cover

To write. Full prose, complete sentences, 1,400 to 2,000 words. Every bullet below is a beat the finished lesson owes the reader, in this order.

  • Encoder-only against decoder-only, and the single difference that separates them: whether a word may see its future.
  • Masked language modelling as the objective that makes bidirectional training possible.
  • Why a bidirectional model cannot generate text left to right.
  • The benchmark story of 2018 and 2019, and why the leaderboard favoured understanding.
  • The reason the decoder branch eventually won: one objective, no task heads, and a use for scale.
Do not overclaim

Do not say BERT lost. It is still the right tool for many classification and retrieval jobs. Say that the generative branch turned out to scale into something the encoder branch could not.
