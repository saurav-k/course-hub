Predicting the future with a straight line

Model loss falls as a power law in size, data and compute, which means a straight line on a log-log plot, which means you can forecast the result of a run you have not paid for.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Until 2020 deciding how big to build a model was a matter of taste and budget. This lesson is about the measurement that turned it into a forecast. It teaches the log-log plot inline and assumes no statistics background.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • GPT-2 and the bet on scale Make the decoder much bigger and train it on much more text, and the fine-tuning step starts to become unnecessary: you describe the task in the prompt instead.

The one tight idea

Model loss falls as a power law in size, data and compute, which means a straight line on a log-log plot, which means you can forecast the result of a run you have not paid for.

2. The decision that had no method

Suppose it is 2019 and you have money for one training run. How big should the model be?

Until January 2020 there was no principled answer to that. You looked at what had worked before, multiplied by something, and hoped. GPT-2 and the bet on scale ends on exactly this note: its four models got smoothly better as they got bigger, so bigger looked worth doing, but nothing said how much bigger or what you would get.

This was an expensive thing not to know. A large training run is a single irreversible purchase. You cannot try three sizes and keep the best one, because you can only afford one, and you find out whether the choice was good several weeks after making it.

On 23 January 2020, ten authors led by Jared Kaplan and Sam McCandlish published Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models, which turned that guess into a forecast. This lesson is about the shape of the answer, which is the part a reader needs; the fitted exponents and the derivation live in the papers course lesson on scaling laws.

3. What a power law is, drawn rather than defined

First, the quantity being predicted. Loss is a single number saying how badly the model predicts the next token: lower is better, and it can never reach zero because language is genuinely unpredictable. Everything below is about how loss falls as you spend more.

Plot loss against compute on ordinary axes and you get a discouraging picture. The curve drops steeply at first, then bends, then crawls along nearly flat. Read naively, that says the returns are gone and further spending is wasted.

Now change the axes. Instead of marking them 1, 2, 3, 4, mark them 1, 10, 100, 1000, so that each equal step along the axis is a multiplication rather than an addition. That is a logarithmic axis, and doing it to both axes gives a log-log plot.

The flattening curve becomes a straight line.

A relationship that is straight on log-log axes is called a power law, and in prose it says one thing: multiplying the input by ten multiplies the loss by a fixed factor, every time. Not subtracting a fixed amount. Multiplying by a fixed factor. The slope of the line is what that factor is.

This is the one piece of arithmetic the lesson needs, and it can stay in words. If the slope is 0.05, then a tenfold increase in compute cuts the loss to about nine tenths of what it was, and it does that again for the next tenfold increase, and again for the one after. A hundredfold buys two of those steps, a thousandfold buys three.

4. What was varied, and over what range

The paper reports a separate power law for each of three quantities, and each holds only when the other two are not the thing holding you back.

The three fits, with the exponent and the measured span each was drawn over. The exponents are the paper's Section 1.2.
What is variedExponentRange the fit was measured over
Model sizeabout 0.076six orders of magnitude
Training dataabout 0.095over two orders of magnitude
Computeabout 0.050eight orders of magnitude

Eight orders of magnitude means the smallest and largest compute budgets in the study differ by a factor of a hundred million, and the line is straight across all of it. That is a lot of evidence for one line, and it is why the result was taken seriously immediately.

Alongside it came a finding that changed what people worked on, stated in the abstract: "Other architectural details such as network width or depth have minimal effects within a wide range." Scale mattered enormously and shape barely mattered at all. A field that had spent years inventing architectures now had a paper saying that inventing another one was worth less than buying more machines.

5. Why a small exponent is the good news

An exponent of 0.05 looks feeble. Multiply your compute by ten and the loss falls by roughly a tenth of itself. That is easy to read as a disappointment, and it is the opposite.

The important property is not the size of the number. It is that the number does not change. A shrinking exponent would mean each tenfold spend bought less than the one before, and the strategy of spending more would eventually stop working. A constant exponent means the tenth tenfold increase pays exactly as well as the first, and nothing in the eight orders of magnitude the study covers shows it shrinking.

Compound that. Ten thousand times the compute is four of those steps, and four steps of a fixed multiplicative gain is a much larger difference than the flattening curve on ordinary axes suggested. The whole industrial argument of the following three years is this sentence: the exponent is small, it is constant, and money is a thing you can multiply.

6. What it is used for

The practical payoff is a planning procedure, and it is worth seeing as a sequence because each step is a real decision somebody makes.

The paper also answers the allocation question directly, and its answer is the most consequential sentence it contains: "As the computational budget C increases, it should be spent primarily on larger models, without dramatic increases in training time or dataset size."

The empirical fit behind that instruction is a pair of exponents. Model size is fitted to grow as compute to the power 0.73, and the data as compute to the power 0.27. Do the arithmetic on those two and ten times the compute means a model about five and a half times bigger, on less than twice the data. Read that instruction, then look at what the industry built over the following two years, and the causation is not subtle. Models grew from hundreds of millions of parameters to hundreds of billions while training data stayed near a few hundred billion tokens.

GPT-3, and learning without learning is what happened when somebody followed this rule at the largest scale anyone had attempted.

7. The caveats the paper wrote down itself

This is the part that gets dropped in retelling, and it is the part that makes the story honest.

A fit is valid over its measured range and promises nothing outside it. A straight line through eight orders of magnitude is strong evidence about those eight orders of magnitude. Extending it is an extrapolation, and an extrapolation is a hope with arithmetic attached.

The authors flagged an internal contradiction. Section 6.3 of the paper is titled "Contradictions and a Conjecture". It observes that two of the three curves eventually cross, so "our scaling laws must break down before this point", and that where the crossing falls "is highly sensitive to the precise exponents from our power-law fits". The authors said, in print, that their own laws must fail somewhere and that they could not say exactly where.

The constants are not fundamental. In the paper's own words, the numerical constants "depend on the vocabulary size and tokenization and hence do not have a fundamental meaning". Change the tokenizer, as Tokens: how text becomes numbers describes, and the numbers move. This is not a law of nature. It is a regularity of a particular measurement setup, and it should always be quoted with that setup attached.

And the allocation rule turned out to be wrong. Not the power laws, which have held up. The instruction about how to split a budget between size and data. Two years later a study that varied one more thing found the opposite split, and by then a great deal of money had been spent the other way. Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint is that story, and the two lessons are kept next to each other in every route through this course so that the claim and its correction arrive together.

A fit, not a law

Quote a scaling law with the range it was fitted over, or do not quote it. The word "law" here means an observed regularity in one set of experiments with one tokenizer, one architecture family and one dataset, and the authors say so themselves. Nothing in this lesson implies that loss keeps falling forever, and the paper explicitly says the trends must eventually level off, since natural language has non-zero entropy.

Retrieval practice

What does a straight line on log-log axes tell you?
Both axes count multiplications rather than additions, so a straight line means a constant ratio rather than a constant difference.
Why is an exponent as small as 0.05 encouraging rather than not?
A constant multiplicative gain compounds, and nothing in the measured eight orders of magnitude shows it shrinking.
What did the paper say a growing compute budget should mostly buy?
That instruction is why models grew a thousandfold while training sets stayed near a few hundred billion tokens, and it is the part later corrected.
What should always accompany a quoted scaling law?
It is an empirical fit rather than a law of nature, and outside its measured span it is an extrapolation with no evidence behind it.
