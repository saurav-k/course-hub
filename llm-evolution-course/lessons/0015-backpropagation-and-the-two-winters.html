Backpropagation, and the two winters

The field stalled twice for two different reasons: the first was an argument about what a single layer could represent, and the second was an obstacle in the arithmetic of training deep stacks.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

What a neural network actually is describes training as nudging weights downhill. This lesson asks how a network with many layers knows which weight to nudge, and what went wrong twice when the answer was not enough.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • What a neural network actually is A network is a stack of two operations, multiply by a table of numbers and then bend the result, and training is nudging those numbers until a measured error gets smaller.

The one tight idea

The field stalled twice for two different reasons: the first was an argument about what a single layer could represent, and the second was an obstacle in the arithmetic of training deep stacks.

2. Which of a million numbers caused this?

A network is a stack of layers, each of which is a table of weights, and training means changing those weights so that a measured error gets smaller. That description is fine until you ask a practical question about a stack more than one layer deep.

The error is measured at the output. A weight in the first layer is several layers away from that measurement. It never touches the output; it affects the numbers handed to the second layer, which affects the numbers handed to the third, and so on. So when the output is wrong by some amount, how much of that is this particular weight's fault, and would nudging it up or down help?

This is called the credit assignment problem, and at any realistic size it is brutal. A network with a million weights has a million of these questions, and they all have to be answered before a single update can be made. Answering one of them by experiment, by holding everything else still and re-running the network to see what happens, would mean a million runs per update. A model with a hundred billion weights would need a hundred billion. That is not slow, it is impossible, and the whole subject depends on there being a better way.

3. Blame, passed backwards

There is a better way, and its shape is worth understanding even though this course will not derive it.

Run the input forward through the stack and keep the intermediate results. Now start at the output, where you know exactly how wrong the answer was, and work backwards one layer at a time. At each layer you already know how much the layer's output was to blame; the weights of that layer tell you how each of its inputs contributed to that output; so you can divide the blame among those inputs and hand the shares backwards to the layer beneath. That layer then does the same for the layer beneath it.

One sweep forward, one sweep backward, and every weight in the stack has been told which way to move. Not a million runs: two. That procedure is backpropagation, and it is the reason there is a field at all.

What matters for the rest of this lesson is the arithmetic of the backward sweep. Every hop backwards multiplies the blame by something before passing it on. Nothing is added along the way; the signal that reaches a layer is the signal from the layer above it, scaled. Remember that word, multiplies. Section 6 is entirely about it.

4. The first stall was an argument, and the argument was correct

The first collapse of the field had nothing to do with the arithmetic. It was a proof about what a network without depth cannot do.

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, and the result everybody remembers is about exclusive-or. Exclusive-or is the rule "one of the two, but not both": 0,0 gives 0, 0,1 gives 1, 1,0 gives 1, and 1,1 gives 0. A single unit of Rosenblatt's kind computes a weighted sum of its two inputs and outputs 1 if that total passes a threshold. Written out, the boundary between the cases it answers 0 and the cases it answers 1 is a straight line.

Draw the four cases as four points on a page, with the two that should give 1 diagonally opposite each other. No straight line separates them from the other two. A single unit therefore cannot compute exclusive-or, and no amount of adjusting its two weights will change that. The rules and and or, by contrast, are each separable by one line without any difficulty, which is what makes the failure so pointed.

Two things are usually got wrong about this episode.

The first is that the result was a mistake. It was not. It is a correct statement about what one layer of that kind can represent, and it is still taught as such in the standard textbooks.

The second is that it proved neural networks could not work. It did not, and the answer was known at the time: stack a second layer, and the pair can compute exclusive-or comfortably. What nobody had was a practical way to train a stack, which is the credit assignment problem of section 2. The result was correct, the response to it was an overcorrection, and funding and interest collapsed for about fifteen years.

5. Why it took until 1986

Backpropagation became common practice with a three-page paper in Nature in October 1986 by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors".

That paper did not invent the computation. The same idea had been arrived at more than once by then, notably by Paul Werbos in 1974, and the standard textbooks cite the earlier work alongside the 1986 paper. What 1986 did was make it known: it landed in a general-science journal, in the middle of a broader revival of interest, with a demonstration that a trained stack develops useful internal structure of its own rather than merely fitting the data.

That is a pattern this course meets repeatedly and it is worth naming here, because the same shape recurs with attention, with the Transformer and with scaling. The gap between an idea existing and an idea mattering is usually not intellectual. It is about who was in a position to notice, what else was in the air, and whether the machines could run it.

6. The second stall was arithmetic, and nobody could argue with it

The moment depth became trainable, a second obstacle appeared, and this one was not an argument that could be overturned. It was a property of the multiplication in section 3.

Every backward hop scales the blame. If that scale factor is consistently a little below one, the blame shrinks geometrically as it travels. Halve it a couple of times and 1.0 becomes 0.25, which is figure 1 and is survivable. Multiply by a half twenty times and 1.0 becomes less than one millionth. The weights in the earliest layers receive an instruction so faint that it is indistinguishable from noise, so those layers barely move at all. The network trains its last few layers and leaves the rest essentially as they were initialised.

The same argument runs the other way. If the scale factor is consistently above one, the blame grows geometrically: multiply by 1.5 twenty times and 1.0 becomes more than three thousand, and the update blows the weights apart. These are the two failure modes, called vanishing and exploding gradients, and they are the same phenomenon with the multiplier on either side of one.

The result was proved rather than merely observed. Yoshua Bengio, Patrice Simard and Paolo Frasconi published "Learning long-term dependencies with gradient descent is difficult" in the IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks in March 1994. A later joint treatment by Sepp Hochreiter, Bengio, Frasconi and Jurgen Schmidhuber states the boundary sharply: if the per-step scale factor exceeds one the error blows up, and if it is below one the error vanishes exponentially and nothing can be learned in acceptable time. With the S-shaped activation functions that were standard at the time, they show that weights below a certain modest size guarantee you are on the vanishing side, and note that this is especially true at the start of training, which is exactly when the network most needs the instruction.

Read that last part carefully, because it is the point of this lesson. The scale factor is below one by default. Nothing has gone wrong. Nobody has written a bug, chosen a bad learning rate or initialised carelessly. The behaviour follows from what the network is made of, and you cannot fix it by being more careful. Fixing it means changing the architecture so that the signal has a route that is not repeatedly multiplied.

7. Why sequences suffer worst

A network reading a sentence has this problem in a sharper form, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of degree.

A network that reads a sequence applies the same small network once per word, carrying a running summary forward from step to step. Reading a thirty-word sentence therefore means thirty applications in a row, and the backward sweep has to travel back through all thirty. A stack thirty layers deep was unusual in the 1990s and something you might choose to avoid. A sentence thirty words long is not a design decision; it is what a sentence is. So the vanishing gradient is not an occasional inconvenience for a network that reads text, it is the default condition, and it bites hardest on exactly the dependencies that matter: the ones that reach far back.

Sequences, memory, and the LSTM is the architecture built to answer this specific problem, and its central trick is to give the memory a route through the network that is added to rather than multiplied through.

8. Two stalls, two different causes

The popular version of this history is a single story called the AI winter, in which a good idea was unfairly dismissed and later vindicated. The record does not support that. There were two stalls with two different causes and two different resolutions.

The first was a representational limit, correctly identified, that the field overreacted to for fifteen years. It was answered by a training method. The second was a numerical property of that very method, correctly identified, that no amount of care could get round. It was answered by architecture, and every architecture in the rest of this course carries the answer somewhere in it.

9. What it owes the next step

This lesson leaves a debt with a precise shape. Depth is now trainable in principle, and unusable in practice for anything long, because the instruction fades as it travels backwards. Text is long by construction. Somebody had to build a network in which information could travel a long way through time without being multiplied down to nothing on the way back. Sequences, memory, and the LSTM is that build, published in 1997 and largely ignored for over a decade, and the reason for that delay is a lesson of its own.

Retrieval practice

What problem does backpropagation solve?
Testing each weight by experiment would take one run per weight, so the backward sweep that divides blame across a whole stack is what makes training affordable.
What did the 1969 exclusive-or result actually establish?
It is a correct statement about one layer, and the answer, stacking a second one, was known at the time; what was missing was a way to train the stack.
Why do gradients vanish in a deep stack?
A factor slightly below one applied twenty times leaves under a millionth of the original signal, which is why the earliest layers receive no usable instruction.
Why is the vanishing gradient worse for a network reading a sentence?
A thirty-word sentence means thirty applications in a row, so the backward sweep has to survive thirty multiplications whether the designer likes it or not.
