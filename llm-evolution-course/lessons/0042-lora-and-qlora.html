LoRA and QLoRA: fine-tuning for the rest of us

You can adapt a large model by training a small number of extra numbers alongside the frozen originals, and if you also store the originals at lower precision the whole job fits on one ordinary machine.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

The open-weights ecosystem needed a way to specialise a model without a data centre. This lesson is that way. It assumes you know what training a weight means, from What a neural network actually is.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • What a neural network actually is A network is a stack of two operations, multiply by a table of numbers and then bend the result, and training is nudging those numbers until a measured error gets smaller.
  • The leak that started an ecosystem A research-only model release leaked within a week, and the tooling that appeared in the following month showed there had been enormous unmet demand for weights anyone could run.

The one tight idea

You can adapt a large model by training a small number of extra numbers alongside the frozen originals, and if you also store the originals at lower precision the whole job fits on one ordinary machine.

2. The problem: adapting a model used to mean rebuilding it

Fine-tuning is the step that turns a general model into a specific one: keep training it on a smaller, narrower body of text until it writes the way your domain writes. ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later is the lesson about why the field organised itself this way, and the arrangement is a good one, because the expensive training run happens once and everyone reuses it.

The trouble is that "cheap" was only cheap relative to pretraining. Fine-tuning as originally practised updates every weight in the model, and to update a weight you must hold, alongside the weight itself, its gradient and the optimiser's running statistics about it. In common practice that is several numbers of bookkeeping per parameter, which is why the memory needed to fine-tune a model is a large multiple of the memory needed to run it.

The LoRA paper states the resulting figure for the largest model of its day. Fine-tuning a 175-billion-parameter model in the ordinary way needed 1.2 terabytes of accelerator memory. That is not a laptop problem or a workstation problem. It is a cluster.

There was a second cost, and for anyone running a product it was worse. Every fine-tuned version is a complete copy of the model. Ten customers with ten specialisations means ten times 350 gigabytes on disk, and swapping between them means loading a new model. The paper opens on exactly this: "deploying independent instances of fine-tuned models, each with 175B parameters, is prohibitively expensive".

3. The idea: leave the model alone and train a correction beside it

Edward Hu and colleagues at Microsoft published Low-Rank Adaptation in June 2021, and the idea is one sentence.

Freeze every original weight. Add a small trainable correction next to each one. Train only the correction, and add its output to the frozen layer's output.

Nothing about the model's shape changes. Input goes in, the frozen layer computes what it always computed, the small correction computes a small adjustment, and the two are added.

That is the one piece of arithmetic this lesson does, and it is worth doing in words because it is the whole efficiency argument. A layer's weights form a square table, twelve thousand by twelve thousand in the largest model here, which is about 151 million numbers. The correction is stored as two thin tables instead: one that squeezes the input down to four values, and one that expands those four back out to full width. Their product has exactly the shape of the big table, so it can be added to it, but storing them costs two times twelve thousand times four rather than twelve thousand squared.

The number four is called the rank, and it is the width of the waist. Why four is enough is the interesting question, and this course answers it at intuition level only. The claim is that what fine-tuning changes about a model is far simpler than what the model contains. Pretraining installed a very rich object; adapting it to legal drafting or to one company's support tickets is a comparatively low-dimensional adjustment, and a narrow waist is enough to express it.

The paper tested that directly and its own result surprised its authors: on the tasks they measured, "a rank as small as one suffices". On one benchmark, rank 1 scored 73.4 and rank 64 scored 73.5 - a sixty-fourfold difference in the size of the correction, and no difference in the result. LLM Papers: lora carries the rank ablations and the mechanism in full, and this course stops here.

4. What that buys

Four things, all from the paper's own measurements on the 175-billion-parameter model.

Ordinary fine-tuning against adapter fine-tuning on a 175-billion-parameter model, from the LoRA paper's sections 4.1 and 5.
QuantityFine-tune everythingTrain the correction only
Trainable numbers175,255.8 million4.7 million
Memory to train1.2 TB350 GB
Size of the saved result350 GB35 MB
Accuracy, one benchmark73.873.4
Accuracy, another benchmark89.591.7

Read the last two rows before the others. The point of the table is not that the cheap method is nearly as good. On one of these two benchmarks it is better, and the paper reports being on par or better across the models it tested. This is a rare thing in engineering: a method that is three orders of magnitude cheaper and does not cost you anything measurable.

The third row is the one that changed what people could build. A saved adaptation is 35 megabytes, roughly ten thousand times smaller than the model it adapts. So instead of ten copies of a model, you keep one model in memory and ten small files, and switching a customer's specialisation means swapping a file rather than loading a model. The paper describes exactly this: "many customized models that can be swapped in and out on the fly on machines that store the pre-trained weights in VRAM".

One more property, easy to miss and important: there is no additional cost at inference time. Because the correction is added to the frozen weights, you can add it once, permanently, and serve an ordinary model. A method that made every request slower would not have been adopted, whatever it saved during training.

5. Quantization, only as far as this lesson needs it

The adapter fixed the bookkeeping. It did not fix the base. Even with nothing to train, the frozen weights still have to be held in memory, and holding 175 billion numbers at two bytes each is 350 gigabytes. That is why the middle column of the table above still says 350 GB. Better than 1.2 terabytes, still not a machine anyone has.

Which raises the obvious question: does each number need two bytes?

Quantization is the answer, and the idea is the same one as storing a photograph with fewer colours. A weight is stored in a format with fewer possible values - four bits instead of sixteen gives sixteen possible values per number instead of many thousands - and each stored weight is the nearest available value to the real one. The model gets four times smaller. Every weight is now slightly wrong.

Where the quality goes is worth being precise about, because "it gets a bit worse" is not a mechanism. Each individual rounding error is tiny. The risk is that they accumulate: a network is a long chain of multiplications, so small errors early can be amplified by the time they reach the output. The engineering of a good quantization format is entirely about spending the few available values where the weights actually are, rather than spreading them evenly over a range that is mostly empty.

By 2023 this was well understood for running a model. It was not understood for training one, because training needs gradients to flow back through the weights and a coarse grid of values is a bad surface to compute gradients on.

6. The combination, and the machine it fits on

QLoRA, published in May 2023 by Tim Dettmers, Artidoro Pagnoni, Ari Holtzman and Luke Zettlemoyer at the University of Washington, put the two ideas together in the only way that works.

Store the frozen base at four bits. Keep the small trainable correction at full precision. Push the gradients back through the quantized base to reach the correction, and update only the correction.

Nothing being trained is quantized, so the coarse-grid problem does not arise. Nothing at full precision is large, so the memory problem does not arise.

The paper's headline is a memory figure and a machine. Fine-tuning a 65-billion-parameter model in the ordinary 16-bit way "requires more than 780 GB of GPU memory". QLoRA does it in under 48 gigabytes, "without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a 16-bit fully finetuned baseline".

Forty-eight gigabytes is one accelerator. That is the whole significance of the number.

The paper demonstrated it by training a family of models it called Guanaco and reporting that its largest reached "99.3% of the performance level of ChatGPT" on a chatbot benchmark after 24 hours on a single accelerator.

Read that claim the way its own authors ask you to

The comparison was scored by having a large model judge which of two answers was better, on a benchmark of eighty prompts. In the same abstract the authors write that "current chatbot benchmarks are not trustworthy to accurately evaluate the performance levels of chatbots", and the paper includes a section on where its model visibly fails. So the memory figure is known - it is a measurement of a machine - and the 99.3 percent is a benchmark result whose own authors tell you not to lean on it. Reading an eval table like a scientist is the general form of this distinction.

7. What it made ordinary

The two papers are dated June 2021 and May 2023, and almost nothing happened with the first one for eighteen months. That is not because it was overlooked. It is because there was no large model outside a laboratory to adapt, so a cheap way of adapting one had nobody to be cheap for.

The leak that started an ecosystem is the lesson about the week that changed: a research-only model release circulated publicly at the start of March 2023, and a repository applying adapter fine-tuning to it appeared on 13 March, eleven days later. The method had been sitting on the shelf, complete, waiting for its input.

Two months after that, QLoRA removed the last hardware requirement, and the shape of the ecosystem settled into the one that still exists:

  • A small number of organisations pay for pretraining, which nothing in this lesson makes cheaper.
  • Everyone else downloads the result and specialises it, on hardware they already own, for the cost of an afternoon.
  • Specialisations are shared as small files rather than as models, so a domain adaptation is something you publish rather than something you host.

There is a quieter consequence too, and it points at where this course goes next. Quantization began as something you did to somebody else's finished model, accepting some damage in exchange for fitting it on your machine. By 2025 laboratories were shipping models already trained and evaluated in a low-precision format, so the reduced precision was no longer a compromise applied afterwards but the format the model was made in. That is a real change in what the artefact is, and it belongs with the rest of the cost story in Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs.

Retrieval practice

Why is fine-tuning a model far more expensive than running one?
The bookkeeping per parameter is several times the parameter itself, which is why fine-tuning memory is a multiple of inference memory.
Why can a very narrow correction be enough to specialise a model?
Pretraining installs the rich object; adapting it is a low-dimensional adjustment, which is why a rank as small as one worked in the paper's tests.
Why does a 35-megabyte saved adaptation change what can be built?
Before, a specialised model meant a full copy per customer; afterwards it means one shared base in memory and a file each.
Why does combining the two methods avoid the usual problem with 4-bit training?
Gradients pass through the quantized base but land on a full-precision correction, so nothing being updated sits on a coarse grid.
