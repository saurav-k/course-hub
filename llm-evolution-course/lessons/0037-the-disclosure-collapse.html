The disclosure collapse

Once models became commercially valuable the technical reports stopped containing technique, so from 2023 onward a reader has to sort every claim into what is known, what is inferred, what is marketing and what is unverifiable.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Everything in this course before 2023 can be checked against a paper you can open. This lesson is about the point where that stopped being true, and it introduces the labelling habit the rest of the course uses.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • A text box changes everything The breakthrough of late 2022 was distribution rather than research: an existing recipe, tuned for conversation, put behind a free text box that anyone could use without reading anything first.

The one tight idea

Once models became commercially valuable the technical reports stopped containing technique, so from 2023 onward a reader has to sort every claim into what is known, what is inferred, what is marketing and what is unverifiable.

2. A technical report that reports no technique

GPT-4 was announced in March 2023, and the accompanying document was submitted to arXiv on the 15th of that month as the GPT-4 Technical Report. Its second section is titled "Scope and Limitations of this Technical Report" and it is short enough to quote almost in full:

"This report focuses on the capabilities, limitations, and safety properties of GPT-4. GPT-4 is a Transformer-style model pre-trained to predict the next token in a document, using both publicly available data (such as internet data) and data licensed from third-party providers. The model was then fine-tuned using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). Given both the competitive landscape and the safety implications of large-scale models like GPT-4, this report contains no further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, training method, or similar."

Read the list at the end slowly, because it is the list of everything you would need in order to know what the thing is. Architecture. Model size. Hardware. Training compute. Dataset construction. Training method. A reader is told the model predicts the next token and was tuned with human feedback, which had been true of every model from that laboratory since 2019.

The document gives its own reason for this in the same sentence - the competitive landscape and the safety implications - and this lesson quotes that reason without going beyond it. Whether either consideration was the operative one, and in what proportion, is not public. Guessing at it would be exactly the kind of unsupported claim the rest of this lesson is about.

The report is not empty, and saying so is part of being fair to it

Section 3 of the same document describes something genuinely useful: the team fitted a power law to the loss of models trained with up to ten thousand times less compute and predicted the final loss of the large run before it finished, accurately. That is a real methodological result, published. What the report withholds is not its findings but the reproduction path, and those are different things. The complaint is not that the document is thin. It is that nobody outside the laboratory can check any of it.

3. What that looked like against its own neighbours

The rupture is easiest to see by putting the report next to documents from the same field, some of them only weeks older.

The GPT-3 paper of May 2020 contains a table listing all eight models the team trained, with parameter count, layer count, model width, head count, batch size and learning rate for each, ending with the 175-billion-parameter model at 96 layers and a width of 12,288. A second table lists the training corpus by name, token count and sampling weight: filtered Common Crawl at 410 billion tokens and 60 percent of the mix, WebText2 at 19 billion and 22 percent, two book collections, and Wikipedia at 3 billion and 3 percent. It goes further than that, and admits a mistake: a bug in the filtering meant some benchmark test items were not removed from the training data, and retraining was too expensive, so the paper characterises the remaining overlap instead of hiding it.

The LLaMA paper of February 2023 - three weeks before the GPT-4 report - lists its corpus the same way, and builds an argument on the listing: that a state-of-the-art model can be trained from publicly available data alone, which you can only claim if you say what the data was.

So the closure was not the field growing up and getting serious. It was one laboratory, at one moment, deciding differently from its own contemporaries, and then the rest of the frontier following.

4. Four labels, and the questions that assign them

If you cannot check a claim, you can at least know that you cannot check it. This course therefore sorts every factual claim about a model into one of four states, and it uses these words from here to the end.

The four claim labels used throughout this course. The test is the question you ask before assigning one.
LabelMeaningThe test question
KnownA document you can open states it, or the artefact itself proves it.Can I point at a paper, a licence file, a model card or a configuration file that says this?
InferredSomebody derived it from evidence and showed the derivation.Is there a named method here that I could re-run and disagree with?
MarketingIt originates in a launch announcement and exists to sell. It may well be true.Does it come with a harness, a prompt, a baseline and a way to reproduce it?
UnverifiableWidely repeated, never confirmed, and no method exists to settle it.If I wanted to check this, what would I even do?

Two consequences follow, and both are uncomfortable.

The first is that a self-reported benchmark table from a frontier laboratory is marketing by this test, not because anyone is lying but because the harness, the prompt and the scaffold are unstated and the baseline was chosen by the party being measured. Reading an eval table like a scientist is the whole of that argument.

The second is that unverifiable is a real answer and not an evasion. A course that says "we do not know how big GPT-4 was" is more accurate than one that repeats a confident number, and the fact that a flagship model's size is unknowable is itself the historically important thing.

5. How a number becomes common knowledge without becoming evidence

Here is the worked example, and it is the most useful thing in this lesson because the pattern is general.

Through the middle of 2023 a specific description of GPT-4's architecture began to circulate: a mixture-of-experts model of roughly 1.8 trillion parameters spread across sixteen experts. It is specific enough to sound like a leak, and it was treated as one. Within months it appeared in commentary, then in explainer articles, then in conference talks, and by 2024 it was being stated flatly, without hedging, as a thing everybody knew.

Trace it backwards and the chain runs out. The claim originates in unattributed industry conversation. No named person with access ever put their name to it. OpenAI has neither confirmed nor denied it, then or since. There is no measurement anyone can run from outside that would settle it, because the model is reachable only through an interface that returns text.

Notice what happened at each hop, because this is the mechanism rather than the anecdote. The original claim carried a hedge - somebody said, unconfirmed, reportedly. Each retelling shortened the hedge, because a hedge is the least interesting part of a sentence and the first thing an editor cuts. After four or five hops the hedge was gone and the number remained, and the number now had the social weight of having been repeated everywhere, which reads exactly like the weight of having been verified. No step in that chain added a single piece of evidence.

The defence is a habit rather than a rule: when a number about a closed model is quoted to you, ask who measured it and what they measured it with. If that question has no answer after two hops, the number is unverifiable, and saying so costs you nothing.

6. What is still checkable, and where to look

This lesson is not an argument that nothing can be known after March 2023. A surprising amount can, and it moved to different places.

  • The artefact itself, when it is downloadable. An open-weight release ships a configuration file, and that file is a fact rather than a claim. GLM-5.2's states 78 layers, a width of 6,144, 256 routed experts with 8 active per token and one shared, a vocabulary of 154,880 and a position limit of 1,048,576. Nobody has to be trusted for any of that. You can read the file.
  • Papers from the laboratories that still publish them. DeepSeek-V3's paper states its own training cost as 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours. That is a measured quantity the authors chose to disclose, and it is the only frontier-scale training cost in the public record that comes from the people who ran the job.
  • Licence files. What you may legally do with a model is written down, in a file, in plain language, and Four axes of open: weights, code, data, licence is about reading it.
  • Repository timestamps. When a laboratory published something, and whether it has published anything since, is a matter of record on a public model host. Who ships open weights now is built entirely out of that one observable.
  • Behaviour, measured by somebody with no stake. An independent evaluation with a published harness is weaker evidence than an architecture diagram and much stronger than a launch table.

There is a pattern in that list worth stating out loud. The open-weight ecosystem is now where the checkable facts live. Not because those laboratories are more honest, but because a downloadable artefact cannot keep a secret about its own shape, and a rented interface can keep all of them. That is one of the reasons the open-weights story in the next few lessons matters beyond who gets to run a model cheaply.

The habit this lesson leaves you with is small and it costs one clause. When you write down a fact about a model, write down where it came from. Every claim in the rest of this course that is not known carries its label, and any claim you meet elsewhere that carries none is asking you to supply the trust yourself.

  • OpenAI, GPT-4 Technical Report, submitted 15 March 2023. The block quotation in section 2 is the whole of the report's own section 2, transcribed from the PDF. The predictable-scaling result defended in the callout is its section 3.
  • Brown et al., Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, submitted 28 May 2020. The eight-model architecture table is Table 2.1, the corpus table with sampling weights is Table 2.2, and the admitted filtering bug is in section 2.2.
  • Touvron et al., LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models, submitted 27 February 2023. Section 2.1 lists the corpus by source and percentage, three weeks before the GPT-4 report.
  • DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report, submitted 27 December 2024. The 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours figure is in the abstract and section 1.

The current openai.com site blocks automated fetching, so the GPT-4 report was read as the arXiv PDF rather than from any announcement page. The 1.8-trillion-parameter claim in section 5 is deliberately given no citation, because giving it one would misrepresent what it is: this lesson could find no named source with access behind it, which is the point being made.

Retrieval practice

What makes the GPT-4 report a rupture rather than an ordinary short paper?
Its section 2 lists architecture, size, hardware, compute, data and method as things it will not discuss, so the omission is a policy rather than an oversight.
Why is a launch-day benchmark table labelled marketing rather than known?
The label is about reproducibility, not about honesty: without a named harness and baseline there is nothing for a reader to re-run.
What actually happens as an unsourced figure spreads?
The qualification is the first thing an editor cuts, so repetition converts a rumour into apparent consensus while adding no evidence at all.
Why do the checkable architecture facts now live with open-weight releases?
The configuration ships with the weights, so layer and expert counts are properties of an artefact rather than assertions anyone has to be trusted for.
