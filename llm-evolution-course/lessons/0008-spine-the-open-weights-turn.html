The open-weights turn

One set of leaked weights in early 2023 revealed enormous latent demand and created a parallel ecosystem, and the word open has been fought over ever since because it is really four separate questions.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.

Chapter 8 of 10. Reading the spine on its own takes an evening. Next chapter: Making it cheap.

Where this lesson starts

When the record closed left the frontier labs disclosing less. This chapter is about the counter-movement that formed in the same months, what it produced, and how the meaning of open has drifted since.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • When the record closed The moment language models became commercially valuable, the papers stopped saying how they were built, so a reader of this field now needs a way to tell a measurement from a claim.

The one tight idea

One set of leaked weights in early 2023 revealed enormous latent demand and created a parallel ecosystem, and the word open has been fought over ever since because it is really four separate questions.

2. A research release, and a torrent

On 24 February 2023, ten days after the report described in When the record closed declined to say how a frontier model was built, Meta published a family of models called LLaMA. The weights were not public. They were available to approved researchers, under a licence that forbade commercial use.

The paper's argument mattered more than the models. It claimed that you could train state-of-the-art models using publicly available datasets exclusively, without proprietary and inaccessible data, and it backed that with a 13-billion-parameter model that outperformed the 175-billion-parameter model of 2020 on most benchmarks. That claim, that public data is sufficient, is the ideological seed of everything in this chapter. It is also a direct consequence of the correction described in The scaling bet: once you know a smaller model trained far longer beats a larger one trained briefly, a small model becomes worth building.

On 3 March 2023, a week later, the weights leaked. Somebody posted a torrent and the link circulated. Within days the largest model in the family was everywhere.

That the leak happened, and roughly when, is settled. Who did it, and what Meta's internal response was, is not, and this chapter is not going to speculate.

3. What the leak actually proved

The interesting part is not the leak. It is what appeared in the four weeks after it.

People wrote software to run the models on ordinary laptop processors instead of graphics cards. People built compressed storage formats that cut the memory needed to hold a model by three quarters, at a small cost in quality. People worked out how to adapt the models to new behaviour by training a small number of extra numbers alongside the frozen originals, cheaply enough to do at home; Making it cheap covers how that works. People generated instruction-following datasets from a paid model's outputs and used them to make the leaked base model conversational.

None of that was hard, and none of it happened before, because nobody outside a handful of organisations had ever had a capable model's weights to work with. The four weeks were a measurement of latent demand, and the measurement came back enormous. Meta's decision to release the next generation openly is best read as a response to that demonstration.

4. "Open" is four questions, not one

In July 2023 Meta released the next generation with public weights and a licence that sounds permissive. The Open Source Initiative, which maintains the definition of the term, said publicly that the licence is not an open-source licence, and used the phrase "open washing" about the marketing around it.

Both sides had a point, and the argument is unresolvable while everybody keeps using one word for four separate things. Ask four questions instead.

  1. Weights. Can you download the parameters and run the model yourself?
  2. Code. Can you see the code that trained it, not just the code that runs it?
  3. Data. Do you know what it read?
  4. Licence. What are you allowed to do with it, including commercially?

The 2023 release scores yes, partly, no and no. You can download it and run it. Inference code is public and the training pipeline is not. The data is described in general terms and not disclosed. And the licence carries a threshold: an organisation above 700 million monthly active users has to ask Meta for separate permission, at Meta's discretion, plus a list of forbidden uses.

That threshold clause is the crux, and it is worth understanding why it disqualifies the licence rather than merely annoying purists. An open-source licence, by the standard definition, cannot discriminate between users or between fields of endeavour. A clause that says "everyone may use this except the handful of companies large enough to compete with us" is exactly such a discrimination. You may think that is a perfectly reasonable commercial position. It is still not what the words "open source" mean, and conflating the two makes it impossible to describe the actual landscape.

The counter-example arrived in September 2023, when a French laboratory released a 7-billion-parameter model under the Apache 2.0 licence, announced by a magnet link and nothing else. No user threshold, no field-of-use restriction, no permission to ask for. For a couple of years that set the bar, and it made the alternative look restrictive by comparison.

5. Where this stands in August 2026

Three things are true at once, and they pull in different directions.

The weights really are downloadable, at frontier-adjacent capability. This is new and it is genuine. A model of a size that would have been the outright frontier in 2023 can now be fetched by anybody with the disk space.

The data is disclosed by essentially nobody. Even the most permissively licensed frontier-scale releases state a token count and nothing about composition. The third of the four questions in figure 2 goes unanswered across the board, closed and open alike.

The licence has split in two. Small models are Apache 2.0 or MIT and genuinely unencumbered. Several of the largest open-weight models now carry a revenue-triggered commercial gate, written to catch one specific business: reselling the model as a hosted service. Below the threshold you may do as you like, including running it inside your own company. Above it you have to go and negotiate.

The honest one-sentence summary of what "open weights" means in 2026: you can run it and adapt it, not necessarily rebuild it or resell it at scale.

6. Who is actually shipping, and how far behind

Two facts about the shape of the open-weight ecosystem are worth stating carefully, because both are easy to get wrong in opposite directions.

The first is an absence. Meta, the organisation that opened this door in 2023, has published no new open-weight base language model since April 2025. That is KNOWN in a specific and limited sense: it is a statement about which artefacts exist in public repositories, which anybody can check. Why it happened is UNVERIFIABLE, and this chapter will state the absence and stop there. Over the same period several laboratories based in China have shipped frontier-adjacent open weights on a cadence of roughly every six to ten weeks, and they now set the open-weight frontier.

The second is a measurement of the gap. The research organisation Epoch AI publishes a composite capability index and reports that, since January 2026, the most capable open-weight models trail the leading closed models by an average of about four months. Their earlier estimate covering 2023 to late 2025 was around three months. Label that INFERRED: it depends entirely on how that index is constructed and on which closed models are publicly benchmarked at all, and Epoch themselves note the gap may be understated because laboratories keep unreleased models out of the comparison. Cite the number with the method attached, and do not round it to "open has caught up".

There is one more consequence of open weights that only becomes visible at the end of this course. A model distributed under a permissive licence and copied onto thousands of machines cannot be withdrawn by anybody, including the organisation that trained it and including a government. A model that exists only behind an interface can be switched off in an afternoon. That asymmetry stopped being theoretical during 2026, and The reasoning turn, and where we are records what happened.

Meanwhile there was a much more ordinary problem in the way. A large open-weight model that anybody may download is not much use if almost nobody can afford to tune it or serve it, and by 2023 that was exactly the situation. Fixing it took four separate ideas, and it is the subject of Making it cheap.

7. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: The leak that started an ecosystem A research-only model release leaked within a week, and the tooling that appeared in the following month showed there had been enormous unmet demand for weights anyone could run.
  • Deep dive: Four axes of open: weights, code, data, licence Open is not one question but four independent ones, almost nothing scores well on all four, and the word is now used for releases that would fail three of them.
  • Deep dive: Who ships open weights now The set of organisations publishing frontier-adjacent open weights has changed completely since 2023, and the change is a fact about release artefacts rather than a story about intentions.

Retrieval practice

What did the March 2023 leak actually demonstrate?
Laptop runtimes, compressed formats, cheap adaptation and instruction datasets all appeared within about four weeks, because none of it was hard.
Why is a licence with a user-count threshold not open source?
"Everyone except those large enough to compete with us" is a discrimination between users, which the standard definition rules out.
Which of the four axes is empty across nearly every release?
Open and closed laboratories alike state how much text a model read and decline to say what it consisted of.
How should the absence of new Llama base models be reported?
What anyone can check is which artefacts exist in public repositories; the motive behind their absence is not available to be checked.
