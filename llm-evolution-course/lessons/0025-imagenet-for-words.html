ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later

Train one model on a mountain of unlabelled text, then adapt it cheaply to each task, and the field's economics invert: the expensive step happens once instead of once per task.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Before 2018 every task needed its own labelled dataset and its own model. This lesson is about the recipe that ended that, and about a choice inside it that decided everything afterwards.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked Delete the recurrence and keep only attention, and every position in a sentence can be computed at the same time with a constant number of operations between any two words.

The one tight idea

Train one model on a mountain of unlabelled text, then adapt it cheaply to each task, and the field's economics invert: the expensive step happens once instead of once per task.

2. The economics before 2018

Suppose in 2017 you wanted a system that decided whether a film review was positive. You would collect several thousand film reviews and pay people to label each one. You would design an architecture for that task, train it from scratch, and tune it. When you then wanted a system that decided whether one sentence implied another, you would start again: a different labelled dataset, a different architecture, a different training run.

Every task carried its own data cost, its own design cost and its own training cost, and none of the three transferred. The GPT-1 paper states the binding constraint of that arrangement in one sentence: labelled data for these specific tasks is scarce, which makes it hard for models trained that way to perform adequately.

Scarce is the operative word. There is an enormous amount of text in the world and almost none of it comes with a label attached. The whole field was working from the thin slice of language that somebody had been paid to annotate, and ignoring the rest.

3. What vision had already done

Computer vision had solved the same problem five years earlier, and the solution had a name people used casually.

Train a large network on ImageNet, a dataset of a million-odd labelled photographs across a thousand categories. Then, for whatever you actually cared about, take that trained network and adapt it rather than starting over. The features it had learned for telling a terrier from a bus turned out to be useful for reading an X-ray or sorting a warehouse. By 2017 essentially nobody in vision trained from scratch, and this practice is what 2012: the year the hardware caught up describes the consequences of.

Text had no equivalent, and the reason it had no equivalent is interesting. Vision's transferable knowledge came from a labelled dataset, and building a labelled dataset for language at that scale is far harder, because there is no agreed set of thousand categories that a sentence belongs to.

The move that unlocked it was to notice that language does not need labelling, because the text already contains its own answer key.

4. Why the supervision is free

Take any sentence and hide the next word. Ask the model to predict it. Check whether it was right. You have just constructed a training example with a correct answer, and nobody was paid anything.

Every word in every document on the internet is one such example. The label is the text itself, one position along, which is why this is called self-supervision rather than supervision. A thousand-word article is a thousand training examples.

ULMFiT, the January 2018 paper that argued for this most directly, makes the case in one clause: language modelling provides data in near-unlimited quantities, for most domains and languages. Compare that with an annotation budget.

The second half of the argument is that predicting the next word is not a trivial task that teaches nothing. To predict the last word of "the trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big" you need to have followed what "it" refers to. To predict the next word after "the capital of France is" you need a fact. To predict how a sentence ends you often need to have registered whether its author is angry. ULMFiT names exactly this: language modelling captures many facets of language relevant to downstream tasks, including long-range dependencies, hierarchical relations and sentiment. That is why the paper calls language modelling the ideal source task and a counterpart of ImageNet for natural language processing, which is where this lesson's title comes from.

5. Two papers, four weeks apart, two answers

In the first quarter of 2018 two papers took the idea seriously and reached opposite conclusions about one crucial detail. Both matter, and neither is the paper people usually name.

ULMFiT, January 2018

Jeremy Howard, at fast.ai and the University of San Francisco, and Sebastian Ruder, at the Insight Centre in Galway and at Aylien in Dublin, posted Universal Language Model Fine-tuning for Text Classification on 18 January 2018. It proposes three stages. Pretrain a language model on general text, in their case WikiText-103, which is 28,595 preprocessed Wikipedia articles and 103 million words. Then continue training that same language model on the text of your target domain, so it adapts to how people write there. Then attach a classifier and fine-tune the whole thing on the labelled task.

Their measured claims are strong. They beat the state of the art on six text classification datasets, reducing the error by 18 to 24 percent on most of them. And with only 100 labelled examples they matched training from scratch on a hundred times more data, which is the transfer-learning claim stated in the currency that matters.

The paper also contributed the three techniques that made it work, all of them about not destroying what the pretrained model knew: different learning rates for different layers, a learning-rate schedule that rises quickly and decays slowly, and unfreezing the layers one at a time from the top rather than training everything at once. Those exist because the obvious approach fails: fine-tune all of a pretrained model aggressively and it forgets what it learned during pretraining, which is a well-named failure called catastrophic forgetting.

ELMo, February 2018

Matthew Peters and six colleagues at the Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence and the University of Washington posted Deep contextualized word representations on 15 February 2018.

Its aim is narrower and its answer is different. Pretrain a language model that reads in both directions, then freeze it, and use its internal states as very good word vectors that get fed into whatever task model you already had. Its innovation is that the different layers of the network capture different things, so the task is allowed to learn its own weighted mixture of them rather than being handed only the top layer. It improved the state of the art on six problems including question answering and textual entailment, and it was easy to adopt because it slotted into existing systems without redesigning them.

Neither of these used a Transformer

This is the detail the compressed version of the story loses. ULMFiT's language model is an LSTM, and its authors describe it as a regular LSTM with no attention and no other sophisticated additions. ELMo's is two layers of bidirectional LSTM with 4,096 units each. Both papers are about the recipe, not the architecture, and both explicitly expect the recipe to work better with a better language model. The Transformer arrives in this story later, when somebody drops it into the slot these two papers had already built.

6. Freeze or fine-tune, and why it decided everything

The two papers disagree about one question, and it turns out to be the question. Does the pretrained model stay separate from the task, or does it become the task?

ELMo says separate. The pretrained model is a better source of word vectors; the task model is still yours to design and train. ULMFiT says it becomes the task. There is one model; you continue training it, and at the end there is no separate task architecture at all.

Fine-tuning won, and it is worth being precise about why, because "it scored better" is only part of it.

A frozen model can only be as useful as its fixed outputs happen to be for your problem, and it leaves the task-specific design work exactly where it was: you still need an architecture per task, which was one of the two costs the whole exercise was meant to remove. Fine-tuning removes both costs at once. There is one architecture, the pretrained one, and adapting it to a task is a short training run rather than a design exercise.

The consequence is the shape of everything after 2018. If the pretrained model is the task model, then making the pretrained model better makes every task better simultaneously, and there is exactly one thing worth investing in. That is the reasoning that turns into the enormous single training runs of the following years, which GPT-2 and the bet on scale takes up.

7. One expensive step, many cheap ones

Stated as economics, the change is an inversion.

Before: cost per task equals a labelling budget plus a design effort plus a full training run. Ten tasks cost ten of those.

After: one very large training run over unlabelled text, paid once, plus a short fine-tuning run per task using a small labelled set. Ten tasks cost one large run plus ten small ones. ULMFiT's measurement of the second half of that is the one to remember: a hundred labelled examples doing the work of ten thousand.

Two things follow that are not obvious.

The first is that this concentrates power. When the expensive step happens once and everyone reuses it, whoever can afford the expensive step decides what everyone else builds on. In 2018 that was a research budget; within five years it was a strategic asset, and much of the argument in Four axes of open: weights, code, data, licence is about who gets to use the result.

The second is that the choice of pretraining text stops being an implementation detail and becomes a decision with consequences, because it is baked into everything downstream. This is the point where the field started arguing about data, and it started immediately.

8. The data argument, which started at once

The three pretrained models of 2018 made three different choices of text, and one of those choices was criticised by name in the paper that came next.

What each 2018 pretraining run was trained on, from each paper's own description.
ModelPretraining textWhat it is made of
ULMFiT, January 2018WikiText-10328,595 Wikipedia articles, 103 million words
ELMo, February 2018The 1B Word BenchmarkNews text, shuffled so sentences are in random order
GPT-1, June 2018BooksCorpusOver 7,000 unpublished books, whole and in order

The GPT-1 paper explains its choice by ruling ELMo's out, and names it while doing so. Its reason is that BooksCorpus contains long stretches of contiguous text, which lets a generative model learn to condition on long-range information, whereas the 1B Word Benchmark, which it notes is used by a similar approach, ELMo, is approximately the same size but is shuffled at sentence level, destroying long-range structure.

Read that carefully. Two corpora of roughly the same size, and one of them is far more valuable, because one preserves the order of a document and the other does not. A model trained on shuffled sentences can never learn that the thing referred to in paragraph four was introduced in paragraph one, because it has never seen a paragraph four.

BERT reached the same conclusion independently a few months later, saying in its own paper that it is critical to use a document-level corpus rather than a shuffled sentence-level corpus such as the Billion Word Benchmark, in order to extract long contiguous sequences.

Two teams, working separately, both stopping to say that a widely used benchmark corpus was the wrong shape. The lesson generalises well beyond 2018: the size of a dataset tells you very little, and what it preserves tells you a great deal. Where the data actually came from follows this argument forward into the era when the corpora were the whole internet.

9. What this sets up

By the middle of 2018 the recipe was settled: pretrain one model on a large body of unlabelled, document-structured text using next-word prediction, then fine-tune the whole thing for each task. The remaining question was which architecture to put in the slot, and by then there was an obvious candidate that neither ULMFiT nor ELMo had used.

Two groups did exactly that within four months of each other, and they made opposite choices about which half of the Transformer to keep. The fork: GPT-1 and BERT is that story.

Retrieval practice

Why is unlabelled text the point rather than a compromise?
Hide the next word and check against the document: no annotator is involved, so the supply of examples is the supply of text.
What separated ELMo's answer from ULMFiT's?
Freezing leaves you designing an architecture per task; fine-tuning collapses the pretrained model and the task model into one thing.
Why did fine-tuning winning matter for what came next?
If the pretrained model is the task model, there is exactly one artefact worth spending money on, which is the reasoning behind every large run after 2018.
Why did two separate teams object to the 1B Word Benchmark?
A model that never sees paragraph four cannot learn that something introduced in paragraph one is still being referred to.
