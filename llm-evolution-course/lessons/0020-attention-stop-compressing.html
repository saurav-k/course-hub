Attention: stop compressing

Do not squeeze the source sentence into one vector at all: keep every position, and at each output step learn a weighting that says which positions matter now.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Sequence to sequence, and the eight thousand numbers ends with a fixed vector that cannot hold a long sentence. This lesson is the fix, and it is the single most important mechanism in the course. It assumes you know what an encoder and a decoder are.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Sequence to sequence, and the eight thousand numbers Read a whole sentence into a single fixed list of numbers and then write another sentence out of it, and that single list is both the elegance and the flaw.

The one tight idea

Do not squeeze the source sentence into one vector at all: keep every position, and at each output step learn a weighting that says which positions matter now.

2. The move, in one sentence

On 1 September 2014 Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio posted a paper whose statement of the problem is hard to improve on. A potential issue with the encoder-decoder approach, they wrote, is that the network needs to compress all the necessary information of a source sentence into a fixed-length vector, and this may make it difficult for the network to cope with long sentences. Their fix, in their own words, frees the model from having to squash all the information of a source sentence, regardless of its length, into a fixed-length vector.

Here is the whole move. Replace one summary, computed once at the end of reading, with a summary recomputed from scratch for every word the decoder writes.

That is the sentence to keep. Everything else in this lesson is what has to be true for that sentence to be buildable.

Two things have to change. The encoder must stop discarding: instead of overwriting its running summary and handing over only the last state, it emits one list of numbers per source word and keeps all of them. A ten-word sentence leaves ten of these behind; a fifty-word sentence leaves fifty. The store grows with the sentence, which is exactly what a fixed-size vector refused to do. And the decoder must acquire a way of choosing among them, one choice per output word.

3. Query, keys and values

The three names for the pieces come from later work and are worth adopting now, because every model in the rest of this course uses them. Think of a filing cabinet.

  • The query is what the decoder is looking for at this instant. There is one query per output step, derived from the decoder's own state: roughly, "I have written this much of the French sentence, and I now need whatever comes next".
  • The keys are what each stored item advertises about itself. There is one key per source word, derived from that word's encoder output.
  • The values are what each stored item actually hands over when you decide to look at it. Again one per source word. In Bahdanau's model the key and the value are the same list of numbers; later models compute them separately, which changes nothing about the idea.

One step of attention is then three operations, and this is the only formula this lesson needs, stated in words. Score the query against every key, producing one raw number per source position that says how well that position matches what is wanted now. Normalise those scores into weights that are all positive and add up to one. Blend the values in those proportions, and hand the result to the decoder as the context for this one word.

Then the decoder writes the word, updates its state, forms a new query, and the whole exchange happens again from the beginning. Nothing is fixed and nothing is thrown away.

4. Why the weights must add up to one

The normalising step looks like a technicality and is not. It is done with a function called softmax, which takes any list of numbers, makes them all positive, and rescales them so they total one. Three consequences follow, and all three matter.

First, the output is a weighted average rather than a sum. If the weights simply added up, a fifty-word sentence would hand the decoder a context vector with numbers roughly five times as large as a ten-word sentence's, and the decoder would have to cope with an input whose scale depends on how long the sentence happened to be. Forcing the weights to total one makes the context vector comparable across every sentence in the corpus.

Second, the weights become readable. A number between 0 and 1 that shares a total of 1 with its neighbours reads as a proportion: this output word drew 76 percent of its context from that source word. Section 6 depends entirely on that readability.

Third, and this is the one that makes the whole thing trainable, softmax is smooth. The obvious alternative is to pick the single best-matching source position and use only that. That version cannot be trained by the usual method, because "pick the best one" has no useful notion of a small nudge: change the scores slightly and either nothing happens or the choice jumps to a different word entirely. Softmax spreads a little weight everywhere, so a small change in a score produces a small change in the output, and the training procedure can work out which direction to nudge every score in. Bahdanau and colleagues say precisely this: the soft alignment allows the gradient of the cost function to be propagated through it.

The mechanism is trainable because it is soft, and it is soft because of the normalising step.

5. It is a soft lookup, not a choice

An ordinary dictionary lookup is hard: you present a key, you get back exactly one value, and every other entry contributes nothing. Attention is the soft version of the same operation. You present a query, it is compared with every key, and you get back a blend of all the values in proportion to how well each key matched.

The paper's own example of why that is better than a hard choice is small and completely convincing. In one of its sentences the English phrase "the man" is translated into the French "l'homme". Any hard alignment maps "the" to "l'" and "man" to "homme", one to one, and that is useless, because you cannot tell whether English "the" should become le, la, les or l' without looking at the word that comes after it. The soft version lets the model look at "the" and "man" at once while producing "l'", and it does, and it gets the elision right.

A second benefit falls out of the same property. A hard alignment has to say what every word maps to, which forces translation systems to invent a null token for words that map to nothing. A blend has no such problem: a source position that is not wanted simply receives a small weight.

6. The alignment plot, and what it is evidence of

Because the weights are proportions, you can draw them. Lay the source words along one axis and the generated words along the other, shade each cell by its weight, and look at the picture.

What appears is roughly a diagonal, because English and French mostly run in the same order. The interesting part is where the diagonal breaks. In one of the paper's examples the English phrase "European Economic Area" becomes the French "zone economique europeenne", which reverses the order of all three words. The model writing "zone" reaches forward past two English words to "Area"; writing the following words it reaches back, one word at a time, to pick up "Economic" and then "European". The weights cross over exactly where the two languages disagree, and you can see them do it.

That picture is a word alignment: a statement of which source words correspond to which target words. It is not a new object. Statistical translation systems had been estimating alignments explicitly since the early 1990s, as their own separate model, trained for that purpose. Here one falls out of a model that was never asked to produce it, as a by-product of being allowed to choose where to look. That is why the paper is called "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate".

What the plot is not

An attention plot is not an explanation of the model's decision. It shows which positions the blend drew from, and that is a genuine fact about the computation. It does not show why, it does not establish that those positions caused the output, and a great deal of later work has shown that very different weightings can produce the same prediction. Read it as a learned weighting that happens to align well on translation, which is exactly what its authors claimed for it.

7. What it was worth

The paper trained two models on the same data, one with attention and one without, at two sentence-length limits, and compared them. The gap is large enough that it needs no commentary.

BLEU on the WMT 2014 English-to-French test set, from Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio. The second column is all test sentences; the third is only those containing no words unfamiliar to the model. Moses is the phrase-based statistical system of the day.
ModelAll sentencesNo unfamiliar words
Encoder-decoder, trained to 30 words13.9324.19
With attention, trained to 30 words21.5031.44
Encoder-decoder, trained to 50 words17.8226.71
With attention, trained to 50 words26.7534.16
Moses, phrase-based33.3035.63

Two readings of that table are worth making explicit. The attention model trained only on sentences up to thirty words beats the plain encoder-decoder trained on sentences up to fifty: 21.50 against 17.82. Less training data, better result, because the architecture stopped wasting it. And on the sentences with no unfamiliar words, attention closes almost the entire gap to the statistical system that had held the field for two decades, at 34.16 against 35.63.

The length behaviour is the direct answer to Sequence to sequence, and the eight thousand numbers, which ends with two research groups disagreeing about whether the fixed vector really hurt on long sentences. Bahdanau's team plotted score against length for both of their models on the same data. The plain encoder-decoder drops away sharply as sentences get longer. The attention model trained to fifty words, in their words, shows no performance deterioration even with sentences of length fifty or more. Same data, same training, one architectural difference, and the sag disappears.

8. Nobody invented this alone, and one person simplified it

Before anything else in this section, read the affiliations off the front page of the paper, because they are the single most commonly mangled fact in this period. Dzmitry Bahdanau was at Jacobs University Bremen, in Germany. KyungHyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio were at the Université de Montréal. None of the three was at Google. The architecture that made attention famous three years later, the Transformer of The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked, is Google's work. The mechanism underneath it is not, and collapsing the two into one Google invention is the error that most retellings of this decade make.

Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio are the right citation for the attention that everything later in this course is built from, and it would still be wrong to tell this as one team having one idea. Late 2014 produced the same shape in several places within weeks. Alex Graves, Greg Wayne and Ivo Danihelka posted Neural Turing Machines on 20 October 2014, in which a network reads from an external memory by attending to it. Jason Weston, Sumit Chopra and Antoine Bordes posted Memory Networks five days earlier, on the same theme. Content-based lookup was in the air; the version that stuck was the one aimed at translation.

One later simplification matters more than it looks. In August 2015 Minh-Thang Luong, Hieu Pham and Christopher Manning compared three ways of computing the score, and one of them, which they call dot, is simply the dot product of the query and the key: multiply the two lists of numbers position by position and add the results up. No extra parameters, no small network, one operation that a graphics card is built to do billions of times a second.

That is the scoring function every model in the rest of this course uses. It survives because it is cheap, and cheapness turns out to matter enormously two years later, which is the subject of The other problem: attention did not make it fast.

Retrieval practice

What does attention compute at each step of writing the output?
Scores over all source positions become weights that total one, and the weighted blend is that step's context; nothing is discarded.
Why must the weights be normalised to add up to one?
Smoothness is the load-bearing one: a hard pick of the best position has no small nudge, so it cannot be trained by gradient descent.
Why is the alignment picture that attention produces surprising?
Statistical translation had estimated alignments explicitly for twenty years; here one appears as a by-product of an unrelated objective.
The attention model trained to thirty words beat the plain one trained to fifty. Why does that matter?
Less training data and a better score is the cleanest possible evidence that the fixed-size summary had been wasting what it was given.
