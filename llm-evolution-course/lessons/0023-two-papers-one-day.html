Two papers, one day

On 12 June 2017 two unrelated papers were posted, one that made models fast and one that made them listen to people, and the rest of this course is those two threads converging.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson is a hinge. It assumes you have felt the two problems that were open in mid-2017: models were slow to train, and there was no way to tell one what you wanted. It names the two answers and then hands off.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The other problem: attention did not make it fast Attention fixed how far a model could reach, but the recurrence underneath it still forced one step per word, so training time grew with sentence length no matter how many machines you had.

The one tight idea

On 12 June 2017 two unrelated papers were posted, one that made models fast and one that made them listen to people, and the rest of this course is those two threads converging.

2. What the record actually says

Two papers were submitted to arXiv on Monday 12 June 2017, thirty-four minutes apart.

At 17:23:59 UTC, "Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences", by Paul Christiano of OpenAI, Jan Leike of DeepMind, Tom Brown, Miljan Martic of DeepMind, Shane Legg of DeepMind, and Dario Amodei of OpenAI.

At 17:57:34 UTC, "Attention Is All You Need", by Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Lukasz Kaiser and Illia Polosukhin, all at Google or working there at the time. All eight carry an equal-contribution mark, and the footnote adds that the listing order is random. It then assigns each author a specific contribution, so the equal mark records equal weight rather than undivided work; The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked quotes the clauses.

Those timestamps are not decoration. They come from the arXiv submission record, which is the primary document for a preprint, and they are the reason this course dates the Transformer to June 2017 rather than to December 2017, when it was presented at a conference, or to some vaguer point in 2015 that the compressed retelling sometimes prefers.

The date matters for one specific reason. Who was in the room: labs, money, and machines, 2014 to 2017 establishes that OpenAI was announced on 11 December 2015. So the architecture that organisation is now famous for was published eighteen months after it was founded, and it was published by a different company.

3. Neither paper is about language models

This is the part worth slowing down for, because both papers are now read backwards through what they caused.

"Attention Is All You Need" is a machine translation paper. Read its abstract and it says so: it proposes a new architecture, it evaluates that architecture on two translation tasks from the 2014 Workshop on Machine Translation, and it adds one further experiment on English sentence parsing to show the architecture generalises past translation. Every headline number in it is a BLEU score, which is a measure of translation quality. It contains no language model. It contains no pretraining. It contains no argument about what happens if you make the model larger. The phrase "large language model" does not appear in it, and language modelling appears only where the paper is citing other people's work.

The most useful way to describe it is this: the Transformer was the last paper of the machine translation era, not the first paper of the language model era. It became the starting gun retroactively, a year later, when other people did something to it that its authors had not proposed.

"Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences" is a paper about Atari games and simulated robots. There is no language in it anywhere. Its problem is one that has nothing to do with text: if you want an agent to do something, you normally have to write down a scoring function for how well it did, and for most things worth wanting nobody knows how to write that function. Their own example is a robot asked to clean a table or scramble an egg. You can write an approximate score, and you will get an agent that maximises your approximate score without doing what you wanted.

Their move is to stop writing the scoring function and learn one instead. Show a person two short video clips of the agent and ask which is better. Fit a model to those choices. Have the agent optimise against that model. They report that this solves complex tasks without access to any scoring function at all, on less than one percent of the agent's interactions, and that they trained a simulated hopping robot to do backflips using 900 comparisons and under an hour of a person's time.

One finding inside that paper outlives everything else in it. They asked people to compare pairs rather than to give an absolute number, and they explain why: comparisons were easier for people to provide, while being equally useful for learning what those people preferred. People are unreliable at scoring and reliable at comparing. Every alignment technique in this course, five years later and at a thousand times the scale, still collects rankings rather than ratings, and that is where the practice comes from.

4. Two threads, named

It is useful to name these two lines and keep the names, because most of the rest of the course is one or the other.

The architecture thread asks: what shape of model, and how big? It runs from the Transformer through the decision to keep only half of it, through pretraining on unlabelled text, through making it larger, through discovering that the relationship between size and quality is measurable.

The preference thread asks: how do you tell it what you want? It runs from the video clips of a hopping robot through the same technique applied to written text, through summaries, through general instructions.

Both threads are necessary and neither is sufficient. The architecture thread alone produces a model that is extraordinary at continuing text and will not answer a question, because continuing text is genuinely what it was trained to do. The preference thread alone produces an excellent method for communicating goals to a system that has nothing much to say. The product everyone eventually used is the point where they meet.

5. Against tidy history

Now the caution, and it is the most important paragraph in this lesson.

The coincidence of the date meant nothing at the time. Nobody on either paper noticed the other one. Neither paper cites the other, and neither had any reason to: one is about translating English into German and the other is about teaching a simulated robot to hop. There is no shared insight, no shared method, and no conversation between the two teams about these two pieces of work.

Even the institutional overlap is thinner than it looks. Dario Amodei and Paul Christiano were both at OpenAI, and Jan Leike, Miljan Martic and Shane Legg were at DeepMind, so the preference paper is a collaboration between the two organisations that Who was in the room: labs, money, and machines, 2014 to 2017 describes as counterweights to each other. That is genuinely interesting. It is also a fact about the alignment community in 2017, not a connection to the eight people at Google publishing a translation architecture the same afternoon.

So why teach the coincidence at all?

Because it is an honest device for holding two threads in mind at once, and because the alternative framings are worse. Told as one line, the story of language models becomes a story about architectures getting bigger, and the moment when a model became something a person could talk to arrives as a mystery. Told as two threads from one date, that moment is where two known lines meet, and both lines are traceable to a document you can open.

Use the date as a bookmark. Do not use it as a cause. A history that makes the past look tidier than it was is teaching a habit of mind that will fail the reader on everything after it.

Retrieval practice

What was "Attention Is All You Need" actually a paper about?
Every headline number in it is a BLEU score, and it contains no language model, no pretraining and no scaling argument.
Why does the preference paper ask people to compare rather than to score?
Its authors found comparisons easier to provide and equally useful for learning a preference, and every later alignment method inherited that.
What is the correct thing to say about the shared date?
Neither paper cites the other, they share no method and no author, and nobody at the time noticed that they landed on the same afternoon.
Why does neither thread produce a usable assistant on its own?
A text continuer will finish your sentence instead of answering it, and a method for communicating goals needs a system with something to offer.
