Post-training after PPO: three things deleted

The alignment recipe got simpler three separate times, and each simplification is best remembered by which component it removed: the reward model, the human rater, or the critic.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Teaching a model to answer gives a three-stage alignment recipe with several moving parts. This lesson is what happened to those parts over the following three years. It assumes you know that recipe.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Teaching a model to answer A model trained to continue text is not trying to be helpful, so you add a stage whose only job is preference: show it demonstrations, learn a stand-in for human taste, and let it practise against that on a short leash.
  • Rewards a machine can check Replace the human rater with a program that can check the answer, and the ceiling on training data moves from how fast people can label to how fast you can generate problems.

The one tight idea

The alignment recipe got simpler three separate times, and each simplification is best remembered by which component it removed: the reward model, the human rater, or the critic.

2. The recipe that everything simplified away from

Teaching a model to answer describes the recipe that turned a text continuer into an assistant, and it has three stages. Show the model demonstrations of good answers and imitate them. Collect human comparisons between pairs of answers and train a separate model to predict which one a person would prefer. Then let the model practise, scored by that stand-in preference model, with a leash that penalises drifting too far from where it started.

It worked, and it was miserable to run.

At the moment of training, four models are resident. The policy, which is the model being improved. A frozen reference copy of it, which the leash is measured against. The reward model, which supplies the score. And a critic, usually about the same size as the policy, whose job is to estimate how good a typical answer to this particular question would be, so that a sample can be judged against that baseline rather than against an absolute number. Around all four sits an online loop in which the policy generates fresh samples throughout training, which makes the whole thing a distributed systems project rather than a script.

The three years after that recipe are best remembered as three deletions. Each one removed a component, each was published separately, and each is now standard.

3. Delete the reward model

Direct preference optimisation was published by Rafailov and colleagues at Stanford on 29 May 2023.

The observation behind it is a piece of algebra rather than an engineering trick. In the standard setup there is a fixed relationship between the reward function and the policy that would be optimal under it, and that relationship can be turned around: the reward can be rewritten in terms of the policy itself. Substitute that in, and the reinforcement learning problem collapses into an ordinary classification loss over the comparison pairs. Raise the probability the model assigns to the answer a person preferred, lower the probability of the one they rejected, both measured relative to the frozen starting model.

The paper's own summary of the payoff is that the method is stable, performant and computationally lightweight, that it eliminates the need to sample from the model during fine-tuning or to do significant hyperparameter tuning, and that it exceeds the reinforcement-learning version at controlling the sentiment of what the model writes.

Historically this is the most consequential of the three, and the reason is economic rather than scientific. Four models in memory became two, and a distributed reinforcement learning system became a training loop that one person can write and run on hardware they already have. That is a large part of why open-weight models caught up on instruction quality across 2023 and 2024: the recipe stopped requiring a research team and became something a small group could reproduce.

The cost is real and it is worth naming. This method is offline. It learns from a fixed pile of comparisons over answers that some earlier model produced, so it never sees what the model being trained would itself have said, and where the pile is thin it has nothing to correct with. A large family of variants followed, each adjusting what is being compared and what it is compared against: KTO in February 2024, ORPO in March 2024, which folds the preference penalty into the imitation stage and drops the frozen reference model too, and SimPO in May 2024. None of them decisively won, which is itself worth knowing. For the algebra and the loss function, the papers course covers this method in full.

4. Delete the human rater

The second deletion was published first, on 15 December 2022, two weeks after the launch of the chat product that made this whole field a consumer story, which A text box changes everything tells. Bai and colleagues at Anthropic called it Constitutional AI, and its target is the bottleneck that everyone could already see: human labelling does not scale.

It has two phases. In the first, the model produces an answer, is asked to criticise its own answer against a written list of principles, revises it accordingly, and is then fine-tuned on the revisions. In the second, two samples are produced and a model rather than a person decides which one better satisfies the principles, and a preference model is trained on that pile of machine-made comparisons. The authors named the result reinforcement learning from AI feedback.

The claim that matters is one sentence from their abstract: the only human oversight is provided through a list of rules or principles. The outcome they report is also worth quoting, because it is a behavioural goal rather than a score: a harmless but non-evasive assistant that engages with harmful queries by explaining its objections to them.

In September 2023 a group at Google put the comparison on the record, reporting that learning from AI feedback achieved comparable performance to learning from human feedback on summarisation and dialogue, and, more surprisingly, that it still worked when the model doing the labelling was the same size as the model being trained, or literally the same checkpoint. That last finding is the seed of every self-improvement loop that followed.

Nobody was actually deleted here, and this is the part the shorthand gets wrong. The humans moved. They stopped ranking individual answers and started writing the principles that the judging model applies, which is a smaller, slower, more deliberate kind of work. The cost is that whatever the judge's own training left in it is now applied at industrial scale with nobody reading the individual decisions.

One dated data point on where that has gone. On 22 January 2026 Anthropic published a new constitution for its models, released in full under a public-domain licence. KNOWN from the announcement itself: it is a detailed document that explains the reasons behind the values rather than only listing rules, and its authors say the explanation is what they expect to make it effective. Whether a long argued document actually trains better behaviour than a short list is not settled, and the document has published critics.

5. Delete the critic

The third deletion arrived on 5 February 2024, in a paper about mathematics. Shao and colleagues at DeepSeek introduced group relative policy optimisation while describing DeepSeekMath, and it removes the critic.

Recall what the critic was for. When a sample scores 0.7, that number means nothing on its own; what matters is whether 0.7 is better or worse than a typical answer to this question. The critic is a learned model that predicts that typical value, and it is expensive because it is usually about the size of the policy.

The replacement is almost embarrassingly direct. For each question, generate a group of answers rather than one, and use the group itself as the baseline. In prose, the one formula this lesson allows itself: an answer's advantage is its own reward minus the average reward of its group, divided by how spread out that group's rewards were. Better than its own siblings gets pushed up, worse gets pushed down. The learned estimate of "how good is a typical answer here" was replaced by an average over sixteen actual answers.

The stated motivation in the DeepSeek-R1 paper that later made this famous is simply to save the training costs of reinforcement learning, and it swept the field for two reasons. It removes a policy-sized model from the training loop. And it pairs perfectly with the shift described in Rewards a machine can check, where the score comes from a program checking the answer rather than from a learned opinion: running a marking script on all sixteen samples costs nothing, whereas running a reward model on them is a real bill.

Its costs are also specific. You now generate a group of answers per question instead of one, so sampling cost rises. You inherit whatever the reward can actually measure, which is a hard limit rather than a tuning problem. And it is not settled: in April 2025 the VAPO work argued that value-based methods can beat the group-based recipe on long chains of reasoning once three known failure modes are handled. For the objective itself, the papers course derives it.

6. What the three deletions have in common

Each deleted component was a learned stand-in for something that could be obtained more directly.

The reward model stood in for the preference data, and the algebra showed the data could be used directly. The human rater stood in for a set of values, and those values could be written down once and applied by a model. The critic stood in for a baseline, and the baseline could be measured by generating a few more samples. In all three cases the removed piece was a model trained to approximate something, and in all three cases the approximation was both expensive and a place where errors could hide.

That is a pattern worth carrying out of this lesson, because it recurs across the whole of this course: the version that ships is usually the one with fewer moving parts, and the sophisticated version is usually the one that was tried first. What did not work, and why that matters is the same shape in the reasoning story, where a search and a step-scoring model were both tried and both dropped.

7. Where the training data comes from now

Once a model can judge, critique and revise, it can also write training data, and by 2026 model-generated data is a central ingredient rather than a curiosity. Model cards describe pipelines that synthesise agent tasks and logical puzzles at scale, and small models are routinely trained on text produced by large ones.

The obvious worry has a name and a paper. In May 2023 Shumailov and colleagues published The Curse of Recursion, which reports that training on model-generated content causes irreversible defects, in which the tails of the original content distribution disappear: the rare things stop being represented, then stop being produced, then stop being learnable. They called it model collapse.

State that result precisely, because it is routinely repeated as though it were a property of models on sale today. It is a result under stated assumptions, and the central assumption is that each generation's real data is replaced by the previous generation's output. In April 2024 Gerstgrasser and colleagues examined the more realistic case in which data accumulates instead, with the generated text added to the real text rather than substituted for it, and reported that the collapse is avoided in their experiments.

So the honest position, and the one this course takes, is three sentences. Repeated replacement degrades a model, which is demonstrated. Accumulation appears not to, which is also demonstrated. Whether any shipped frontier model is anywhere near either regime is UNVERIFIABLE, because no laboratory discloses the composition of its training data, only its size.

The sharper concern is not collapse at all. It is that a loop which improves a model using signals the loop can compute will only improve what those signals can measure, which is the same limitation that Rewards a machine can check describes for checkable rewards, arriving by a different route.

Retrieval practice

What does direct preference optimisation remove from the recipe?
The reward can be rewritten in terms of the policy, so the comparison pairs can be trained on directly and the stand-in scorer is unnecessary.
In learning from AI feedback, what do people still supply?
The human judgement moves from ranking thousands of pairs to writing one document, which is the whole economic argument and the whole risk.
How does group-relative optimisation replace the critic?
A learned guess at the typical answer's value is replaced by measuring the value of several actual answers to that same question.
What is the correct way to state the model-collapse result?
Later work found that accumulating generated data alongside real data avoids the effect, so the assumption of replacement is doing the work.
