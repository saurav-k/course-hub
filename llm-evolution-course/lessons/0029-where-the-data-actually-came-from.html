Where the data actually came from

The dataset behind GPT-2 was scraped by its authors and filtered by a proxy for human approval, it was not a public crawl, and that filtering idea outlived the model that introduced it.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson exists to correct a specific and very common misconception about where early training data came from. It assumes only that you know a language model is trained on text.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • GPT-2 and the bet on scale Make the decoder much bigger and train it on much more text, and the fine-tuning step starts to become unnecessary: you describe the task in the prompt instead.

The one tight idea

The dataset behind GPT-2 was scraped by its authors and filtered by a proxy for human approval, it was not a public crawl, and that filtering idea outlived the model that introduced it.

2. The picture almost everybody has

Ask most people where a language model's training data comes from and you get a version of this: there is an enormous pile of internet text somewhere, and the labs download it and train on it.

By 2020 that picture is close to true. In 2019 it was not true at all, and the gap between those two years is the whole of this lesson.

The picture is usually assembled out of three real things, each of which exists and each of which is being remembered slightly out of place.

3. What WebText was

GPT-2, published by OpenAI on 14 February 2019 and described in GPT-2 and the bet on scale, was trained on a corpus its own authors built, called WebText. Nobody gave it to them and nobody else had a copy. Its recipe is stated in one paragraph of the paper and it is worth following exactly, because every step is a judgement call.

The problem the authors set themselves was quality at scale. They wanted a very large amount of text and they wanted it to be worth reading, and they say why the obvious approach fails: "Manually filtering a full web scrape would be exceptionally expensive".

So they borrowed a filter that other people had already applied, for free. Reddit is a link-sharing site: users post links to pages elsewhere, and other users vote them up or down, with the net vote count called karma. OpenAI scraped every outbound link that had received at least 3 karma. Their own description of the reasoning is careful and modest: this "can be thought of as a heuristic indicator for whether other users found the link interesting, educational, or just funny".

Three is a very low bar. It removes spam and dead links and not much else. The paper calls it a heuristic and does not claim more for it.

What followed was ordinary data engineering. The 45 million links were fetched, the readable text pulled out of the HTML with two open-source extractors, and the result deduplicated and cleaned. Links created after December 2017 were excluded. Wikipedia was removed, and for a reason that is not about quality: it "is a common data source for other datasets and could complicate analysis due to overlapping training data with test evaluation tasks". That is a decision about honest evaluation, not about content.

The finished corpus was "slightly over 8 million documents for a total of 40 GB of text".

Three corrections about this corpus are worth carrying, because all three are commonly got wrong.

The first is stated in OpenAI's own model card: "WebText is made of data derived from outbound links from Reddit and does not consist of data taken directly from Reddit itself." GPT-2 was not trained on Reddit comments. Reddit was the recommendation engine; the training text was the articles that were linked to. You can see this directly, because OpenAI published the thousand most common domains in the corpus. The top of the list is Google, Archive, Blogspot, GitHub and the New York Times.

The second is a filter that appears nowhere in the paper. The model card, published nine months later in November 2019, records that "Before generating the dataset we used a blocklist to ensure we didn't sample from a variety of subreddits which contain sexually explicit or otherwise offensive content." That is a substantial content decision, documented only after the fact, and it is a good early example of documentation catching up with practice.

The third correction is about the corpus you can actually get hold of, and it matters to anyone who goes looking. WebText was never released. OpenAI described how it was built and published the domain list, but never distributed the text, so there is no copy of GPT-2's training data to download. What exists instead is OpenWebText, built in 2019 by Aaron Gokaslan and Vanya Cohen at Brown University, who describe it in their own words as "an open source effort to reproduce OpenAI's WebText dataset" and who acknowledge working from a limited public description. They followed the same recipe from Reddit submissions and finished with 8,013,769 documents and 38 GB of text, which is strikingly close to OpenAI's own "slightly over 8 million documents" and 40 GB. Close is not the same, and the difference is the point: a reader who downloads a dataset called WebText is reading a careful reconstruction of what GPT-2 read, not what GPT-2 read. The distinction is worth stating once here, because it is routinely lost in writing about this model.

4. What it was not, and why

There is an organisation that scrapes the whole web and keeps every page. It is called Common Crawl, it is a nonprofit founded in 2007 by Gil Elbaz, it has been crawling since 2008, its archive passed ten petabytes, it publishes a new crawl roughly every month, and it gives all of it away free. It is exactly the thing the popular picture imagines.

GPT-2 did not use it, and the paper says why in plain language. The authors tried it. They cite earlier work that noted a large number of Common Crawl documents "whose content are mostly unintelligible", and they add: "We observed similar data issues in our initial experiments with Common Crawl."

So the sequence is the opposite of the popular one. The public crawl existed, was free, was orders of magnitude larger, and was rejected on quality grounds, after which the authors went and built something smaller by hand.

Hugging Face belongs even further from this story in 2019. It is a software company, and its lasting contribution began as a port of Google's BERT into PyTorch, in a repository created on 29 October 2018. Its datasets library did not exist until March 2020. Line the dates up and the arithmetic is decisive: WebText's cutoff is the end of December 2017, ten months before that repository existed, and more than two years before there was a datasets library at all. Hugging Face is the front door through which almost every learner meets GPT-2 today, which is exactly why it gets mistaken for the house.

The one genuine 2019 appearance is a peer, not a supplier: OpenAI's own release report notes that "NLP company Hugging Face decided not to release some of its internal language models".

5. The filter outlives the corpus

Here is the part that makes this more than a correction.

Fifteen months later, in Language Models are Few-Shot Learners of 28 May 2020, the same lab came back to Common Crawl and used it after all - as 60 per cent of the training mix for a model a hundred times larger. The obvious question is what changed, and the answer is not that Common Crawl got cleaner.

What changed is that they now had a definition of clean, and it was WebText. From the paper's appendix: "Using the original WebText as a proxy for high-quality documents, we trained a classifier to distinguish these from raw Common Crawl. We then used this classifier to re-sample Common Crawl by prioritizing documents which were predicted by the classifier to be higher quality."

Follow that chain end to end and it is genuinely startling. A person upvoted a link in 2017. OpenAI treated three such upvotes as evidence a human found the page worth reading. That produced WebText. WebText became the positive examples for a classifier. The classifier scored 45 terabytes of Common Crawl. The surviving 570 gigabytes became the largest component of GPT-3's training data.

One detail rescues this from being purely mechanical, and it is a nice piece of engineering judgement. The classifier was not used as a hard cut-off. Documents were kept randomly with a probability tilted towards high scores, and the paper says why: they wanted "mostly documents the classifier scored highly, but still include some documents that were out of distribution". Filtering too hard would have taught the next model that the world looks exactly like WebText.

6. The general lesson

Data curation is a modelling decision. It decides what the model has read, which decides what it can say, which decides everything the reader of this course will later meet as a capability or a failure. Tokens: how text becomes numbers shows the same corpus reaching forward in a different way: the merges in a tokenizer are counted on it, so the corpus decides what every language costs to write for the rest of the model's life.

And it is reliably the least documented decision in the pipeline. GPT-2's architecture is given in a table anyone can check. Its blocklist appears only in a document published nine months afterwards. The trend continues: for the frontier models of the 2020s, training data is generally not disclosed at all, which The disclosure collapse takes up.

The practical habit to take away is a question to ask of any model, and to notice when it cannot be answered: who decided what this read, and what was their filter. For GPT-2 the answer is unusually clear, and unusually humble. It was three upvotes.

Keep the units the source used

WebText is 40 GB and just over 8 million documents, because those are the units the paper gives. It never states a token count, so any token figure for WebText is somebody's estimate from an assumed bytes-per-token rate, not a disclosure. GPT-3's corpus is quoted here in tokens because that is the unit its paper uses. Do not convert between documents, gigabytes and tokens unless a source hands you both.

8. Sources to read first

  • Radford, Wu, Child, Luan, Amodei and Sutskever, Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners, OpenAI, 14 February 2019. Section 2.1 is the whole of the WebText recipe, including the Common Crawl rejection, the karma threshold, the 45 million links, the 8 million documents and 40 GB, and the Wikipedia removal.
  • The GPT-2 model card, openai/gpt-2, last updated November 2019. The source for the outbound-links clarification and for the subreddit blocklist, neither of which is in the paper.
  • The top 1,000 domains in WebText, published by OpenAI. The composition of the corpus, in link counts.
  • Aaron Gokaslan and Vanya Cohen, OpenWebText Corpus, 2019, read 22 August 2026. The reproduction described in section 3, in its authors' own words, with its method and its final 8,013,769 documents and 38 GB.
  • Brown et al., Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, arXiv v1 28 May 2020. Table 2.2 for the training mix, section 2.2 for the 45 TB and 570 GB figures, and Appendix A for the WebText-as-proxy classifier and the deliberately soft filter.
  • The Common Crawl Foundation, about page, for its own account of what it is, when it started, and how the archive is published.
  • Solaiman et al., Release Strategies and the Social Impacts of Language Models, arXiv v2 13 November 2019. The single 2019 mention of Hugging Face as a peer lab.
  • The huggingface/transformers repository, whose history records its creation on 29 October 2018 as a PyTorch port of BERT.

Every source here was opened and read before this lesson was written, and every quotation is checked against the document rather than against a summary of it. The GPT-2 paper has no arXiv version, so it is read from the copy OpenAI hosts.

Retrieval practice

What was the quality filter behind GPT-2's training corpus?
Manually filtering a web scrape was ruled out as too expensive, so the authors reused a judgement strangers had already made for free.
Why did GPT-2's authors not train on Common Crawl?
The archive was free, public and vastly larger; the paper says the authors experimented with it and rejected it on quality.
How did the same lab make Common Crawl usable a year later?
The old corpus became the definition of good text, so its filter carried forward into a dataset a thousand times larger.
