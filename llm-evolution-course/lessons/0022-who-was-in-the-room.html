Who was in the room: labs, money, and machines, 2014 to 2017

The institutions that would build large language models were founded, funded and already compute-constrained before the architecture that made them possible existed.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson is not about a mechanism. It exists so that the corporate decisions of the next decade land without needing to be explained twice. It can be read at any point in the course.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

This lesson has no prerequisites in this course. It assumes only curiosity, and it states its own starting point above.

The one tight idea

The institutions that would build large language models were founded, funded and already compute-constrained before the architecture that made them possible existed.

2. Why the order of these events matters

The compressed version of this story puts the architecture first. A paper appears, everybody sees what it implies, labs are founded to pursue it, money arrives. That order is wrong, and getting it wrong makes the following decade unreadable.

Here is the actual order. The buying started in January 2014. The lab that would eventually ship the product was announced in December 2015. The public decided that machine learning was real in March 2016. Everyone concluded that computing power was the binding constraint during 2016. The architecture arrived in June 2017, from a company that had been doing this for years and was not one of the new labs.

Read in that order, the 2015 to 2017 period stops being a mystery. These people were not waiting for an invention. They had written down what they wanted to build, they knew they did not know how to build it, and they had already worked out that whatever the answer turned out to be, it would be expensive.

3. January 2014: a research group becomes an asset

DeepMind was a London research company founded in 2010 by Demis Hassabis, Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman, working on reinforcement learning and games. Google acquired it in January 2014.

The price was never officially disclosed. Reported figures range from roughly 400 million to roughly 650 million US dollars, and Google has never confirmed any of them. This course says "January 2014, price never officially disclosed" and stops there, which is the honest position and also the more interesting one, because the significance is not the number.

The significance is that a research group with no product became something a large company would buy outright. Until then, deep learning talent moved by hiring. After January 2014, a whole lab was a thing that could be acquired, which changed what founding one meant.

4. December 2015: a counterweight, announced

On 11 December 2015 a post titled "Introducing OpenAI" appeared, bylined Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever and the OpenAI team. It opens by saying what the organisation is: a non-profit artificial intelligence research company whose goal is to advance digital intelligence in the way most likely to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to generate financial return.

Three parts of that announcement are worth carrying forward, because this course returns to all of them.

The reason for the non-profit structure is stated directly: because it is hard to predict when human-level artificial intelligence might come within reach, it will be important to have a leading research institution which can prioritise a good outcome for all over its own self-interest.

The commitment to publishing is equally direct: researchers will be strongly encouraged to publish their work, whether as papers, blog posts or code, and any patents will be shared with the world. Hold on to that sentence. The disclosure collapse is about what happened to it.

The people, since several of them recur. Sam Altman and Elon Musk as co-chairs; Ilya Sutskever as research director; Greg Brockman as chief technology officer; founding members Trevor Blackwell, Vicki Cheung, Andrej Karpathy, Durk Kingma, John Schulman, Pamela Vagata and Wojciech Zaremba; advisors Pieter Abbeel, Yoshua Bengio, Alan Kay, Sergey Levine and Vishal Sikka.

5. A pledge is not a payment

The most repeated number in this era is the billion dollars, and it is repeated wrongly. Here is the sentence it comes from, verbatim, from the same announcement:

Sam, Greg, Elon, Reid Hoffman, Jessica Livingston, Peter Thiel, Amazon Web Services (AWS), Infosys, and YC Research are donating to support OpenAI. In total, these funders have committed $1 billion, although we expect to only spend a tiny fraction of this in the next few years.

Read the last clause. The announcement itself says this is a commitment rather than a bank balance, and says so in the same sentence as the number.

Where the number came from is also on the record now, because OpenAI published the email in March 2024. Musk wrote to Brockman, copying Altman, on 22 November 2015: they needed a much bigger number than 100 million dollars to avoid sounding hopeless relative to what Google or Facebook were spending, so they should say they were starting with a one billion dollar funding commitment, and he would cover whatever anyone else did not provide.

The number was chosen as a signal of seriousness to competitors, by the person who would make up the shortfall. That is not a scandal; it is how a founding announcement works. It is only misleading when it is later quoted as an amount of money that existed.

What actually arrived is contested, and this is the point in the lesson where the method in section 8 gets used for the first time. In the same March 2024 post, OpenAI states that the non-profit raised less than 45 million dollars from Musk and more than 90 million from other donors, which is roughly 135 million against a one billion headline. Musk's own court filings put his contribution higher. OpenAI published its figures while in litigation with Musk, so they are one party's account, published by an interested party, at a moment when it was useful. Both of those things are true at once and neither is a reason to hide the numbers.

The gap between the pledge and the payment is not a gotcha. It is the background to everything that happens to this organisation's structure in 2019, when a non-profit that had concluded it needed billions of dollars a year had to find them somewhere.

6. March 2016: the moment the public noticed

On 28 January 2016 DeepMind published AlphaGo in Nature, describing a program that combined deep neural networks with tree search and had beaten the European Go champion five games to nil the previous October. In March 2016 AlphaGo played Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players in the world, over five games in Seoul, and won four to one. DeepMind puts the audience at over 200 million people worldwide.

The moment people remember is the thirty-seventh move of the second game, which the human commentators watching initially read as a mistake. DeepMind's own account says the program estimated the chance of a human playing it at one in ten thousand.

Two things about AlphaGo are worth being exact about, because this course is about causation and it would be easy to draw a line that is not there.

Its technical connection to language modelling is close to zero. Nothing in AlphaGo is an ancestor of the architecture in The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked. Reinforcement learning becomes central to language models much later and by a different route.

Its effect on everything else was enormous, and it was an effect on belief rather than on capability. Before March 2016, "artificial intelligence is progressing quickly" was a research opinion. After it, it was a boardroom fact, and budgets moved accordingly.

One detail ties the threads together. Ilya Sutskever is a co-author of the AlphaGo paper, affiliated to Google. Nature received the manuscript on 11 November 2015, one month before he was announced as OpenAI's research director.

7. 2016: compute becomes the constraint everybody can see

Three events in 2016 say the same thing in three different registers.

On 19 May 2016 Google announced the Tensor Processing Unit, a chip built for nothing but machine learning, claiming an order of magnitude better performance per watt for the job. The striking part of the announcement is not the claim but the timing: Google said the chips had already been running in its data centres for more than a year. The largest player had been quietly building its own silicon before anyone outside knew there was a race.

On 5 April 2016 NVIDIA launched the DGX-1, a rack-mounted box holding eight of its new graphics cards and drawing 3,200 watts, reported at the time as listing for 129,000 US dollars. In the week of 9 August 2016, NVIDIA's chief executive Jen-Hsun Huang personally hand-delivered the first production unit to OpenAI's office in San Francisco and signed the chassis. NVIDIA published its own account on 15 August 2016.

The correct mental image is not a man carrying a graphics card out of an office. It is a chief executive wheeling a three-rack-unit, 3,200-watt machine into a non-profit's office and autographing it.

Was it a gift? The record is genuinely ambiguous, which is worth teaching rather than smoothing over. Musk's own message on 9 August 2016 thanked NVIDIA for donating the first DGX-1 to OpenAI. NVIDIA's post about the delivery, published six days later, never uses the words donate or gift, and describes it as a delivery. The reporting at the time noted the list price and said explicitly that it was not clear whether OpenAI had paid. So: Musk publicly called it a donation, and NVIDIA never said either way.

What the people receiving it said is the part that matters most for this course. NVIDIA's post quotes Sutskever saying that their advances depend on graphics cards being fast, and that the speed of their computers is, in some sense, the lifeblood of deep learning. It quotes Karpathy saying that one very easy way of always getting their models to work better is to just scale the amount of compute.

That is August 2016, ten months before the Transformer paper exists. The scaling argument that becomes the organising idea of the entire next decade is already being said out loud, by the people who will act on it, with no architecture to apply it to yet. Predicting the future with a straight line is where that intuition finally gets measured.

8. June 2016: the destination, written down in advance

On 20 June 2016 OpenAI published a post called "OpenAI technical goals", bylined Sutskever, Brockman, Altman and Musk. It names four: build a metric out of simulated environments, build a household robot, build an agent with useful natural language understanding, and solve many games with one agent.

Goal three, quoted from the post, is the one to read twice. They planned to build an agent that could perform a complex task specified by language, and ask for clarification about the task if it was ambiguous. They then said, of the more advanced linguistic goals, that there were promising algorithms for supervised tasks such as question answering, parsing and translation, but that there were not any for the ability to carry a conversation, the ability to fully understand a document, or the ability to follow complex instructions in natural language.

Set that beside the chat assistants of 2023 and it is uncanny. Conversation, document understanding, instruction-following, clarifying questions: the destination was correct in June 2016, described in the right words, six and a half years early. The same document says plainly that no algorithms existed for any of it.

The destination was set in mid-2016. The vehicle arrived in mid-2017, and it was built somewhere else, for a different purpose. That is the sentence this whole lesson exists to earn.

9. How to talk about contested corporate history

Four of the claims in this lesson are contested, and this course handles all four the same way: state the date, state each side in one sentence, name who is saying it, and stop. Adjudicating between parties who are in litigation is not a history course's job, and pretending there is no dispute is worse than saying there is one.

The four contested claims in this lesson, and what is not contested about each.
ClaimNot contestedContested
The DeepMind priceThe acquisition, January 2014The amount. Never officially disclosed; reports vary widely
OpenAI's fundingThe $1 billion commitment and its caveat, both in the 2015 announcementHow much arrived. OpenAI says roughly $135M in total; Musk's filings differ
The DGX-1Hand-delivered by NVIDIA's chief executive, week of 9 August 2016Whether it was a gift. Musk said donated; NVIDIA never used the word
Why Musk left the boardFebruary 2018, and the board composition afterwardsThe reason. Conflict of interest at the time; incompatible later accounts from both parties

One further habit is worth naming, because it applies well beyond this lesson. A source published during a dispute, by a party to that dispute, is still evidence. It is evidence of what that party asserts, on a date, in public, where it can be checked against what they said before. Treat it as that and it is useful. Treat it as a neutral record and it is not.

What is wrong with saying OpenAI was founded with a billion dollars?
The same sentence that names the billion also says they expected to spend a tiny fraction of it in the next few years.

Its technical connection to language modelling is close to zero; its effect on budgets and expectations was enormous.
