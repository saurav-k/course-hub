Deep dive · Before the neural turn · Lesson 0013

Translation without a linguist

Model the source language and the translation separately, learn both by counting a parallel corpus, and search for the target sentence that scores best, with no grammar written by anyone.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Counting gives a way to score how likely a sentence is, which is what The statistical turn: guessing the next word establishes. This lesson is about the first hard task that idea won: translation.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The statistical turn: guessing the next word Language is predictable, predictability can be measured, and a machine that counts what usually follows what beats a machine that reasons about grammar.

The one tight idea

Model the source language and the translation separately, learn both by counting a parallel corpus, and search for the target sentence that scores best, with no grammar written by anyone.

2. A sentence in a bag

In June 1990 seven researchers at the IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center published a paper called "A Statistical Approach to Machine Translation". The authors were Peter Brown, John Cocke, Stephen and Vincent Della Pietra, Fred Jelinek, John Lafferty, Robert Mercer and Paul Roossin, and they had spent the previous fifteen years making counting work for speech recognition. Their opening argument is that Warren Weaver had proposed attacking translation statistically in 1949 and nobody had the machines for it, and that computers were now five orders of magnitude faster than in 1950 and large machine-readable corpora were readily available. They felt it was time to give statistics a chance.

Before any translation happens, the paper runs an experiment that is the best single demonstration of what a language model is, and it deserves to be much better known. They call it bag translation from English into English. Take an English sentence, cut it up into individual words, put the words in a bag, and try to recover the original sentence from the bag alone. The only tool is a three-word language model built for a speech recogniser, which scores any arrangement of those words by how likely that word order is. Try the arrangements, keep the highest-scoring one.

They tried it on the 38 sentences of fewer than eleven words in a random sample of 100. Twenty-four of the 38, sixty-three per cent, came back exactly right. Another eight came back with the meaning intact but the words rearranged, taking the total that preserved the meaning to 32 of 38, or eighty-four per cent. The paper prints one from each category. "Please give me your response as soon as possible" came back word for word. "Now let me mention some of the disadvantages" came back as "Let me mention some of the disadvantages now". "In our organization research has two missions" came back as "In our missions research organization has two", which the authors label garbage. They add, honestly, that longer sentences would have gone much worse.

Hold on to what just happened. Nothing in that experiment knows any grammar. A table of three-word counts, taken from unrelated speech-recognition work, put an English sentence back in order two times out of three. If a pile of counts can do that much of the job of producing English, then the remaining job is only to work out which words should be in the bag.

3. The noisy channel: two questions instead of one

That is exactly how the paper splits the problem. You are shown a French sentence and you want the English sentence it came from. Rather than model that directly, pretend the English sentence was sent through a noisy channel that garbled it into French, and ask which English sentence best explains what came out.

Scoring a candidate English sentence then means asking two separate questions. Does this sound like English? That is answered by a language model trained on English text alone, and English text alone is abundant. Would that English have produced this French? That is answered by a translation model, trained on documents that exist in both languages. Multiply the two scores together and you have the score of the candidate.

The obvious objection is why not skip the detour and model the English directly given the French, and the 1993 follow-up paper answers it with an image worth keeping. Translating, the authors write, is like springing from a well-formed French string into the sea of well-formed English strings and hoping to land on a good one. The two factors do different jobs. The translation model gives a high score to any English string, well-formed or not, that has the right words in roughly the right places to account for the French. The language model gives a high score to any well-formed English string regardless of its connection to the French. Only together do they concentrate probability on English that is both fluent and faithful. Their translation models, the authors say cheerfully, are prodigal, spraying probability all over the place, and two of them waste much of it on things that are not strings at all. It does not matter, because that model never has to produce the answer on its own.

4. Why a parliament turned out to be the right corpus

The translation model needs documents that exist in two languages and say the same thing, which is called a parallel corpus. In 1990 there was essentially one good source of them, and the reason is a law rather than a technology.

By law, the proceedings of the Canadian parliament are kept in both French and English. Members speak in whichever language they choose, the remarks are recorded in that language, and after the sitting a team of translators produces the complete proceedings in both. These are called the Hansards, after the publisher of the British parliamentary record in the early 1800s. The IBM group obtained about a hundred million words of English and the corresponding French from the Canadian government.

The proceedings are not translated sentence by sentence, so the group had to find the sentence pairs themselves, which they did with an algorithm based on nothing but sentence length. That produced about three million pairs, of which roughly ninety-nine per cent really were translations of one another. Notice how much of the work is already being done by an accident of Canadian law and an algorithm that counts characters. This is what the statistical turn looks like in practice: the expensive, judgement-laden step is replaced by a cheap mechanical one that is wrong one per cent of the time and does not care.

5. Alignment, the thing nobody wrote down

A pair of sentences is not yet a translation model. To learn that chien has something to do with dog, you need to know which word in one sentence produced which word in the other, and that is called an alignment.

Nobody labelled the alignments, and nobody was going to. Alignment is a hidden variable: something the model needs, that the data does not contain, and that has to be inferred from the data at the same time as everything else. The paper is explicit that this is the same situation as speech recognition, where you have the script of a sentence and the sound wave, but no indication of which part of the wave is which part of the script.

The way out is a loop, and it is a loop worth understanding because the same shape recurs throughout machine learning. Start with a guess so ignorant it contains no information: assume every French word is equally likely as the translation of every English word. With that guess you can compute how probable each possible alignment of each sentence pair is. With those alignment probabilities you can recount the word pairs, weighting each alignment by how probable it currently looks, and get a better guess. Repeat. Each pass makes the observed sentence pairs more probable than the last, and the estimates sharpen.

In the paper's first experiment they took the nine thousand commonest words of each language, and estimated some eighty-one million parameters from forty thousand sentence pairs of about eight hundred thousand words per language, starting from that deliberately uninformative guess. The paper prints what the loop learned about the single English word the: it translates most often into le and la. That is not surprising, the authors note, but they emphasise that it was determined completely automatically. Nobody told the machine that French has articles or that they have genders.

The 1993 companion paper, "The Mathematics of Statistical Machine Translation: Parameter Estimation", works this out in full as a series of five models of increasing sophistication, each used to initialise the next. Model 1 ignores word order entirely. Later models add where a word tends to move to, and how many French words one English word tends to produce, which they call its fertility: nobody produces both n' and personne, and some words produce nothing at all.

6. The answer is whatever scores highest, so the decoder is a search

The 1993 paper names the three problems this approach creates, and the naming is worth memorising because the third one is usually forgotten: the language modelling problem, the translation modelling problem, and the search problem.

Once translation is defined as the highest-scoring candidate, producing a translation means searching for it, and the space is impossibly large: every sentence in the target language is in principle a candidate. So the decoder builds the answer incrementally, keeping a list of partial hypotheses, extending the most promising ones, and discarding the rest. It is the same shape as any other heuristic search, and like any other heuristic search it can fail in two distinguishable ways. Either the model scored the wrong sentence highest, which is the model's fault, or the model would have scored the right sentence highest but the search never found it, which is the decoder's fault. The 1990 paper separates those two failure modes explicitly when reporting results.

That distinction is a permanent inheritance. Every system in the rest of this course has a scoring model and a procedure that turns scores into output, and every one of them can be wrong in either of those two ways.

7. What it produced, and what it established

The 1990 results are modest and reported without flattery. The group decoded seventy-three new French sentences from elsewhere in the Hansards and sorted the outputs into five categories. Only five per cent came out exactly as the human translation had. Counting the three categories the authors considered reasonable translations, the system succeeded forty-eight per cent of the time.

How Brown et al. graded their own 1990 decoder, on 73 French sentences from the Canadian Hansards
CategoryWhat it means
ExactIdentical to the published human translation
AlternateSame meaning, slightly different words
DifferentA legitimate translation, but not the same meaning as the human one
WrongSensible English, but not a translation of that French
UngrammaticalNot properly formed English at all

It would be a serious mistake to read those numbers as a failed experiment that neural translation later cleaned up. The statistical approach begun in that paper was the state of the art in machine translation for roughly the next twenty years, it went into production at scale, and by the mid-2000s a phrase-based descendant of it was what people meant when they said online translation. It was still what Google Translate ran on until 2016.

The deeper inheritance is not the systems but the habits. Three of them come from this era and all three outlived it. First, that a translation system is trained, so the interesting question about a system is what data it was trained on. Second, that quality is a measured quantity on held-out data rather than a matter of opinion, which led directly to automatic evaluation metrics: BLEU, published by another IBM group in 2002, is a compare-against-human-translations score, and it is still the number quoted in the papers that eventually replaced statistical translation. Third, that a system has a scoring model and a search, and that they fail separately.

And the headline claim of 1990 stands exactly as written. Two languages were translated between, and no linguist wrote a single rule in either of them. The rules were not written down. They were estimated.

8. What it owes the next step

Statistical translation left one thing conspicuously unresolved, and it is the thing the next architecture attacked.

The system is a committee. There is a language model, a translation model with its alignments and fertilities, and a search procedure, and each is trained separately on different data with different objectives before being bolted to the others. Every joint in that assembly is a place where a decision made for one component's benefit hurts another's. The obvious question, once neural networks could handle sequences at all, was whether one model could read the whole source sentence and write the whole target sentence, trained end to end on the same parallel corpora, with no separate alignment step and no committee. Sequence to sequence, and the eight thousand numbers is that attempt, and the way it succeeded created the constraint that most of the rest of this course is spent escaping.

Retrieval practice

What did the bag-translation experiment show about a language model on its own?
Word counts alone rebuilt 24 of 38 short sentences exactly, so much of the work of producing English needs no grammar at all.
Why score a candidate with two models rather than model the translation directly?
The translation model may spray probability anywhere, because the language model is what insists the output be well-formed English.
What is a word alignment, in this system?
The corpus contains sentence pairs but no word links, so the links are inferred by a loop that starts from a guess containing no knowledge.
Why is the decoder in a statistical translation system a search procedure?
Defining translation as the best-scoring candidate makes producing one a search, which is why a system can fail either by scoring wrongly or by never finding the right candidate.
