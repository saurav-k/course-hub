The leak that started an ecosystem

A research-only model release leaked within a week, and the tooling that appeared in the following month showed there had been enormous unmet demand for weights anyone could run.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

The disclosure collapse leaves the frontier labs publishing less. This lesson is about the counter-movement, and about what happens when a capable model becomes available to people who were never going to be granted access.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The disclosure collapse Once models became commercially valuable the technical reports stopped containing technique, so from 2023 onward a reader has to sort every claim into what is known, what is inferred, what is marketing and what is unverifiable.

The one tight idea

A research-only model release leaked within a week, and the tooling that appeared in the following month showed there had been enormous unmet demand for weights anyone could run.

2. What was released, and on what terms

In the last week of February 2023 Meta announced LLaMA, a family of four models at 7, 13, 33 and 65 billion parameters. The paper was submitted to arXiv on 27 February.

Its argument is in the abstract and it is an argument about data, not about size: it is possible "to train state-of-the-art models using publicly available datasets exclusively, without resorting to proprietary and inaccessible datasets", and the evidence offered is that the 13-billion-parameter model beats GPT-3 at 175 billion on most benchmarks while the 65-billion one is competitive with the best models of the time.

That claim obliges the paper to say what the data was, and it does, by source and percentage: filtered Common Crawl at 67 percent, the C4 corpus at 15 percent, then code from GitHub, Wikipedia, books, papers from arXiv and questions from Stack Exchange. Anyone could in principle assemble something similar. The claim was checkable, and it was meant to be.

The weights were a different matter. They were not published. They were made available on request to approved researchers, under a licence that permitted research use only. The contemporary description, from the reporting a week later, is that access was restricted to "approved researchers, government officials, or members of civil society".

So the release was two halves pointing in opposite directions: a paper arguing that the ingredients of a frontier model were public, and a distribution mechanism ensuring the model itself was not.

3. A week

The public record of what happened next is unusually clear for something usually described as murky, because parts of it are timestamped in places that keep timestamps.

On 2 March 2023, six days after the announcement, somebody opened a pull request against Meta's own LLaMA repository on GitHub titled "Save bandwidth by using a torrent to distribute more efficiently", containing a torrent link to the weights. It was not merged. It is still there, with its date on it.

On 7 March 2023, Joseph Cox reported at Vice that a torrent file had been posted to 4chan the previous week and that the model had "leaked online for anyone to download". Meta did not deny it. Its statement to the outlet restated the research-sharing rationale and added the sentence that concedes the event: "While the model is not accessible to all, and some have tried to circumvent the approval process, we believe the current release strategy allows us to balance responsibility and openness."

What this lesson will not tell you

Who uploaded the torrent, why, or whether the first copy came from an approved researcher. None of that is on the public record, then or now. The identity of the leaker is unverifiable in the sense The disclosure collapse defines: widely speculated about, never confirmed, and no method exists from outside that would settle it. What is known is the sequence of dated artefacts below, because each of them is a file with a timestamp you can go and look at.

What makes this interesting is not the leak. It is what the following fortnight demonstrated about how much unbuilt work had been waiting.

4. What appeared, and how fast

Three artefacts, all created within eleven days of the torrent, between them define the open-weights ecosystem that still exists.

Running it at all: llama.cpp, 10 March 2023

Georgi Gerganov's first commit to llama.cpp is dated 10 March 2023 and its message is "Initial release". It is a plain C and C++ implementation of LLaMA inference that runs on an ordinary processor, storing the weights at reduced precision so that a model that wants tens of gigabytes at full precision fits in the memory a laptop actually has. The technique is quantization, and LoRA and QLoRA: fine-tuning for the rest of us takes it up properly.

The consequence was immediate and social rather than technical. Until that week, "running a large language model" meant renting accelerators. Afterwards it meant leaving a laptop on for a while. The population of people who could experiment went from those with a compute budget to those with a computer.

Making it answer: Stanford Alpaca, 13 March 2023

A base model trained on next-token prediction does not behave like an assistant, and the fix - fine-tuning on demonstrations of good answers - had been documented in the InstructGPT work of March 2022, which Teaching a model to answer describes. The obstacle was the demonstrations. Writing tens of thousands of them costs a contracted team months.

Stanford's Alpaca skipped that by having a model write them. Its repository describes exactly what it is: a 7-billion-parameter LLaMA fine-tuned on "52K instruction-following data generated by the techniques in the Self-Instruct paper", where the generating model was a commercial one reached through an API. A preliminary human evaluation found the result behaved similarly to the commercial model it had learned from.

That is a large claim about cost. The expensive human step in the recipe had been replaced by an API bill.

Specialising it cheaply: alpaca-lora, 13 March 2023

Alpaca still fine-tuned all of the model's weights, which needs research hardware. The alpaca-lora repository, created the same day Alpaca was released, redid the same tuning by freezing the model and training a small correction beside it, which brought the job onto a single consumer graphics card.

The method was two years old - it was published in June 2021 for adapting very large commercial models - and had been sitting unused by anybody outside a laboratory, for the obvious reason that there had been no large model outside a laboratory to adapt.

5. The demand was latent, not created

Look again at what those eleven days did and did not contain.

No new architecture. No new training objective. No new optimisation method. The efficient inference implementation is an engineering exercise; the instruction-tuning recipe was published a year earlier; the adapter method was published nearly two years earlier. Every component was known, documented and unused.

The missing input was a capable set of weights that people outside a handful of organisations were allowed to touch, and when that appeared the rest assembled itself at the speed of people typing.

The best evidence that this was read as a demonstration rather than a disaster is what Meta did next. In July 2023 it released Llama 2 with the weights published outright and a licence permitting commercial use, four months after the accident. Whether the two facts are connected is not something the public record settles, and this lesson does not assert it. It only notes the sequence.

6. The ambiguity, stated and not resolved

This episode is legally and ethically unsettled, and the honest thing is to lay out the positions rather than pick one.

  • A licence was breached. The weights were distributed under terms permitting research use by approved recipients, and they were redistributed to everybody. That is not in dispute.
  • The safety argument for restricted access was made in good faith and predated this. The model they would not release covers the 2019 version of it. Restricting access to a capable model in order to study its misuse first is a coherent position, and the leak ended that possibility for this model permanently.
  • The access argument was also made in good faith. A model that only five organisations can inspect cannot be independently audited, and "approved researcher" is a category those organisations define.
  • Nobody involved chose the outcome. The public got the weights through an act nobody has claimed and no institution authorised, which means it is not a precedent anybody can point to as a decision.

You can hold all four of those at once, and this course does.

7. From here, there are two frontiers

The lasting consequence is structural. Before March 2023, "the state of the art" was a single list. Afterwards there are two, and they behave differently.

Two questions follow, and they are the next two lessons. The first is what "open" actually means once you read the licence rather than the announcement, which is Four axes of open: weights, code, data, licence. The second is who is still doing it, which is Who ships open weights now, and the answer in 2026 is not the organisation this lesson is about.

9. Sources to read first

  • Touvron et al., LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models, submitted 27 February 2023. The public-data argument is in the abstract, and the corpus by source and percentage is section 2.1.
  • Pull request 73 on Meta's LLaMA repository, "Save bandwidth by using a torrent to distribute more efficiently", opened 2 March 2023. Read for its timestamp, which is the earliest dated public artefact in this sequence.
  • Joseph Cox, Facebook's Powerful Large Language Model Leaks Online, Vice, 7 March 2023. The account of the 4chan torrent, and Meta's statement quoted verbatim in section 3.
  • The llama.cpp repository. Its first commit, "Initial release" by Georgi Gerganov, is dated 10 March 2023 in the GitHub record.
  • The Stanford Alpaca repository. Created 10 March 2023, released 13 March 2023. The 52,000 generated instructions, the research-only licensing, and the preliminary human evaluation are all described in its own README.
  • The alpaca-lora repository, created 13 March 2023.
  • Touvron et al., Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models, submitted 18 July 2023. The release four months later, with published weights.

The three repository dates in section 4 were read from the GitHub API on 22 August 2026 rather than taken from any secondary account of the period, because those timestamps are the load-bearing evidence for the whole argument about latency. Note the distinction the API forces: a repository's creation date and its first public release are not always the same day, and Alpaca is an example - created on the tenth, released on the thirteenth.

Retrieval practice

Why does this lesson say the demand was latent rather than created?
The runtime, the tuning recipe and the adapter method were all shelf-ready and blocked on one missing input, which is why the response took days.
What did the paper have to disclose in order to make its own argument?
You cannot claim that publicly available data is sufficient without saying which publicly available data you used.
How did Alpaca avoid the cost of writing instruction data by hand?
The expensive human step was replaced by an API bill, which is what made instruction tuning affordable outside a laboratory.
What is the lasting structural consequence of this episode?
One list became two, one reachable only through an interface and one you can download, and the distance between them is a quantity with a method behind it.
