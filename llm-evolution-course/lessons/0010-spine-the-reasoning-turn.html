The reasoning turn, and where we are

The last new axis was to let a model spend more computation at the moment you ask it, which is a second way to buy capability that does not require training a bigger model.

Where this lesson starts

Making it cheap left models that were cheap enough to use everywhere and still limited to one pass of thought per question. This chapter is the last of the ten, and it is deliberately provisional.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Making it cheap Once capability was possible, the binding constraint became price, and the answer was to stop paying for the whole model on every token.

The one tight idea

The last new axis was to let a model spend more computation at the moment you ask it, which is a second way to buy capability that does not require training a bigger model.

2. "Thinking" is a region of tokens, and nothing else

Start by killing the metaphor, because it does more damage than any other piece of vocabulary in this field.

When a model of 2026 thinks, nothing new happens inside it. There is no deliberation engine, no separate reasoning module and no pause. It is doing exactly what every model in this course has done: predicting the next piece of text.

What changed is what it predicts, and what it was trained to predict. Before answering, the model writes a long stretch of working, marked off by a tag. One 2025 model's own training template says so in plain words: the assistant first thinks about the reasoning process and then provides the answer, and the two are enclosed in separate tags. That opening tag is a token like any other. There is nothing behind it.

Why does a scratchpad help at all? Because a model performs a fixed amount of computation per piece of text it emits. For a hard problem, one piece of text worth of computation is not enough. Write five thousand pieces of working first, each one visible in the context when the next is written, and the model has done five thousand times as much computation on your problem and has an external memory of its own partial results sitting where it can read them.

Teaching a model to be useful records the 2022 discovery that you could ask for this by writing "let us think step by step" in the prompt. The change after 2024 is that models were trained for it, and trained to be good at it, which turns an occasionally helpful prompting trick into a dial you can turn.

Now the caveat, and it belongs in the body of this chapter rather than in a footnote. The visible working is not an explanation of what the model did. It is a sequence of tokens that was rewarded for correlating with correct final answers. Published work has shown models producing traces that do not reflect the factors actually driving their answers. Treat the trace as a scratchpad that demonstrably helps, and never as an account you can audit.

3. Rewards a program can check

The training signal is the actual innovation, and it is where most explanations go vague.

The recipe in Teaching a model to be useful trains on preferences: two answers, a person says which is better, a scorer learns to imitate that, the model optimises against the scorer. That is a good way to teach tone, helpfulness and format. It is a poor way to teach mathematics, because a person skimming a proof cannot reliably tell a subtly wrong one from a right one, and any scorer trained on their judgements inherits exactly that blind spot.

Reasoning training replaces the learned opinion with a program.

The 2025 model that made this public has a reward function with precisely two rules. Put the final answer in a box, and a script checks it against the known answer; if the task is code, run the tests. And put the working inside the right tags. That is the whole reward function. No scorer network. No human raters. The name for this, coined in November 2024, is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.

The consequence is a change of ceiling. Preference training is limited by how fast people can label, and people are expensive and slow. Verifiable rewards are limited by how fast you can generate problems that a program can mark, and that is a machine task. Removing a human from the inner loop is why the reasoning era started when it did.

What the model learns from that is worth stating carefully, because it is genuinely surprising. Nobody instructs it to write at length. It is given hard problems, allowed to try many times, and rewarded only when it lands on the right answer. It discovers on its own that going back over its work, checking, and abandoning a failed approach raise the hit rate, so the working gets longer because length is rewarded rather than because length was requested. The paper's own framing is that rather than teaching the model how to solve a problem, they provided the right incentives and it developed the strategies itself.

And now the limitation, which is severe and structural rather than accidental. Only what a verifier can grade improves. This is why the models of 2026 are startling at competition mathematics and merely good at writing a nuanced memo. The asymmetry is a direct consequence of the training method, and it should be expected to persist for exactly as long as the method does.

4. What did not work, and why that is worth a section

Through 2023 and 2024 the consensus expectation was that reasoning models would be the game-playing recipe applied to language: a search over possible continuations, guided by a learned estimate of how promising each position is. That is roughly how the systems that beat human champions at board games worked, and the analogy was irresistible.

It is not what worked, and two laboratories reported so independently within days of each other in January 2025.

One of them published a section titled "unsuccessful attempts", which is rare enough to be worth reading on its own. Scoring each intermediate step with a learned model failed for three reasons they name: it is hard to define what a step even is in general reasoning, hard to judge whether an intermediate step is correct, and introducing such a scorer invites the model to game it. Tree search failed too: the space of possible continuations is vastly larger and less well defined than the legal moves in a board game, and training a position evaluator good enough to guide the search proved very hard.

The other laboratory stated in its abstract that it had established a simple and effective framework without tree search, value functions or step-level scorers.

Two teams, working separately, found that the sophisticated thing did not work and the simple thing did. That is good history and it is better epistemics: the field's most-anticipated mechanism was not the mechanism that arrived, and the only reason anybody outside those laboratories knows is that one of them chose to publish the failures.

5. Tools, and why agents are a systems problem

A model that can think for a long time is still a model that can only produce text. The other half of the last two years is letting it act.

The pattern was named in October 2022: interleave reasoning with actions, so that the model's working helps it plan and revise while its actions let it fetch information it does not have. By 2023 that had become a trained capability rather than a prompting pattern, and a standard interface: give the model a description of the functions available, and it emits a structured call.

The remaining problem was combinatorial. With several models and many tools, every pairing needed its own integration, so the work grew as the product of the two. A protocol published in November 2024 addressed that by standardising the interface between a model's host application and a tool server, so that the work grows as the sum instead. It was patterned deliberately on the protocol that lets any code editor talk to any programming language's tooling, which had solved the same shape of problem a decade earlier. In December 2025 it was donated to a neutral foundation, which is the point at which it stopped being one vendor's specification.

Teach the security problem alongside it, because it is not a footnote. A tool returns text, and the model that reads that text also has the authority to take actions. Text arriving from a tool can therefore contain instructions, and researchers have documented poisoned tool descriptions and poisoned results that cause a model to leak data through other tools it is connected to. That is the central unsolved security problem of this era, and it is structural rather than a bug anybody can patch.

Here is the claim that matters most about 2026, and it is well evidenced. Capability keeps rising and reliability does not rise with it.

One research group measures the length of task a model can complete, in terms of how long the same task takes a person, and reports that this has been doubling roughly every seven months for years. Cite that as a trend and not as a current number, because their own methodology has changed and their published figures go stale quickly.

Against that, three separate 2026 papers report the same thing from different angles. Recent capability gains have produced only small improvements in reliability. Capability rankings and reliability rankings diverge, with inversions on long tasks. And, counter-intuitively, the strongest models have the highest rates of catastrophic failure, up to nineteen per cent in one study, precisely because they attempt ambitious multi-step strategies that sometimes spiral. A 2025 evaluation of realistic tool-use tasks makes the gap concrete: the best model of the day succeeded on about 53 per cent of tasks when given one attempt, and on about 34 per cent when required to succeed on four attempts out of four.

The distance between those two percentages is the entire agent problem. The model is no longer the binding constraint on what an agent can attempt. The harness around it is: context management, verification, error recovery and the tool surface.

6. Where this stands, on 21 August 2026

This section is deliberately provisional and is written to be replaced rather than amended. Events belong in the chronicle; what follows is the shape of the field rather than its news.

Commoditised. A million positions of context. A thinking effort dial rather than a separate reasoning product. Tool calling through a standard protocol. Native handling of images and video in the same model as text. Sparse expert architecture. Quantised deployment formats shipped by the laboratory rather than produced afterwards by somebody else. A small dense model that would have been described as frontier in 2023.

Contested. Whether benchmarks still measure anything, now that the older ones are saturated and the newer ones are harness-dependent and occasionally audited by a competitor at a convenient moment. Whether agents are reliable enough to deploy without supervision. Whether the gap between open and closed weights is closing or holding. Whether the capital expenditure now committed to this industry has a return. And whether more pre-training scale still buys much, which nobody outside a frontier laboratory can answer because nobody outside one has the data.

New, and nobody predicted it. In June 2026 the United States Department of Commerce ordered a laboratory to suspend access to its two newest models for any foreign national, anywhere, under export-control rules, following a reported jailbreak with national-security implications. The laboratory had no reliable way to check nationality in real time and suspended the models for everybody. The restriction was lifted three weeks later after additional safeguards, and the models were redeployed. Whatever you think of the decision, it established three facts: a frontier model can be treated as a controlled export, a government can order one switched off, and the enforcement mechanism works only because the weights never left the laboratory. That last point is the sharp end of The open-weights turn: a permissively licensed model already copied onto thousands of machines cannot be recalled by anyone at all.

7. What is still open

This is the end of the spine, so it ends with questions rather than a summary. Each of these is stated so that a future reader can tell whether it has been answered.

  • Does more pre-training scale still deliver, and would anyone outside a frontier laboratory be told if it did not?
  • Does training against checkable rewards generalise beyond checkable domains, or are we building systems that are superhuman where a verifier exists and merely good everywhere else, permanently?
  • Can a reasoning trace ever be made a faithful account of the computation, and if not, what is the safety story for a system whose main visible artefact is an unfaithful narrative?
  • Does the open-weight frontier survive the revenue gates, or does "open weights, gated commerce" become the permanent settlement?
  • Can reliability be made to improve alongside capability, or is the finding that the strongest models fail hardest a structural result?

You have now read the whole arc, from a program in 1966 that had no model of meaning to systems that spend minutes of computation on a question and then act on the answer. If you want any single step at full depth, every chapter names the deep dives that zoom into it, and the papers course next door carries the mathematics and the code that this one deliberately left out.

8. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: Thinking is just more tokens A reasoning model does not think in any new sense: it writes a region of working tokens before it answers, which buys both more computation per question and an external place to keep intermediate results.
  • Deep dive: Rewards a machine can check Replace the human rater with a program that can check the answer, and the ceiling on training data moves from how fast people can label to how fast you can generate problems.
  • Deep dive: What did not work, and why that matters The field expected the game-playing recipe of search plus a learned value function to transfer to language, two labs reported independently that it did not, and the simple recipe worked instead.
  • Deep dive: Post-training after PPO: three things deleted The alignment recipe got simpler three separate times, and each simplification is best remembered by which component it removed: the reward model, the human rater, or the critic.
  • Deep dive: Tools, protocols, and the agent loop Tool use went from a prompting pattern to a trained capability to an industry protocol, and the loop it produces is simple enough to draw and hard enough to make reliable.
  • Deep dive: Why agents are a systems problem What a model can do on a single attempt keeps improving while how often it succeeds every time keeps not improving, and that gap is where the binding constraint now sits.
  • Deep dive: Multimodality, and the model as an interface Once images, audio and text all become sequences of tokens in one model, the model stops being a text tool and becomes the place where several kinds of input meet.
  • Deep dive: Where we are now A dated snapshot of what is frontier, what has become a commodity, and what is genuinely contested, written to be replaced rather than edited.
  • Deep dive: What is still open, and what would change it The interesting questions in this field are not predictions but open problems, and each one can be stated with what would count as an answer, so that a future reader can tell whether it has been resolved.
