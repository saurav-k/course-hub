The Transformer turn

If attention already tells every word which other words matter, the recurrence underneath it is only slowing things down, and deleting it turns a sequential machine into a parallel one.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.

Where this lesson starts

The bottleneck, and the fix called attention ended with a machine that could reach across a sentence but still had to walk through it one word at a time. This chapter is about the paper that deleted the walking.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The bottleneck, and the fix called attention Translating a sentence by squeezing all of it into one fixed list of numbers works until the sentence gets long, and attention is the idea of not squeezing it at all.

The one tight idea

If attention already tells every word which other words matter, the recurrence underneath it is only slowing things down, and deleting it turns a sequential machine into a parallel one.

2. Keep the attention, delete the walking

By 2016 a translation model had two parts pulling in opposite directions. Attention let any output position draw on any input position in one move. The recurrent network underneath still insisted on reading the sentence one word at a time, because the state after word ten cannot be computed until the state after word nine exists. The bottleneck, and the fix called attention ends exactly there: reach was solved and speed was not.

The field tried three ways out in eighteen months. You could replace recurrence with convolutions, which are parallel, and both a Google team and a Facebook team did, in October 2016 and May 2017. You could compute only part of the network for each input, which Noam Shazeer and colleagues did in January 2017. Or you could ask whether the recurrence was doing anything that attention was not already doing.

The third path had already been walked, quietly, on a different task. In June 2016 Ankur Parikh, Oscar Tackstrom, Dipanjan Das and Jakob Uszkoreit published a model for deciding whether one sentence implies another. It used attention and no recurrence at all, it was, in their words, trivially parallelisable, and it reached the state of the art without relying on any word-order information. A year later Uszkoreit is the person the Transformer paper's own footnote credits with proposing that recurrent networks be replaced by self-attention.

Self-attention is the same mechanism pointed inwards. Ordinary attention lets the output sentence look at the input sentence. Self-attention lets every word of one sentence look at every other word of the same sentence, and mix in whatever it finds useful. In "the animal did not cross the street because it was too tired", the word it needs to pick up something from animal rather than from street. Every word does this at once, independently, which is the whole point.

3. What deleting recurrence buys

"Attention Is All You Need" was posted to arXiv on 12 June 2017 by eight authors at Google: Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Lukasz Kaiser and Illia Polosukhin. All eight carry an equal-contribution mark and a footnote saying the listing order is random, which is unusual and worth knowing. The abstract's claim is a subtraction: the model dispenses with recurrence and convolutions entirely.

The most important object in the paper is a table with three columns, and the two that matter are these. A recurrent layer needs a number of sequential steps that grows with the sentence length, and information travelling from the first word to the last has to make that many hops. A self-attention layer needs one sequential step, and information travels from any word to any other word in one hop.

Those two changes are the same change seen from two angles. One hop means nothing has to survive a long journey, so the model stops fighting distance. One step means every position can be computed at the same moment, so a graphics card with thousands of units can use all of them instead of waiting in line.

The results follow from that. The smaller of the two models in the paper trained in twelve hours on eight graphics cards and beat everything previously published on English-to-German translation. The larger one trained in three and a half days and scored 28.4 on that benchmark, more than two points ahead of the previous best, including ensembles. It used roughly an order of magnitude less computation to get there than the production system it displaced. That is why it was adopted so fast: it was better and cheaper at the same time.

4. What deleting recurrence costs

Two bills arrive with self-attention, and the field has been paying both ever since.

The first is the arithmetic. If every word attends to every word, a ten-word sentence needs a hundred comparisons and a hundred-word sentence needs ten thousand. The work grows with the square of the length. That is fine for a sentence and painful for a document, and essentially every efficiency technique in Making it cheap is a way of paying that bill down.

The second is that self-attention has no idea what order the words are in. Shuffle them and the arithmetic gives the same answer, because a weighted blend does not care which order you blend in. The paper's answer is to add a fixed pattern of waves to each word's numbers, one wave per dimension at geometrically spaced frequencies, so every position carries a distinct fingerprint and nearby positions carry similar ones. It is a pretty solution and the paper is honest that it was not a better one: they also tried simply learning a position vector for each slot and reported that the two produced nearly identical results. Both of the 2018 models described in section 6 below used the learned version instead.

5. It was a translation paper, and it was not the starting gun

The compressed version of this story says the Transformer started the era of large language models. Read the paper and that is not what it is.

It evaluates on two machine translation tasks and on English sentence parsing. It contains no language model, no pretraining, and no argument about what happens if you make things bigger. The phrase "large language model" does not appear in it. It is better read as the last paper of the machine translation era than the first paper of the era that followed, and it became a starting gun only in retrospect.

The dates make the same point more sharply. OpenAI was announced on 11 December 2015 as a non-profit, eighteen months before this paper existed, and the architecture it is now famous for was invented somewhere else. In June 2016 it published a list of four technical goals, one of which was an agent with useful natural language understanding that could hold a conversation, understand a document and follow complex instructions, and it said plainly that no algorithms existed for any of that. The destination was written down correctly in mid-2016. The vehicle arrived a year later, from Google.

What everyone did already understand by 2016 was that computation was the binding constraint. Google announced its own machine-learning chip in May 2016 and revealed it had already been running in data centres for over a year. In the week of 9 August 2016, NVIDIA's chief executive personally delivered the first production DGX-1, a 3U box holding eight graphics cards, to OpenAI's San Francisco office, and inscribed the chassis by hand. Elon Musk thanked NVIDIA publicly for donating it; NVIDIA's own announcement never used that word, and a contemporary report noted that the machine listed at $129,000 and that it was not clear whether OpenAI had paid for it. The teachable image is not somebody palming a graphics card. It is a three-thousand-watt box being wheeled into a non-profit's office and signed like a guitar, with the question of who paid for it left unanswered on the record.

6. One architecture, two children

Because it was a translation paper, the Transformer has two halves. The encoder reads the whole source sentence at once, every word seeing every other word in both directions. The decoder writes the target sentence one word at a time, allowed to see everything the encoder produced but only the words it has already written itself, because a model that can see the answer cannot be trained to predict it.

Within eighteen months the field had cut it in half twice, in opposite directions.

In June 2018 Alec Radford, Karthik Narasimhan, Tim Salimans and Ilya Sutskever at OpenAI kept the decoder and threw away the encoder, trained it to predict the next word on seven thousand unpublished books, and then adapted it to each task. They called it generative pre-training, which is where the initials GPT come from.

In October 2018 Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee and Kristina Toutanova at Google kept the encoder and threw away the decoder. Their model, BERT, cannot be trained to predict the next word, because every word can already see every other word, so predicting a word would be trivial. Instead they hid fifteen per cent of the words at random and trained it to fill in the blanks.

BERT won, and for about eighteen months it was not close. It beat the decoder-only model head to head at matched size on the benchmarks everyone was scored on, Google released the weights and the code immediately, and fine-tuning it was cheap enough that one graphics card bought you a state-of-the-art classifier. Eighteen days after the preprint, a small company called Hugging Face created a repository to port it to a different framework, and that repository became the standard way the whole field loads everyone else's models. That is what releasing weights buys, and it is worth remembering when The open-weights turn reopens the argument about releasing weights in 2023.

It would be wrong to say the encoder branch was a dead end. Encoder models are still the right tool for classifying and searching text and are far cheaper at those jobs. What happened is that the interesting problem moved to generation, and an encoder cannot follow it there.

7. One more thing: a model never sees words

Everything above talks about words, and no model in this course has ever seen one.

A network reads numbers, so text has to be cut into pieces from a fixed list, each with an identifier. One entry per word never finishes, because somebody will always type a name, a typo or a web address you have no entry for. One entry per character never runs out but makes every sentence enormously long, and the previous section explains why length is expensive.

The compromise is called byte-pair encoding. It was a data-compression trick from 1994, repurposed for text in 2015: start from individual characters, repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair and merge it into a new unit, and stop when the list is as long as you want. Common words end up as one piece; rare words break into familiar fragments. From 2019 the standard version starts from raw bytes rather than characters, of which there are exactly 256, which means nothing in any writing system can ever be missing from the list. That is where the odd number 50,257 comes from in the 2019 model at the centre of The scaling bet: 256 single bytes, 50,000 learned merges, and one special marker.

Keep that in mind, because it has consequences that surface repeatedly. Capitalisation costs extra pieces. Numbers split in arbitrary places, which is a large part of why these models were bad at arithmetic. And a language whose script never appeared much in the training text pays several times more pieces per sentence than English does.

So by the end of 2018 the field had a fast architecture, a way of turning text into numbers, and two rival ways of pretraining a model on more text than anyone could label. What it did not have was any principled idea of how big to build one, or how much text to feed it. Choosing those numbers was guesswork on a multi-million-dollar bet, and the four years in which guesswork turned into a forecast are The scaling bet.

8. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: Two papers, one day On 12 June 2017 two unrelated papers were posted, one that made models fast and one that made them listen to people, and the rest of this course is those two threads converging.
  • Deep dive: The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked Delete the recurrence and keep only attention, and every position in a sentence can be computed at the same time with a constant number of operations between any two words.
  • Deep dive: ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later Train one model on a mountain of unlabelled text, then adapt it cheaply to each task, and the field's economics invert: the expensive step happens once instead of once per task.
  • Deep dive: The fork: GPT-1 and BERT Apply the same pretraining recipe to the two halves of the Transformer and you get two incompatible models, one that understands and one that writes, and for eighteen months the understanding one wins.
  • Deep dive: Tokens: how text becomes numbers A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

Retrieval practice

What does deleting recurrence actually change?
One sequential step means the hardware is fully used, and one hop between any two words means nothing has to survive a long journey.
What does self-attention cost that recurrence did not?
Everything attending to everything is quadratic, and a weighted blend is indifferent to order, so position has to be added by hand.
What kind of paper is "Attention Is All You Need"?
It evaluates two translation tasks and English parsing; the leap to pretraining on the internet is a separate idea that arrives in 2018.
Why can BERT not write a story?
An encoder is bidirectional by construction, which is exactly what makes next-word prediction meaningless and fill-in-the-blank necessary.
