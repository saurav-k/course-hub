Deep dive · The reasoning era · Lesson 0051

What did not work, and why that matters

The field expected the game-playing recipe of search plus a learned value function to transfer to language, two labs reported independently that it did not, and the simple recipe worked instead.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson is about negative results, and it is the epistemics lesson of the reasoning story. It assumes you have read Rewards a machine can check.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Rewards a machine can check Replace the human rater with a program that can check the answer, and the ceiling on training data moves from how fast people can label to how fast you can generate problems.

The one tight idea

The field expected the game-playing recipe of search plus a learned value function to transfer to language, two labs reported independently that it did not, and the simple recipe worked instead.

2. The analogy everybody was working from

Between 2016 and 2017 a program beat the strongest human players in the world at Go, and the recipe it used became the most admired result in machine learning. It had two parts. A search that looked ahead over many possible continuations of the game, and a learned value function that looked at a position and estimated how likely it was to lead to a win, so that the search knew which branches were worth exploring.

When the question turned to making a language model reason, those two parts were the obvious things to import. A model producing a long piece of working is, after all, making a sequence of choices, and each choice opens a branch. Score the branches, search the good ones, and you have the Go recipe applied to sentences.

This was not a fringe idea that somebody later invented in order to knock down. There is a published line of work behind it, and it is worth naming, because the strength of the expectation is what makes the negative result interesting.

  • November 2022. Uesato and colleagues at DeepMind ran the first comprehensive comparison of supervising the final answer against supervising each reasoning step, on grade-school word problems. They found that supervising the process, or using a reward model that emulated it, was necessary to get the intermediate steps right rather than only the final answer.
  • May 2023. Lightman and colleagues at OpenAI published Let's Verify Step by Step, reporting that process supervision significantly outperformed outcome supervision on a hard mathematics dataset. They also released PRM800K, eight hundred thousand step-level human feedback labels. That dataset is the best available measure of how much the field believed in this: somebody paid for eight hundred thousand human judgements about individual lines of working.
  • September 2023. Feng and colleagues published a framework whose title says the expectation out loud, that tree search of the kind used for board games can guide a language model's decoding and its training.
  • August 2024. Snell and colleagues studied how to spend computation at the moment a question is asked, and one of the two mechanisms they analysed was searching against dense verifier reward models that score each step.

Then, in September 2024, a laboratory shipped a model that visibly spent time working before it answered and did not say how it had been built. Into that silence, the analogy hardened into an assumption. A great many people, in public and in private, said the same sentence: it is the board-game recipe, applied to language.

3. What two laboratories actually reported

On 22 January 2025 two papers appeared on arXiv on the same day, from two companies that were not collaborating.

The first is DeepSeek-R1, and its section 4.2 is titled Unsuccessful Attempts. A section with that title in a frontier model paper is close to unheard of, and it is the reason this lesson exists.

The step-scoring model

They tried training a model to score each intermediate step, which the literature calls a process reward model, and they name three limitations that stopped them. It is challenging to explicitly define what a fine-grained step even is in general reasoning. Deciding whether a given intermediate step is correct is itself hard, because automated annotation may not yield satisfactory results and manual annotation does not scale. And once a model-based scorer is in the loop, in their words, it inevitably leads to reward hacking: the policy learns to produce working that the scorer likes rather than working that is right, and fixing that means retraining the scorer, which costs more compute and complicates the pipeline.

Their conclusion is carefully bounded. Such a scorer is genuinely useful for reranking a handful of candidate answers or guiding a search, but its advantages were limited compared with the overhead it added to large-scale reinforcement learning in their experiments.

The tree search

They also tried the search itself, and they say plainly that they were inspired by the Go programs. They broke answers into parts, prompted the model to tag the steps that the search needed, used a pre-trained value model to guide the search to answers, and then trained on what it found.

Two things broke. Unlike chess, where the search space is relatively well defined, token generation presents an exponentially larger search space, so they had to cap how far each node could expand, and capping it left the model stuck in local optima. And the value model that guided every step of the search turned out to be very hard to train well, which they contrast directly with the board-game case: AlphaGo's core success relied on a value model that improved progressively, and that principle proved difficult to replicate for token generation.

The other laboratory, the same day

Kimi k1.5, from Moonshot AI, does not have a failure section. It makes the same point in its abstract instead, as a claim about what it did not need: it establishes a simple and effective reinforcement learning framework without relying on more complex techniques such as Monte Carlo tree search, value functions, and process reward models, and reports results matching the leading closed reasoning model of the day.

So two teams, working separately and publishing on the same day, agreed: the sophisticated machinery was not necessary, and one of them added that it had actively tried it and it had not worked. What replaced it is the subject of Rewards a machine can check: sample a group of whole answers, let a program mark the final one, and reinforce the samples that beat their own group's average.

4. Why a board is not a sentence

It is worth understanding why the transfer failed, because the reason is structural rather than a matter of anybody trying harder.

The branching factor. In a board game the moves available in a position number in the tens or the low hundreds, and they are enumerable: a program can list them exactly. A language model choosing its next token is choosing from its whole vocabulary at every single position, and the vocabulary is large. DeepSeek-R1's own published configuration file gives its vocabulary as 129,280 entries. A search that looks even a few tokens ahead is therefore looking at a number of branches with no useful analogy in a board game, which is what the paper means by an exponentially larger space.

The state. A board position is a complete description of the game. Two players handed the same position know everything there is to know, and a program can check it, compare it with another, and store it. Half of a written argument is not a state in that sense. There is no agreed way to say where the reasoning has got to, two passages that look different may be the same step in different words, and the same passage may be a good step or a bad one depending on what the model does next. That is the same difficulty as defining a step, seen from the other side.

The referee. A board game ends in a win or a loss, and that fact is free, exact and unarguable. A partial piece of reasoning has no such ending, so the only way to score it is to train something that guesses, and anything that guesses can be fooled by the thing it is scoring. This is why the reward-hacking complaint is not incidental. It is what happens when the referee is itself a learned model being optimised against.

5. What the negative result is worth, and what it is not

Almost nothing in this literature reports a failure. Laboratories publish what worked, conferences accept what worked, and the result is a public record that systematically overstates how well any given idea travels, because the attempts that went nowhere are invisible. A reader of that record cannot tell the difference between an idea nobody tried and an idea several people tried and quietly dropped.

Section 4.2 of that paper closes part of the gap, and it bought three things. It stopped other teams spending a year rebuilding something two teams had already abandoned. It corrected a belief that a large part of the field held, in public, with confidence. And it is specific enough to be argued with, which is the property that makes a claim scientific rather than promotional.

Now the limits, because overstating a negative result is as bad as overstating a positive one, and the authors say so themselves. Their own sentence is that they share their failure experiences to provide insight, but that this does not imply that these approaches are incapable of developing effective reasoning models.

What was actually established is narrower than "search does not work for language". It is this: two named teams, at their scale, with their compute budget, in early 2025, found that step-scoring models and tree search cost more than they returned, and shipped without them. The same paper still recommends a step-scoring model for reranking a small number of candidates. And the argument is live rather than closed: in April 2025 the VAPO work reported that value-based methods can beat the simpler group-based recipe on long chains of reasoning once three known failure modes are handled, which is a direct counter-claim from a different team.

That is the reading skill this lesson is really teaching, and Reading an eval table like a scientist generalises it: a result is a statement about a setting, and the setting is the part that gets dropped when the claim is repeated.

6. What this made possible

There is a practical consequence, and it is the debt this lesson hands to the next one.

A recipe with a search, a value model and a step scorer in it is a distributed systems project. A recipe that samples a group of answers and marks the last line is a training loop that a small team can write and run. Because the thing that worked was the simple thing, it was reproducible, and within months the same approach was visible in open-weight releases from several organisations. Simplicity is not only elegant here. It is the reason the reasoning recipe spread as fast as it did.

That is one instance of a pattern that runs through the whole of post-training since 2022, where the recipe got simpler three separate times and each simplification is remembered by the component it deleted. Post-training after PPO: three things deleted is that story.

8. Sources to read first

  • DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning, arXiv v1, 22 January 2025. Section 4.2, "Unsuccessful Attempts", is the primary source for every quoted limitation in sections 3 and 4 of this lesson. The published config.json for the released weights gives the vocabulary size of 129,280.
  • Kimi Team, Kimi k1.5: Scaling Reinforcement Learning with LLMs, arXiv, 22 January 2025. The abstract is the source for the claim that the framework needs no tree search, value functions or process reward models.
  • Uesato et al., Solving math word problems with process- and outcome-based feedback, arXiv, 25 November 2022.
  • Lightman et al., Let's Verify Step by Step, arXiv, 31 May 2023. The source for process supervision outperforming outcome supervision on MATH, and for the release of PRM800K.
  • Feng et al., AlphaZero-like Tree-Search can Guide Large Language Model Decoding and Training, arXiv, 29 September 2023.
  • Snell et al., Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters, arXiv, 6 August 2024.
  • Yue et al., VAPO: Efficient and Reliable Reinforcement Learning for Advanced Reasoning Tasks, arXiv, 7 April 2025. The counter-claim quoted in section 5.

Retrieval practice

What did the two January 2025 papers actually establish?
The paper says outright that reporting a failure does not imply the approach cannot work, so the claim is about their setting and their scale.
Why is searching over a board easier than searching over a sentence?
Search needs an enumerable move set, a comparable state and a free exact outcome, and a half-written answer supplies none of the three.
Why does a learned scorer for each step invite reward hacking?
A guessing referee can be optimised against, which is why the paper calls retraining it a recurring cost rather than a one-off.
Why is a published failure unusually valuable in this literature?
Without failure reports a reader cannot distinguish an untried idea from one several teams tried and abandoned.
