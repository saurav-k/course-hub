The bottleneck, and the fix called attention

Translating a sentence by squeezing all of it into one fixed list of numbers works until the sentence gets long, and attention is the idea of not squeezing it at all.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.

Chapter 3 of 10. Reading the spine on its own takes an evening. Next chapter: The Transformer turn.

Where this lesson starts

When meaning became geometry left words as positions in a space. This chapter is about sentences rather than words: how a machine reads one and writes another, and the single point where that pipeline broke.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • When meaning became geometry Neural networks turned words from symbols that are either identical or unrelated into positions in a space where closeness means similarity, and that single change is what made everything after it possible.

The one tight idea

Translating a sentence by squeezing all of it into one fixed list of numbers works until the sentence gets long, and attention is the idea of not squeezing it at all.

2. Read the whole thing, then write the whole thing

A word on its own is not a task. Translation is a task, and it has an awkward shape: the input is a sentence of some length, the output is a sentence of a different length, and the words do not line up one to one. German puts the verb at the end. French needs two words where English needs one. You cannot walk down the source sentence emitting a target word per step.

The design that solved this is called the encoder-decoder, and it splits the job in two. The encoder reads the source sentence, one word at a time, keeping a running summary as it goes. When it reaches the end, that running summary is the sentence: a fixed list of numbers, the same length whether the sentence was four words or forty. The decoder then starts from that list and writes the target sentence, one word at a time, each new word conditioned on the summary and on what it has written so far.

Be concrete about what the running summary is. It is a list of numbers, of a size the designer picks in advance, that gets overwritten after every word the encoder reads. When meaning became geometry introduced a word as a position in a space of a few hundred numbers; this is the same kind of object, standing for a whole sentence instead of one word.

Read, then write. The elegance is that neither half needs to know anything about grammar, and neither half needs the two sentences to be the same length. Nobody specifies how meaning should be encoded in that list of numbers. The training loop decides, because whatever encoding lets the decoder produce good translations is the encoding that reduces the error.

Three groups arrived at this within a year. Nal Kalchbrenner and Phil Blunsom got there first, in October 2013. Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues formalised it in June 2014. In September 2014 Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le made it beat the best statistical translation system of the day, scoring 34.81 on the standard English-to-French translation benchmark against 33.30 for the phrase-based system it was up against. That result is what made the field take neural translation seriously.

3. The problem is that one list of numbers

The seq2seq paper says the quiet part out loud, in a single sentence: the deep network it describes uses 8,000 real numbers to represent a sentence.

Eight thousand numbers is a lot for "the cat sat on the mat". It is not a lot for a forty-word legal clause with three subordinate constructions in it. Both get exactly the same amount of room, because the summary is a fixed size by construction. Everything the decoder will ever know about the source has to survive that squeeze.

Cho and colleagues measured what that costs, in a short paper a few days later: translation quality degrades rapidly as sentence length and the number of unfamiliar words increase. Short sentences are fine. Long sentences fall apart.

There is also a tell inside the seq2seq paper itself. Its authors found that feeding the source sentence to the encoder backwards improved results substantially. That works because it shortens the distance between the first source word and the first target word, so the correction signal during training has a shorter path to travel. A trick that helps by shortening distances is a trick that tells you the architecture was already fighting distance.

4. Attention is the decision not to compress

On 1 September 2014 Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio published the fix, and their statement of the problem is hard to improve on. A potential issue with the encoder-decoder approach, they wrote, is that the network needs to compress all the necessary information of a source sentence into a fixed-length vector, which may make it difficult to cope with long sentences. Their solution frees the model from having to squash all the information of a source sentence, regardless of its length, into a fixed-length vector.

The mechanism is simpler than its name. Instead of one summary at the end, the encoder emits one list of numbers per source word and keeps all of them. Then, at every single step of writing the output, the decoder does three things. It scores each source position for how relevant it is right now. It turns those scores into weights that add up to one, so they read as percentages. It takes the weighted blend of the source positions and uses that as its context for this one word.

The next output word gets a fresh set of weights. Nothing is fixed, nothing is thrown away, and the model decides where to look as it goes.

An example makes the reordering concrete. English says "the European Economic Area" and French says the equivalent of "the area economic European". A decoder writing the French word for "area" needs to reach forward past two English words; when it later writes the French for "European" it needs to reach back. With one fixed summary, none of that is visible: the decoder has a blur of the whole sentence and no way to ask which part it needs now. With attention the weights simply cross over at that point, and you can see them do it.

The most striking part is what those weights look like when you draw them. Lay the source words along one axis and the output words along the other, and shade each cell by its weight. What appears is roughly a diagonal, with the diagonal breaking and crossing over exactly where the two languages order their words differently. That is a word alignment, which is the object statistical translation systems had been estimating explicitly since the early 1990s. Nobody told this model to produce one. It fell out of being allowed to choose what to look at, which is why the paper is titled "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate".

5. Nobody invented attention on their own

Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio are the right citation for the attention that the architecture in The Transformer turn is built from, and it would still be wrong to tell this as one team having one idea.

Late 2014 produced the same shape in several places at once. Alex Graves, Greg Wayne and Ivo Danihelka published Neural Turing Machines in October 2014, in which a network reads from an external memory by attending to it. Jason Weston, Sumit Chopra and Antoine Bordes published Memory Networks in the same month, on the same theme. Kalchbrenner and Blunsom had reached neural encoder-decoders a year earlier without attention at all. A year later, in August 2015, Minh-Thang Luong, Hieu Pham and Christopher Manning simplified Bahdanau's scoring function and offered a version that is simply the dot product of two lists of numbers, which is the direct ancestor of the scoring used by every model this course goes on to describe.

The honest summary is that content-based lookup was in the air, several groups reached for it, and the version that stuck was the one aimed at translation.

6. What attention did not fix

Attention fixed reach. Any output position could now draw on any input position, however far away, without anything having to survive a fixed-size squeeze.

It did not fix speed, and the reason is structural. Underneath the attention, both halves are still recurrent networks: each one reads a sequence by processing one word, updating a running state, then processing the next. To compute the state after word ten you must already have the state after word nine. That is a hard dependency, and it means a graphics card with thousands of idle units has to wait its turn, word by word. When meaning became geometry made the case that the entire 2012 breakthrough was about doing thousands of independent operations at once; recurrence is exactly the thing that stops you doing that.

That matters more than it sounds, because of what makes the hardware fast. A graphics card is wide rather than quick: it wins by doing thousands of independent operations at the same moment, which is the argument When meaning became geometry makes about 2012. A model that insists on finishing word nine before starting word ten is a model that leaves nearly all of that width idle. So the machine best suited to this arithmetic was being used at a fraction of its capacity, and every increase in sentence length made that worse rather than better.

The cost was not theoretical. In March 2017 a Google team burned more than 250,000 graphics-card hours tuning recurrent translation architectures on a single language pair, which is what the state of the art cost to move by that point.

So by mid-2017 the field had a mechanism that could reach anywhere in a sentence, bolted on top of a mechanism that forced it to walk through the sentence one step at a time. Somebody was going to ask whether the walking was still necessary. The Transformer turn is what happened when they did.

Retrieval practice

What exactly is the bottleneck in an encoder-decoder without attention?
The summary is a fixed-size list of numbers, so a forty-word sentence is squeezed into the room a four-word sentence needed.
What does attention actually compute at each output step?
Scores over all source positions become weights that sum to one, and the weighted blend is that step's context; nothing is discarded.
Why is the alignment picture that attention produces surprising?
Statistical translation had estimated alignments explicitly for twenty years; here one appears as a by-product of an unrelated objective.
What problem did attention leave completely untouched?
Reach was fixed and parallelism was not: computing the state after word ten still requires the state after word nine.
