Long context: capacity is not competence

The advertised context length is how much text a model will accept, not how much it can use, and the two numbers are routinely different by a large factor.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.
Where this lesson starts

Products advertise context windows as a headline feature. This lesson is about what that number does and does not promise. It assumes you know what the key-value cache costs, from the serving lesson.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs Generating a token requires re-reading everything the model has already seen, so serving is a memory problem before it is an arithmetic problem, and most of the engineering since 2022 is about that memory.
  • Tokens: how text becomes numbers A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

The one tight idea

The advertised context length is how much text a model will accept, not how much it can use, and the two numbers are routinely different by a large factor.

2. Two different numbers, both called the context length

Every product built on a language model advertises a context length: 128,000, 200,000, a million. The unit is tokens rather than words, because a model reads pieces chosen by a tokenizer rather than words, which Tokens: how text becomes numbers describes. Beyond that, almost every reader takes the number to mean something it does not mean.

The advertised figure is a capacity claim. It says that the software will accept that much text without returning an error, and that the model's position handling has been arranged to cover that many positions. It says nothing about whether the model can find, combine or act on what is in there.

The number a reader actually wants is a competence figure: the length beyond which the model stops using what you gave it. That number is smaller, it is usually much smaller, and it is almost never the one on the marketing page. This lesson is about the gap between the two, why it exists, and what the field did once it stopped trying to close it.

3. Failure one: the middle is used least

The first well-documented failure was published in July 2023 by a group at Stanford, Berkeley and Samaya AI. They took a question-answering task, buried the document containing the answer among distractor documents, and then moved it: to the start of the input, to the middle, to the end, changing nothing else.

The accuracy curve they got is U-shaped. Models did best when the needed information was at the very beginning or at the very end, and got worse when it was in the middle. The two lobes have names borrowed from psychology, primacy and recency, and they are not something anyone designed into these models.

The sharpest single result in that paper is worth stating exactly, because it is stranger than the curve. With the relevant document placed in the middle of its input, GPT-3.5-Turbo scored lower than the same model given no documents at all, which was 56.1 per cent. Being handed the answer, in the wrong position, was worse than not being handed it. The paper also found that models with extended context windows often performed identically to their ordinary-length siblings, which is the first published version of this lesson's whole point.

The practical advice that fell out of this is real and is still repeated: put the important thing last, or first. The paper does not settle why the two lobes exist, and this course will not guess at a cause it cannot source. What it establishes is the shape, and the shape is enough to change how a prompt is written.

4. Failure two: retrieval is not comprehension

By 2024 the standard way to advertise a long window was the needle-in-a-haystack test: hide one distinctive sentence in a very long document and ask the model to repeat it. Models passed it almost perfectly, and the passing screenshots became marketing.

In April 2024 a group at NVIDIA published RULER, which asked what that test actually measures. Their answer is that finding one distinctive fact is the easiest thing a long context can be asked for, because the needle does not resemble anything around it. RULER keeps that test and adds twelve more that do not have that property: several needles at once, needles that resemble the haystack, chains of references that have to be followed from one part of the document to another, and questions whose answers require aggregating across the whole input rather than locating one spot in it.

Then they defined a usable length, and the definition is the part worth carrying away. Take the average score of a small, ordinary, short-context model at 4,000 tokens as a passing threshold. A model's effective length is the longest input at which it still clears that bar. It is a deliberately unambitious standard: not "does it excel", but "is it still as good as a plain 2023 model was on a short document".

The paper's own summary of seventeen models is the sentence to remember: almost all of them dropped below the threshold before reaching their claimed context lengths, and although every one of them claimed 32,000 tokens or more, only about half could hold up at 32,000.

Notice what kind of failure this is. These models did not refuse the input or crash. They answered, fluently, and were wrong more often as the input grew. A capacity claim and a competence claim can differ by a factor of four, or sixty, without anything visibly breaking.

5. How the window got longer, and why that is a separate question

It is worth knowing roughly how a window is extended, because it explains why capacity outran competence rather than the two moving together.

A model trained with a rotary position scheme encodes each position as a rotation applied to its queries and keys, so the score between two positions depends on the angle between them, which depends on their distance. The papers course derives it. The relevant property here is simple: the model has only ever seen angles corresponding to distances up to its training length, and it does not generalise to angles it has never seen. Feed it a longer document and the position signal is unfamiliar rather than merely large.

The family of fixes, of which YaRN in August 2023 is the best known, works by changing the mapping from distance to angle so that a longer document is squeezed into the range of angles the model already understands, with the different frequencies in the scheme treated differently so that fine local detail is preserved while coarse long-range structure is stretched. A short amount of further training then adapts the model to the new mapping. YaRN's stated advantage over earlier methods was cost: ten times fewer tokens and 2.5 times fewer training steps.

That is the crux. Extending a window is a cheap, targeted operation on the position scheme plus a small amount of training. Teaching a model to reason across a million tokens is not a targeted operation on anything. The two got separated because one of them was easy, and the advertised number tracks the easy one.

6. Failure three: it costs, even when it works

The third failure mode is not about quality at all. Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs establishes that generating each token requires re-reading a stored key and value for every token already seen, at every layer, and that this store grows with the conversation. A long context is therefore a large store re-read once per generated token, and the arithmetic of comparing every position with every position grows with the square of the length.

So even a model that uses its full window well is slow and expensive at the top of that window, and that cost is what motivated the whole selective-attention programme covered in Sparsity moves to attention. It also creates an awkward interaction with this lesson: selective attention improves the cost failure by having each query attend to a shortlist of positions instead of all of them, which is a bet that the omitted positions did not matter. Whether that bet holds is measured by exactly the kind of demanding evaluation described in section 4, which is why these two lessons have to be read together rather than separately.

7. The turn: manage the context instead of lengthening it

By 2026 the interesting engineering had moved from making the window bigger to deciding what goes in it. The clearest evidence for that is not a paper but a product surface: the features vendors added, and the reasons they gave for adding them.

Anthropic's developer documentation, read on 22 August 2026, describes three of them. Compaction automatically summarises older context when a conversation approaches the limit, and the documentation's stated rationale is blunt: as a conversation grows, response quality degrades, so compaction replaces older content with a concise summary. Context editing selectively clears specific content, such as tool results that are no longer needed, and its rationale is stated in the same register: context is a finite resource with diminishing returns, and irrelevant content degrades model focus. Memory tools let a system write things down outside the conversation and read them back on demand.

Read that against the marketing. The same industry that advertises a million-token window ships, documents and recommends machinery whose explicit purpose is to keep far less than a million tokens in front of the model. Both things are true at once and neither is dishonest. The window is genuinely that large, and using all of it is genuinely a bad idea.

The cost of managing is information loss, and it should not be glossed over. A summary is smaller than what it summarises, so a compaction step throws away detail that a later turn may need, and no summariser can know which detail that will be. This is a real trade rather than a free improvement, and it is one of the reasons Why agents are a systems problem exists as a lesson.

8. How to read a context-length claim

Four questions, in order, and they work on any claim of this kind.

Is this a capacity number or a competence number? If nobody named an evaluation, it is capacity. A figure with no test behind it is a statement about what the software accepts.

What was the test, and was it retrieval? A single distinctive fact hidden in a long document is the easiest possible long-context task. If the demonstration is a needle-in-a-haystack chart, it has shown that the model can look something up, and nothing else.

Where in the input was the information? The July 2023 result means a demonstration that puts the crucial material at the end of the prompt is measuring the model's best case.

Does the vendor ship context management for this model? This is the most useful question and the least often asked. If the same company selling a million-token window also sells automatic summarisation for long conversations, and documents it by saying that quality degrades as the conversation grows, then the vendor has already told you what the usable length is like. Believe the engineering documentation over the headline.

Retrieval practice

What does an advertised context length actually promise?
It is a capacity claim about what is accepted, and a competence figure needs a named evaluation on a named date to exist at all.
What is the sharpest finding in the July 2023 positional study?
Information that is present but badly placed can be worse than information that is absent, which is why fitting in the window and being used are separate questions.
Why does the needle-in-a-haystack test overstate long-context ability?
Tracing chains of references and aggregating across a whole document are the tasks that separate models, and the vanilla test contains neither.
What is the strongest everyday evidence that usable length is short?
A company selling a million-token window and also selling automatic compaction for it has documented the answer in its own engineering pages.
