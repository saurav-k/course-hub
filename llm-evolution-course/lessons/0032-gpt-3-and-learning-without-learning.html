GPT-3, and learning without learning

A model whose weights never change gets better at a task when you put a few worked examples in the prompt, which is a kind of learning that leaves no trace in the model.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Predicting the future with a straight line gives a rule for how big to build. This lesson is what happened when someone followed it, and the surprise that arrived with the result. It assumes you know that a trained model's weights are frozen.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Predicting the future with a straight line Model loss falls as a power law in size, data and compute, which means a straight line on a log-log plot, which means you can forecast the result of a run you have not paid for.
  • Where the data actually came from The dataset behind GPT-2 was scraped by its authors and filtered by a proxy for human approval, it was not a public crawl, and that filtering idea outlived the model that introduced it.

The one tight idea

A model whose weights never change gets better at a task when you put a few worked examples in the prompt, which is a kind of learning that leaves no trace in the model.

2. Following the rule, at the largest scale anyone had tried

In January 2020 a study from OpenAI turned model sizing from a guess into a forecast, and told anyone with a growing budget to spend it mostly on making the model bigger. Predicting the future with a straight line is that study. Four months later, on 28 May 2020, thirty-one authors led by Tom Brown published Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, which is what following that instruction looks like.

The headline model has 175 billion parameters, 96 layers and a width of 12,288, against GPT-2's largest at 1.56 billion and 48 layers. It was trained on 300 billion tokens and used 3.14 × 1023 floating-point operations, a number the paper prints in a table rather than leaving anybody to estimate. For comparison, the paper GPT-2 came from disclosed no compute figure at all.

The part that matters most for this course is what did not change. The paper says it uses "the same model and architecture as GPT-2", with one exception about how attention is arranged in alternating layers. Two years and a hundredfold increase in size, and essentially no architectural idea. That is what "scale beats shape" meant in practice, and it is why this lesson is short on mechanism.

Eight models were trained, not one, from 125 million parameters up to 175 billion, so that everything reported could be reported as a curve against size rather than as a single number.

3. Three ways to ask, one frozen model

Here is the thing that surprised people, and it needs its terms defined carefully because they are routinely used loosely.

A trained model's weights are frozen. Training is over; nothing about the model changes when you use it. Every one of the settings below uses the same frozen model, and the only difference is what text you put in front of the question.

Three prompt formats, defined by what goes into the input. None of them changes a weight.
NameWhat the input contains
Zero-shotA description of the task, then the question
One-shotA description, one worked example, then the question
Few-shotA description, several worked examples, then the question

The examples are text. They are read, they are used, and when the answer has been produced they are gone, because there is nowhere for them to persist. The paper puts a practical bound on how many fit: the context window holds 2,048 tokens, which "typically fits 10 to 100 examples".

Now the measurements. Take two-digit addition, asked in words, on the 175-billion model.

Accuracy on two-digit addition and on SAT-style analogy questions, for the same 175-billion model in three prompt formats. Both from the paper's own tables.
Prompt formatTwo-digit additionSAT analogies
Zero-shot76.9 per cent53.7 per cent
One-shot99.6 per cent59.1 per cent
Few-shot100.0 per cent65.2 per cent

Read the first column again. Showing the model one worked sum takes it from 76.9 per cent to 99.6 per cent, and not one number inside the model was altered. The analogy column is the same effect on a task that has nothing to do with arithmetic, and the paper notes that the few-shot score of 65.2 per cent sits above the 57 per cent average of the human college applicants the original questions were written for.

4. Why this is genuinely strange

In every other sense of the word, learning means a change that persists. A model learns during training because gradients move its weights, and the moved weights are still there tomorrow.

Here nothing moves. The model reads examples, does better, and finishes the forward pass with exactly the parameters it started with. Ask it the same question tomorrow without the examples and you are back to 76.9 per cent. The improvement was real, measurable, and left no trace anywhere.

The name given to this is in-context learning, and the honest position is that the name is a description of a behaviour rather than an explanation of it. Something in the forward pass is using the examples. The paper reports the effect, reports that it grows with model size, and does not claim to know why, and neither does this lesson. If you want the tables in full, the papers course lesson on GPT-3 has them.

The reason the effect looked important rather than cute is that it strengthened with scale. A trick that works at 175 billion and not at 1.3 billion looks like a property of size, which meant it was expected to keep improving, which fed straight back into the argument for spending more.

5. Where it worked and where it did not

The paper is unusually good about reporting its failures, and a reader who takes only the successes away has been misled by their own summarising rather than by the authors.

Selected results for the 175-billion model, with what each was measured against. All from the paper.
TaskFew-shot resultAgainst
TriviaQA71.2 per centState of the art among fine-tuned closed-book systems
CoQA85.0 F1Competitive with fine-tuned systems
Two-digit multiplication29.2 per centTrivially solved by a calculator
Five-digit addition9.3 per centTrivially solved by a calculator
Word-in-context, WiC49.4 per cent76.1 for the best fine-tuned system, and 50 for a coin

That last row deserves the emphasis it gets in the paper. A model that had just matched the state of the art on a knowledge benchmark performed at chance on deciding whether one word means the same thing in two sentences. The authors also name a whole family of tasks the approach struggles with, "natural language inference tasks like the ANLI dataset, and some reading comprehension datasets like RACE or QuAC".

And one admission belongs in every account of this era, because it is a fact about economics rather than about models. The authors tried to remove overlaps between their training data and the benchmarks they were reporting, and: "Unfortunately, a bug in the filtering caused us to ignore some overlaps, and due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model." At 3.14 × 1023 operations, you get one attempt. A known bug in the evaluation setup was documented rather than fixed, because fixing it meant paying for the entire run again.

6. The commercial turn

Two weeks after the paper, on 11 June 2020, OpenAI announced something with no precedent in this story: the model would be available through a paid programming interface, and not as a download.

The post asks the obvious question of itself, and gives three reasons, all of which should be reported because taking only one of them makes the account dishonest in one direction or the other.

First, money. "Commercialising the technology helps us pay for our ongoing AI research, safety, and policy efforts." A run at this cost has to be paid for by something.

Second, access. The models "are very large, taking a lot of expertise to develop and deploy and making them very expensive to run", which "makes it hard for anyone except larger companies to benefit". An interface lets a small organisation use a model it could never host. This argument is easy to be cynical about and is straightforwardly true.

Third, control. It is "inherently safer to release them via an API and broaden access over time, rather than release an open source model where access cannot be adjusted if it turns out to have harmful applications". This is the argument from The model they would not release, grown into an operating model. And it comes with an operational commitment: the post states that access will be terminated for use cases causing harm, listing harassment, intentional deception, radicalisation, astroturfing and spam. That is a thing you can do with an interface and cannot do with a file somebody has downloaded.

What that changed is the whole later shape of this course. A model that exists only behind an interface cannot be inspected by outsiders, cannot be run offline, and can be altered or withdrawn without notice, so every claim about it becomes a claim about a service rather than about an artefact. It also created the vacuum that the open-weights movement grew into, which The leak that started an ecosystem takes up.

And the immediate technical debt was left unpaid. Every model in this lesson was sized by the rule from January 2020, and that rule was about to be shown to have held the wrong thing fixed. Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint collects it.

A behaviour, not a mechanism

In-context learning names something that was measured. It is not a claim that the model is running an optimiser inside its forward pass, or building a temporary model of the task, or anything else. Several such explanations have been proposed since and none is settled, so this lesson reports the effect and its size and stops there.

Retrieval practice

What is the difference between zero-shot and few-shot in this paper?
Both settings use one identical frozen model, and the only thing that changes is the text placed in front of the question.
Why is the jump from 76.9 to 99.6 per cent surprising?
The examples are read during one forward pass and are gone afterwards, so the improvement has to be paid for again in every request.
What is the correct thing to say about in-context learning?
The paper reports the effect and how it grows with size, and proposes no mechanism, so neither should a summary of it.
Why did OpenAI say it shipped an interface rather than the weights?
The post gives all three reasons, and quoting only the first or only the third misrepresents the argument in opposite directions.
