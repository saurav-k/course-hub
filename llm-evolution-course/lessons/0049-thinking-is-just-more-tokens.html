Deep dive · The reasoning era · Lesson 0049

Thinking is just more tokens

A reasoning model does not think in any new sense: it writes a region of working tokens before it answers, which buys both more computation per question and an external place to keep intermediate results.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Products began advertising models that think. This lesson says exactly what that means mechanically, and it exists partly to kill a metaphor. It assumes you know that a model generates one token at a time, and it builds on Chain of thought: prompting becomes a lever.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Chain of thought: prompting becomes a lever Asking a large model to write out its working before answering improves its accuracy on multi-step problems, which means the prompt is not just a request but a control on how much computation the model spends.

The one tight idea

A reasoning model does not think in any new sense: it writes a region of working tokens before it answers, which buys both more computation per question and an external place to keep intermediate results.

2. What a thinking model actually emits

From September 2024 onwards, products began to advertise models that think. The word did a great deal of work in the marketing and almost none in the machine, so start with what is literally emitted.

A model of this kind predicts one token at a time, exactly as before. What changed is what it has been trained to predict first. Before writing anything addressed to the user, it writes a long stretch of tokens for itself, and that stretch is marked off by ordinary tokens acting as delimiters. The DeepSeek-R1 report, published on 22 January 2025, gives its training template in full, and the relevant sentence is worth quoting exactly because it removes any remaining mystery: the assistant first thinks about the reasoning process in the mind and then provides the user with the answer, and the reasoning process and answer are enclosed within <think> </think> and <answer> </answer> tags respectively.

That is the mechanism. <think> is a token. There is no separate module, no pause, no second system. The same next-token prediction that writes the answer writes the working, and the delimiters are how the harness knows which is which.

Whether the reader gets to see that region is a product decision rather than a technical one, and it has moved twice. When these models first appeared commercially in September 2024, the working was hidden: third-party reporting from the day of the launch describes reasoning tokens as tokens that are not visible in the API response but are still billed and counted as output tokens, with a suggested budget of around 25,000 of them for prompts that benefit. That single design decision is worth pausing on, because it is the moment computation at answer time became a metered product. Four months later the weights and the visible traces of a model of this kind were published openly, and everyone could read what one writes to itself. By 2026 the traces at the very top of the market are closed again: one vendor's documentation for its 2026 flagships, read on 22 August 2026, states that the raw chain of thought is never returned, that a caller may have a readable summary or nothing in its place, and that thinking cannot be switched off at all. Hidden, then open, then hidden again above a certain price: the mechanism did not change across any of those three moves.

3. Why more tokens is literally more computation

Here is the part that makes "thinking" a reasonable name for something quite unglamorous.

A Transformer performs a fixed amount of computation to produce one token. Fixed. There is no mechanism by which a hard question causes more layers to run than an easy one. The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked describes the stack of identical blocks that produces that fixed cost, and nothing in it looks at the difficulty of the question.

So for a model that answers immediately, the computation available to solve a problem is one token's worth, then another token's worth for the next word of the answer, and so on. If the problem needs more thought than that per step, there is nowhere for the extra thought to go.

Now let the model emit five thousand tokens before its answer. Each of those tokens costs a full pass through the stack, so the model has now spent five thousand passes on this question instead of a handful. That is the entire trick. Depth was fixed at design time and cannot be changed at answer time, so the field bought more computation in the only currency available, which is length.

It is worth being clear that this is a genuine mechanism and not a metaphor. The extra computation is real, it appears on the bill, and it is the reason a question that used to cost a fraction of a penny can now cost noticeably more when the same model is asked to work through it.

4. The working is also a place to put things

There is a second benefit, separate from the first, and confusing the two is the commonest mistake made about these models.

The tokens the model writes go into its own context, which means it can read them back. A partial result written down at step forty is available to be looked at again at step four hundred, without having to be held in whatever internal state a single forward pass carries. That is an external memory, made of text, that the model builds for itself as it goes.

This is why the working looks like working. Restating the problem, listing what is known, computing an intermediate value and labelling it, noticing that a value contradicts an earlier one: these are all things that are useful precisely because they persist in a form the model can attend to later. Chain of thought: prompting becomes a lever is where this course covers the 2022 discovery that you could get this behaviour by simply asking for it. The models discussed here were trained to do it rather than asked, and the difference in reliability is large.

The two benefits are separable and it is worth keeping them apart. More tokens buys more computation even if none of them says anything useful. Useful tokens additionally buy a durable record. Real gains are a mixture of the two, and no published work this course could find cleanly separates their contributions.

5. The clearest demonstration: refuse to let it stop

If the account above is right, then holding everything else fixed and changing only how many tokens the model is allowed to emit should change how often it is right. That experiment was published on 31 January 2025 by a group at Stanford and the Allen Institute, and it is unusually clean.

They fine-tuned an ordinary 32-billion-parameter instruction model on one thousand carefully chosen questions with their worked solutions. Then they added an intervention they call budget forcing, which has two halves. To spend less, cut the working off and make the model answer. To spend more, watch for the token that would end the working region, suppress it, and append the word "Wait" instead, so the model carries on from where it was about to stop.

Nothing else changes. Not the weights, not the prompt, not the question. The only variable is whether the model is permitted to stop when it wanted to. Their reported result is that suppressing the stop two, four and six times raised accuracy on the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination questions from 50 per cent to 57 per cent, and they observe that the extra tokens often go into double-checking an answer and fixing an incorrect step.

The same paper reports that this simple recipe was enough for its model to exceed a leading commercial reasoning model on competition mathematics by up to 27 per cent, which was a considerable embarrassment for the assumption that the capability required elaborate machinery.

6. And then it flattens

The honest half of that result is in the same paper, and it is routinely left out of summaries.

The gains do not continue. The authors report that the accuracy curve eventually flattens at six repetitions of the intervention, and they say why: suppressing the end-of-working token too often can drive the model into repetitive loops rather than into further reasoning. Their general statement about the methods they studied is that these approaches eventually flatten out, or else fail to scale further because of control problems or because the context window runs out.

Take that seriously, because it is the difference between a technique and a magic wand. Spending more at answer time is a real lever with a real ceiling, the ceiling arrives sooner than the enthusiasm did, and running into it looks like a model repeating itself rather than like a model failing. A related result from August 2024 puts the same boundary a different way: spending answer-time computation instead of parameters wins on problems the smaller model already sometimes solves, and that qualification is part of the finding rather than a hedge attached to it.

7. What the trace is not

This lesson exists partly to refuse a claim, and the refusal belongs in the body rather than in a footnote.

The working is not an explanation of how the answer was produced. It is a sequence of tokens that was selected for correlating with correct final answers. Nothing in the training makes it a truthful report of what actually drove the output, and there is direct evidence that it is often not one.

A study published in May 2023 planted a bias in the prompt, for instance by reordering multiple-choice options so that the answer was always the first one, and found that models systematically failed to mention that bias in their written reasoning while nonetheless following it, producing fluent justifications for answers the bias had chosen. On a suite of thirteen tasks, biasing the models towards wrong answers dropped accuracy by as much as 36 per cent, with the written reasoning rationalising the wrong answers rather than revealing why they were given.

That result predates the trained reasoning models, so it was reasonable to hope the newer ones would be better. A study published in May 2025 tested exactly that on current reasoning models, using six kinds of hint placed in the prompt. Its finding is that when a model used a hint, it mentioned having used it in fewer than one in five cases in most settings tested; that outcome-based reinforcement learning improved this at first and then stopped improving it; and that when reinforcement learning increased how often models exploited a flaw in the reward, their willingness to say so in the trace did not increase with it.

So hold two things at once, and this is the sentence to carry away from the lesson. The written working is a computational scratchpad that demonstrably helps, and it is not an account you can trust of why the answer came out as it did. Reading a trace tells you what the model wrote. It does not tell you what the model did.

That distinction matters more than it might seem, because the training method that produced these models rewards only the final answer. Nothing in it ever asked the working to be true. Rewards a machine can check is where this course covers that training method and what else follows from it.

Retrieval practice

Mechanically, what does a model do differently when it is said to think?
The delimiters around the working region are ordinary tokens, and the same next-token prediction writes both the working and the answer.
Why does writing more tokens amount to more computation per question?
Nothing in a stack of identical blocks makes a hard question run more layers than an easy one, so extra computation has to be bought as extra tokens.
What did the budget-forcing experiment establish, and what did it also show?
Suppressing the stop token and appending one word moved AIME24 accuracy from 50 to 57 per cent, and beyond six interventions the model tended into repetitive loops.
What can you conclude from reading a model's written working?
Models given a planted hint mentioned using it in well under a fifth of cases while still following it, so the trace is a scratchpad that helps rather than a record you can trust.
