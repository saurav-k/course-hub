Teaching a model to be useful

A model trained only to continue text is not trying to answer you, and the work of 2021 and 2022 was inventing a training stage whose whole job is to make it try.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.

Chapter 6 of 10. Reading the spine on its own takes an evening. Next chapter: When the record closed.

Where this lesson starts

The scaling bet left a model that could predict text extremely well and was still awkward to use. This chapter explains the gap between predicting text and answering a question, and the three-stage recipe that closed it.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The scaling bet Between 2018 and 2022 the field discovered that the loss of a language model falls along a straight line as you spend more, which turned model building from a craft into a budgeting exercise, and then discovered the first version of that line was wrong.

The one tight idea

A model trained only to continue text is not trying to answer you, and the work of 2021 and 2022 was inventing a training stage whose whole job is to make it try.

2. Predicting text is not answering you

Ask a model that has only ever been trained to continue text "What is the capital of France?" and a perfectly reasonable continuation is "What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Italy?" On the internet, a question is very often followed by more questions. The model is not broken and it is not being unhelpful. It is doing exactly what it was trained to do, and it was never trained to answer anybody.

The OpenAI team that fixed this stated the diagnosis in the first line of their paper: making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent. Scale, which The scaling bet shows had been the field's whole strategy since 2019, does not close this particular gap, because the gap is not a shortage of capability. It is a mismatch between what the model was trained to do and what you want from it.

You cannot simply train it properly instead, and the reason is worth sitting with. Ordinary training needs a loss: a number saying how wrong each output was. For next-word prediction that is easy, because you know what the real next word was. For "be helpful" there is no right answer to compare against. Helpfulness is not a string.

The route round that was invented in a completely different setting, and on a date worth noticing. On 12 June 2017, the same day the Transformer went up, Paul Christiano, Jan Leike and colleagues at OpenAI and DeepMind published a method for training agents to play games and control simulated robots without anyone writing a reward function. Show a person two short clips of the agent, ask which is better, fit a model to their choices, and let the agent optimise against that. One of those two papers made models fast. The other made them listen to people. Everything in this chapter is the second one arriving in language, five years later.

One finding from that 2017 paper survives into every system since. They tried asking people for absolute scores and found it hard for humans to give consistent fine-grained quantitative judgements, while comparisons worked almost as well and were far more reliable. People are bad at scoring and good at comparing. That single observation is why everything below asks for rankings rather than ratings.

3. Stage one: show, do not tell

The recipe that closed the gap has three stages, and the first is the obvious one.

Hire people. Give them real prompts of the kind users actually send. Have them write the answer they wish the model had given. Now you have pairs of a prompt and a good answer, and you can train in the ordinary way, because a good answer is a string you can compare against. This is called supervised fine-tuning.

It works, and it does not go far on its own. Writing a good answer is slow, so you never get many. It also teaches from exactly one answer per prompt, when what you want the model to learn is the difference between a better answer and a worse one. OpenAI's team used about thirteen thousand prompts for this stage, which is a strikingly small number for something that changed how a technology behaves.

4. Stage two: build a machine that has taste

The second stage converts helpfulness into a number, which is the trick the whole thing turns on.

Instead of asking people to write answers, take the answers the model already produces, show a person several of them for the same prompt, and ask them to put them in order. Ranking is much faster than writing and much more reliable, for the reason established in 2017.

Then train a second network on those rankings. Its only job is to look at a prompt and an answer and output a single number: how much a person would have liked this. It is called a reward model, and it is trained from the language model itself, with the part that predicts words replaced by a part that predicts one score, because judging text well requires understanding text.

Two details from the primary paper are worth keeping because they are counter-intuitive. The reward model was smaller than the model being trained: 6 billion parameters judging a 175 billion parameter policy. OpenAI wrote that this saved a great deal of computation and that training a 175 billion parameter judge was unstable. And the people doing the ranking were about forty contractors, hired through two platforms and selected by a screening test. It is worth saying out loud that the behaviour of what became the most widely used software of its kind was shaped by the judgements of roughly forty people, and that this is stated in the paper rather than being a rumour.

5. Stage three: practise, on a short leash

Now that helpfulness is a number, you can optimise it. The model writes an answer, the scorer scores it, and the model adjusts to score higher next time. That is reinforcement learning, and the specific algorithm used in 2022 was one called PPO.

There is an obvious failure waiting here. The scorer is a model of human taste, not human taste itself, and it has blind spots. Left alone, a policy optimising against it will find those blind spots and exploit them, producing text that scores brilliantly and reads like nonsense.

The fix is a penalty for drifting. Every step the model takes away from where it started costs it some reward, so it can still move, but the move has to be worth it. The reason this works is more interesting than "stay close to the original". The scorer was trained on answers sampled from the original model. Once the policy wanders far from that region, the scorer is being asked to judge text of a kind it has never seen, and its opinions out there are meaningless. The leash is not there to keep the model humble. It is there to keep the model inside the region where its own reward signal is still trustworthy.

The headline result is the one to remember. A 1.3 billion parameter model put through this recipe produced answers that labellers preferred to those of the 175 billion parameter model it was built from. A hundredfold size difference, beaten by three stages of preference training.

And the cost is the reason every laboratory adopted this within months. Pretraining the large model took about 3,640 petaflop-days. The supervised stage took about 4.9, and the reinforcement stage about 60. The alignment work is under two per cent of the pretraining bill, and OpenAI drew the obvious conclusion in the paper: increasing investment in aligning existing models is more cost-effective than training larger ones.

One honest complication. OpenAI reported that this training slightly degraded performance on standard academic benchmarks, coined the phrase "alignment tax" for that, and engineered most of it away by mixing ordinary pretraining back into the reinforcement stage. Three weeks later Anthropic reported that at their scale alignment training improved performance on almost all evaluations, so they did not observe the tax at all. Both are published measurements and they disagree. This is a live empirical question rather than a settled fact, and a course that picks a side is misreporting it.

6. The prompt turns out to be a lever

A separate discovery in the same period changed what a prompt is for.

In January 2022 Jason Wei and colleagues at Google reported that if you ask a large model to write out its working before giving an answer, its accuracy on multi-step problems improves substantially. Not because the model learned anything. Because a model does a fixed amount of computation per piece of text it emits, so a model that writes two hundred words of working before answering has done far more computation on your problem than one that answers immediately, and it has an external place to keep intermediate results where it can see them.

That reframes the prompt. It is not only a request. It is a control on how much thinking the model spends. Nobody made much of that at the time, and by 2025 it had become an entire paradigm; The reasoning turn, and where we are is where that lands.

7. A text box changes everything

By January 2022 the preference-trained models were the default behind OpenAI's paid interface, and an early version had been deployed there a year before that. The capability existed. Almost nobody outside the developer world had ever touched it, because reaching it required an account, a key and a willingness to read documentation.

On 30 November 2022 OpenAI put a free web page in front of it.

What was technically new was small, and OpenAI said so plainly. Their announcement describes the model as a sibling of the preference-trained one, trained using the same methods with slight differences in the data collection setup, and fine-tuned from a model in an existing series that had finished training earlier that year. The main change was the shape of the training data: instead of one instruction and one answer, the people writing demonstrations wrote whole conversations, playing both the user and the assistant. That is where the behaviours everyone found astonishing come from, the following up and the referring back, because those are properties of dialogue data rather than of a new mechanism.

Nothing else about it was disclosed. The size of the model behind that launch, its training data and the number of people who shaped it have never been published, and this chapter will not guess. Note the date at which that silence begins, because it is the subject of When the record closed.

Five days later the interface had a million users.

The lesson to take from November 2022 is uncomfortable and important. Every ingredient existed already: the architecture from 2017, decoder-only pretraining from 2018, scale from 2020, preference training from 2017 through 2022, and instruction following in a paid product since January 2021. What was missing was a free text box and no waiting list. The last step in the chain was a distribution event, not a research one, and a course that treats it as a scientific breakthrough misdates the entire era.

It also changed the incentives permanently. A research artefact became a product with enormous commercial value, and within four months the papers stopped saying how any of it was built. When the record closed is about what that did to the evidence, and about how to read this field once the primary sources became marketing.

8. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: Teaching a model to answer A model trained to continue text is not trying to be helpful, so you add a stage whose only job is preference: show it demonstrations, learn a stand-in for human taste, and let it practise against that on a short leash.
  • Deep dive: Chain of thought: prompting becomes a lever Asking a large model to write out its working before answering improves its accuracy on multi-step problems, which means the prompt is not just a request but a control on how much computation the model spends.
  • Deep dive: A text box changes everything The breakthrough of late 2022 was distribution rather than research: an existing recipe, tuned for conversation, put behind a free text box that anyone could use without reading anything first.

Retrieval practice

Why can you not train helpfulness the ordinary way?
Next-word prediction has a known target and helpfulness has none, so the recipe first has to turn human preference into a number.
Why are people asked to rank answers rather than score them?
The 2017 preference paper found humans could not give consistent fine-grained scores, while comparisons were nearly as informative and far more reliable.
Why is there a penalty for drifting from the starting model?
The leash keeps the policy inside the region where its own reward signal is still valid, which is what prevents it exploiting the scorer's blind spots.
What was actually new about the launch of November 2022?
OpenAI describes it as a sibling model using the same methods; the change of substance was dialogue-shaped data behind an interface with no waiting list.
