The other problem: attention did not make it fast

Attention fixed how far a model could reach, but the recurrence underneath it still forced one step per word, so training time grew with sentence length no matter how many machines you had.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Attention, the subject of Attention: stop compressing, solved reach. This lesson is about the constraint that was left, and it is the reason the Transformer exists. It assumes you know what attention computes.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Attention: stop compressing Do not squeeze the source sentence into one vector at all: keep every position, and at each output step learn a weighting that says which positions matter now.

The one tight idea

Attention fixed how far a model could reach, but the recurrence underneath it still forced one step per word, so training time grew with sentence length no matter how many machines you had.

2. Two different questions about a sequence model

By the end of 2015 the field had a translation architecture that worked: an encoder and a decoder, both recurrent networks, with attention bolted between them so that every output position could draw on every input position. Attention: stop compressing is the story of how that came about, and it closes a question that had been open since the first neural translation systems.

The question it closed is reach. Can information from the first word of a sentence get to the last word of the translation without being crushed? With attention, yes, and it can do so from any position to any position.

There is a second question, and attention does not touch it. How long does it take? Not how many operations in total, which is a different thing, but how many of those operations have to happen one after another rather than all at once.

That second question turns out to be the one that decides what gets built, and the reason is the hardware. A graphics card is wide rather than quick. It wins by doing thousands of independent small operations at the same instant, which is the argument 2012: the year the hardware caught up makes about why deep learning became practical at all. Work that must happen in a strict order cannot use that width. A model that insists on finishing word nine before it starts word ten is a model that leaves nearly all of a very expensive machine idle.

3. The dependency, stated concretely

A recurrent network reads a sequence by carrying a running state. To produce the state after word ten, it applies its little network to the state after word nine together with word ten. So the state after word nine must exist first. That is not a performance characteristic that better engineering can remove. It is what recurrence means.

The Transformer paper names the cost in one sentence: this inherently sequential nature precludes parallelisation within training examples, which becomes critical at longer sequence lengths, because memory limits how many examples you can batch together instead.

Read the second half of that sentence carefully, because it is the part people skip. There is an obvious escape: if you cannot run the words of one sentence at the same time, run many sentences at the same time. That works, and it is what everyone did. It stops working exactly when sentences get long, because a long sentence uses more memory, so fewer of them fit on the card at once, so there is less independent work available precisely when you need more of it. The two problems are the same problem, and they compound.

There is a measurement of this sitting in plain sight in the sequence-to-sequence paper of September 2014. Its authors report that their model ran at about 1,700 words per second on a single graphics card, which was too slow, so they spread it across a machine with eight cards, one layer per card and the remaining four cards splitting the output layer. The result was 6,300 words per second.

Eight times the hardware, 3.7 times the speed. That is what the sequential dependency costs, measured by the people who paid it, and even with all eight cards busy their training run still took about ten days.

4. Path length, the other quantity worth tracking

Sequential steps are about time. There is a second quantity, about learning, and the two get confused because they often have the same value.

Path length is the number of operations that information has to pass through to get from one position to another. It matters because of how these networks are trained: a correction signal travels backwards along that same path, and it gets weaker at every step it passes through. Backpropagation, and the two winters is the lesson on why that happens; the short version is that a signal multiplied by a number smaller than one, thirty times over, arrives as nothing. The shorter the path between two positions, the easier it is for the model to learn a relationship between them.

In a recurrent layer, information from word one reaches word thirty by passing through twenty-nine intermediate states. In a convolutional layer, which looks at a small window of neighbouring positions at once, you cover more ground per layer but you have to stack layers to reach across a sentence, so the path grows with the depth of the stack. In an attention layer, every position is compared with every other position directly, so the path from any word to any other word is one operation, regardless of how far apart they are.

Two columns, one idea. A recurrent layer needs n steps in order and a signal travels n hops. A self-attention layer needs one step and a signal travels one hop, from any position to any other. That is the entire argument for the architecture that arrives in June 2017.

The price is in the first column, and it is worth stating now because the whole next decade is spent paying it: self-attention's total work grows with the square of the sentence length, because every position is compared with every position. The paper is straightforward about the trade: self-attention is cheaper than recurrence only while the sentence is shorter than the vector size, which was comfortably true of the sentences it was translating and stopped being true the moment anyone wanted to read a document.

5. What it was costing by 2017

The clearest evidence that this had become the binding constraint is what the state of the art cost to move.

In March 2017 Denny Britz, Anna Goldie, Minh-Thang Luong and Quoc Le at Google published a study whose entire purpose was to establish which design choices in a recurrent translation model actually mattered, because nobody knew: results were reported on different data with different tuning, and could not be compared. Their opening diagnosis names the constraint outright, saying that these architectures are expensive to train, typically requiring days to weeks of graphics-card time to converge, which makes an exhaustive search over design choices prohibitively expensive. Their answer required, by their own account, over 250,000 graphics-card hours, all on the standard English-to-German translation task.

That number is the field admitting something. A quarter of a million card-hours to tune an architecture, rather than to invent one, is not a sustainable way to make progress, and it was the direct consequence of every experiment being slow for structural reasons.

6. Three escapes, tried in eighteen months

Between late 2016 and mid-2017 three different routes out of recurrence were attempted, by three different groups, and all three are in the record.

Convolutions

A convolutional layer looks at a fixed window of neighbouring positions and can compute every window at the same time, so it has no sequential dependency at all. Nal Kalchbrenner and colleagues at DeepMind posted ByteNet on 31 October 2016, using dilated convolutions to translate in linear time. Jonas Gehring, Michael Auli, David Grangier, Denis Yarats and Yann Dauphin at Facebook AI Research posted convolutional sequence to sequence learning on 8 May 2017, and it beat Google's production translation system, in their words, at an order of magnitude faster speed.

Five weeks before the attention answer, the convolutional answer was working and winning. What it could not fix is the third column of the table: a convolution sees a window, so reaching across a long sentence still requires stacking layers, and the path between distant words still grows.

Conditional computation

On 23 January 2017 Noam Shazeer, with Azalia Mirhoseini, Krzysztof Maziarz, Andy Davis, Quoc Le, Geoffrey Hinton and Jeff Dean, posted a layer containing thousands of small networks and a router that sends each example to only a few of them. It reached 137 billion parameters while doing only a small fraction of that much arithmetic per example.

Note what this attacks and what it does not. It decouples how much a model knows from how much it computes, which is a real and lasting idea. It does not remove the sequential dependency, because in that paper those layers sit between stacked recurrent layers, which are still recurrent. This is the ancestor of the sparse models that make the 2024 efficiency turn possible, and this course takes that up properly in Mixture of experts, part one: the router rather than teaching routing twice. What matters here is the date and the name: Shazeer is on this paper five months before he is on the Transformer.

Attention with nothing underneath it

On 6 June 2016, a year before anything else in this section, Ankur Parikh, Oscar Tackstrom, Dipanjan Das and Jakob Uszkoreit at Google published a model for deciding whether one sentence implies another. It uses attention to break the problem into pieces that can be solved separately, which makes it, in their words, trivially parallelisable. It has no recurrence and no convolution. It reached the state of the art on its benchmark with almost an order of magnitude fewer parameters than the previous best, and it did so, again in their words, without relying on any word-order information at all.

That last clause is doing two jobs. It is a boast, and it is a warning: a model built only from attention genuinely does not know what order its input is in, which is a problem somebody will have to solve.

Jakob Uszkoreit is the person who, a year later, proposed replacing recurrent networks with self-attention. The Transformer paper says so in its own author-contribution footnote. This 2016 paper is the missing link between the two, and it is the reason the idea did not come from nowhere.

7. The question this leaves open

So, by the first half of 2017, the position was this.

Attention could reach anywhere in a sentence in one hop, and it was bolted on top of a mechanism that insisted on walking through the sentence one word at a time. Two of the three routes out kept the walking. The third abandoned it entirely and worked, on one task, with the admission that the resulting model had no idea what order its words were in.

The obvious question had been asked and not yet answered at scale: if attention already connects every position to every other position, what exactly is the recurrence still for? Could you keep the attention, delete the sequential step, and add the word order back some other way?

That is the question, and this lesson deliberately stops at it. The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked is where it is answered, by eight people at Google, in a paper posted on 12 June 2017.

Retrieval practice

Why does recurrence waste a graphics card?
Computing the state after word ten requires the state after word nine, so nearly all of the card's width has no work available to it.
What does maximum path length measure, and why does it matter?
A signal weakens at every step it passes through, so a shorter path makes a long-range relationship easier to learn.
Why were convolutions not the answer, given that they parallelise?
A convolution sees a window, so reaching across a sentence means stacking layers, and the path between distant words grows with the depth.
What did the 2016 attention-only model give up in exchange for being parallel?
Its authors advertised this as a strength, and it is also the one objection the 2017 architecture had to answer.
