Sparsity moves to attention

Once the feed-forward half of the block is cheap, attention is what is left paying the quadratic bill, so the same selective trick was pointed at attention itself.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.
Where this lesson starts

Mixture of experts, part one: the router and Mixture of experts, part two: why it is hard make the feed-forward half of the block sparse. This lesson is about where the cost went next. It assumes you know both that attention grows with the square of the length and what a router does.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Mixture of experts, part two: why it is hard A router that is trained only to be useful will send almost everything to a few experts, so every practical system needs something that stops the router eating itself, and three generations of that fix are the real story.
  • Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs Generating a token requires re-reading everything the model has already seen, so serving is a memory problem before it is an arithmetic problem, and most of the engineering since 2022 is about that memory.

The one tight idea

Once the feed-forward half of the block is cheap, attention is what is left paying the quadratic bill, so the same selective trick was pointed at attention itself.

2. What is left after the cheap half is cheap

A Transformer block has two halves. Self-attention lets every position look at every other position, and a feed-forward network then processes each position on its own. Mixture of experts, part one: the router describes how the second half was made sparse: replace the one feed-forward network with many small ones and run only a few per token, so a model can hold hundreds of billions of parameters while computing with tens of billions.

That worked, and the consequence is arithmetic. The two halves scale differently with the length of the text. A feed-forward network costs the same for every token regardless of how much came before it, so its bill grows in proportion to the length. Attention compares each position with every earlier position, so its bill grows with the square of the length. Sparsity cut a constant off the term that grows slowly, and left the term that grows quickly untouched.

Put a number on it. At a context of one thousand tokens, the last position compares itself with one thousand keys. At one million tokens, it compares itself with one million. Across a whole one-million-token prompt that is roughly five hundred billion comparisons per attention head per layer, and the model has many heads and many layers. Meanwhile the feed-forward half, sparse or not, has done the same fixed amount of work for each of those million tokens and no more.

So the crossover point moved. In 2017, translating a thirty-word sentence, attention was the cheap part and the feed-forward network was the expensive one, which is exactly the trade The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked describes the original paper making. By 2025, with agents wanting to read whole repositories and long transcripts, and with the feed-forward half made sparse, attention was the bill. The tidy way to hold the last few years is that 2024 was the year sparsity conquered the feed-forward network and 2026 is the year it reached attention: the same idea, pointed at the other half of the block.

3. The shape of the fix: look cheaply, then attend properly

The dominant answer in 2025 and 2026 is a two-stage attention step, and its shape is the same wherever it appears.

Stage one is a cheap indexer: a small, deliberately lightweight scorer that looks at all the earlier positions and produces a rough judgement of which ones might matter to this query. Stage two is ordinary, exact attention run over only the shortlist the indexer returned. Nothing is approximated in stage two. The approximation is entirely in the claim that the positions left off the shortlist did not matter.

The clearest production-scale example is DeepSeek Sparse Attention, introduced with DeepSeek-V3.2 on 2 December 2025. A third-party paper describes it compactly: a lightweight lightning indexer selects the top-k most relevant tokens per query, reducing core attention from work that grows with the square of the length to work that grows with the length multiplied by k.

The whole idea then reduces to one integer, and that integer is written down in public. These models ship their configuration files alongside their weights, and the field is called index_topk. Read on 22 August 2026, DeepSeek-V4-Pro sets it to 1,024, DeepSeek-V4-Flash sets it to 512, and both DeepSeek-V3.2 and GLM-5.2 set it to 2,048. DeepSeek-V4-Pro also declares a maximum position of 1,048,576.

Read those two numbers together, because their ratio is the lesson. At a one-million-token context, a query in that model attends fully and exactly to 1,024 keys, and does not attend to the other 1,047,552 at all. Everything else about the layer is unchanged. This is a claim any reader can check for themselves in a way that a benchmark score is not, and it is the cheapest demonstration in this course of why open weights make claims verifiable.

4. Why the selector is harder here than it was for experts

Both halves of the block now have a small learned component choosing what to compute. They are not the same difficulty, for four reasons worth separating.

The candidate set is not fixed. A router chooses among a set number of experts, known when the model is designed, and that number does not change while the model runs. An indexer chooses among every position seen so far, so its candidate set grows without limit during a single request.

A mistake is a different kind of mistake. If a router picks a mediocre expert, the token still gets processed and the answer is a little worse. If an indexer omits a key, the information in that position is not merely down-weighted, it is absent, and the omitted position may be the one holding the answer.

The selector's own cost is the remaining problem. To decide which thousand positions matter, the indexer has to look at all million, so the indexer itself still does work that grows with the square of the length. The March 2026 paper that named this most clearly says so directly: the indexer retains quadratic complexity and must run independently at every layer. This is a good general lesson about optimisation, and worth carrying beyond this page: a fix of this kind usually relocates a cost rather than deleting it.

Choosing the top few is not differentiable. Keeping the highest thousand scores and discarding the rest is a hard cut, so there is no smooth path along which the indexer can learn from the final error the way ordinary weights do. The published approach is to train the indexer against the attention pattern that full attention would have produced, so it learns to imitate a teacher rather than to minimise the model's loss directly.

The third of those has produced the most interesting recent work. IndexCache, published 12 March 2026, observes that the shortlists chosen by neighbouring layers are highly similar, and exploits that: keep real indexers on a small set of layers and let the majority reuse the nearest one's shortlist. On a 30-billion-parameter model using DeepSeek Sparse Attention, its authors report removing 75 per cent of indexer computation with negligible quality degradation, for up to 1.82 times faster prefill and 1.48 times faster decoding.

A second line attacks the granularity instead. MiniMax Sparse Attention, published 11 June 2026, scores blocks of keys and values rather than individual positions, and selects a top-k subset of blocks per group of query heads, which makes the shortlist cheaper to compute and much friendlier to how accelerators actually read memory. On a 109-billion-parameter model its authors report parity with ordinary grouped-query attention while reducing per-token attention computation by 28.4 times at a one-million-token context, and, with their own kernel, 14.2 times faster prefill and 7.6 times faster decoding on H800 hardware. They published the kernel.

5. The other answer: do not use attention for most layers

The second family does not make attention selective. It replaces attention in most layers with something whose cost grows in proportion to the length rather than with its square, and keeps a small number of ordinary full-attention layers to do the work that the cheap mechanism cannot.

The cheap mechanisms in question descend from the state-space line of work, of which Mamba, published 1 December 2023, is the best-known member. The relevant property for this lesson is that such a layer carries a fixed-size running state forward through the sequence instead of keeping every past position available, which makes its cost per token constant and its memory constant, and which also means it cannot look up an arbitrary earlier position on demand. That limitation is exactly what the interleaved full-attention layers are there to cover.

What is notable is that this arrived at the frontier as a majority component rather than as a replacement, and the ratios are declared in the configuration files. Read on 22 August 2026, one flagship sets full_attention_interval to 4, so three cheap layers are followed by one full-attention layer, ninety-two layers deep. Another has ninety-three layers, of which sixty-nine use a linear-cost attention variant and twenty-four are full attention. Neither is willing to give up ordinary attention entirely, and both are willing to use it in roughly a quarter of their layers.

6. What is actually settled here, and what is not

This page sits in the moving zone of the course, and the badge at the top is not decoration. Be precise about which parts of it are which.

Settled enough to teach. That attention became the binding cost once the feed-forward half was made sparse is arithmetic, not opinion. That the shape of the answer is select-then-attend is visible in several independent systems from different labs. That the selection budgets are small integers, and what those integers are, is readable in published configuration files by anyone who wants to check.

Not settled. Every speed-up figure in this lesson is reported by the lab that built the system, measured against a baseline that lab chose, on hardware it chose. MiniMax published its kernel, which makes its numbers more checkable than most, and none of these figures is an independent reproduction. Nor is it settled which family wins. Selective attention and hybrid linear stacks are both shipping at the frontier in 2026, in the same month, from labs with similar resources, and that is usually a sign that the question is open rather than that one side has not noticed the other.

The most important open question is one this lesson has already stated twice: the shortlist is a bet that the omitted positions did not matter. Measuring whether that bet holds is not a question about efficiency at all. It is a question about whether a model can still use what it was given, which is what Long context: capacity is not competence takes up.

Retrieval practice

Why did attention become the binding cost once experts were introduced?
A feed-forward network costs the same per token however long the text is, while attention compares each position with every earlier one, so only one of the two halves gets worse as contexts grow.
In a select-then-attend design, where exactly is the approximation?
Attention over the shortlist is exact, so the entire risk of the method sits in the claim that the keys left out could not have changed the answer.
What is the catch that the indexer itself introduces?
Deciding which thousand positions matter requires scoring all of them, which is why the follow-on work is about reusing one layer's shortlist across its neighbours.
How should a reader treat the speed-up figures in this lesson?
Every figure here comes from the group that built the system, on hardware and against a baseline of its own choosing, and one published kernel makes a number checkable rather than checked.
