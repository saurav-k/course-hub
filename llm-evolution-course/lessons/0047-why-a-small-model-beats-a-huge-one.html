Why a small model now beats a huge one from 2020

A model a fraction of the size now outperforms the largest models of 2020 on most tasks, and almost none of the reason is a better architecture.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson collects causes that the course introduces separately and weighs them against each other. It assumes you have met the corrected scaling rule in Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint and the post-training stages in Teaching a model to answer.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint The first scaling recipe held the wrong thing fixed, so the models built on it were far larger than they needed to be and far less trained than they should have been, and once that was corrected the scarce resource became text.
  • Teaching a model to answer A model trained to continue text is not trying to be helpful, so you add a stage whose only job is preference: show it demonstrations, learn a stand-in for human taste, and let it practise against that on a short leash.

The one tight idea

A model a fraction of the size now outperforms the largest models of 2020 on most tasks, and almost none of the reason is a better architecture.

2. The claim, stated so that it can be checked

"A small model today beats a huge model from 2020" is a slogan until it names a benchmark, a harness and a date. So here is the smallest honest version of it.

GPT-3, published in May 2020 with 175 billion parameters, was evaluated on MMLU, a 57-subject multiple-choice examination, by the benchmark's own authors in September 2020. It scored 43.9 per cent in the few-shot setting. Mistral 7B, whose paper was submitted in October 2023, has 7.3 billion parameters and reports 60.1 per cent on the same benchmark. Phi-3-mini, whose paper was submitted in April 2024, has 3.8 billion parameters and reports 69 per cent.

Now the caveats, because they are half the lesson. Those three numbers were produced by three different groups running three different harnesses, and MMLU scores move by several points depending on how the prompt is formatted and how the answer is extracted, so this is a comparison of three published claims rather than one controlled experiment. The Phi line in particular was challenged: an independent benchmark built in May 2024 to mirror grade-school arithmetic without being in anyone's training data found that the Phi and Mistral families, and some Llama models, showed signs of overfitting to the public test set, while several frontier models did not. Treat a headline score as a claim with an author, not as a measurement of a model.

Even with all of that discounted, the direction is not in doubt, and the size ratio is startling: a model between twenty and fifty times smaller does better on a broad knowledge examination than the model that made the field famous. This lesson is about where that came from. The short answer is that it came from six places, and that a better architecture is the smallest of them.

3. Six causes, none of which is a better Transformer

One: far more training data

GPT-3's own paper states that all its models were trained for a total of 300 billion tokens. Phi-3's paper states that its 3.8-billion-parameter model was trained on 3.3 trillion: eleven times the text, poured into a model forty-six times smaller. Llama 3's paper puts its flagship's corpus at 15.6 trillion, which is fifty-two times what GPT-3 saw. The whole regime moved by more than an order of magnitude. Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint is where this course covers the 2022 result that showed the 2020 models were badly undertrained for their size, and the practice after 2023 went past even that correction into deliberate overtraining, because training is paid once and inference is paid forever.

Two: much better training data

The 2020 recipe was to take a large web crawl, filter it lightly and train. Where the data actually came from covers what those corpora were. What changed afterwards was aggressive deduplication, much harder quality filtering, and, from June 2023 onwards, deliberately generated training text. The Phi line pushed that argument to its limit, arguing that a small model trained on textbook-quality material could match models several times its size, and its 3.8-billion-parameter model was trained on 3.3 trillion heavily filtered and synthetic tokens. That is the line the contamination work above put a question mark against, and both halves of that belong in the reader's head at once.

Three: a tidier block

Four small changes to the Transformer block became standard between 2019 and 2023, and section 4 below says what they are. Each is worth a few per cent. Together they are worth more than that, and much less than the reader expects.

Four: post-training

GPT-3 had none. It was a model trained to continue text, being asked to answer questions, and the few-shot prompting it became famous for was a way of coaxing a continuation into looking like an answer. Every small model in the comparison above has been through supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations and then some form of preference training, which Teaching a model to answer covers. A large share of the usable gap between 2020 and now is here rather than in the base model, which is why the gap looks smaller when you compare raw continuation quality than when you compare answers.

Five: distillation from a much stronger model

This one gets its own section below, because it is the cause most often stated loosely.

Six: being allowed to think before answering

GPT-3 produced an answer in one pass per token with no scratchpad. A small model in 2026 may write thousands of working tokens before its answer, which is more computation spent per question and an external place to keep intermediate results. Thinking is just more tokens is where this course covers the mechanism. The measured version of the claim is from August 2024: in a comparison holding total arithmetic fixed, spending it at answer time rather than on a larger model let a smaller base model outperform one fourteen times its size, on problems where the smaller model already had a non-trivial chance of succeeding. That last clause is a real restriction and is part of the finding.

4. What actually changed inside the block

The 2017 architecture and a 2024 one are recognisably the same design. Four substitutions account for most of the difference, and it is worth naming them precisely so that "architectural improvements" stops being a vague phrase.

Position handled as a rotation. The original scheme added a fixed pattern of numbers to each position's vector before the first block. Rotary position embedding, published in April 2021, instead rotates the query and the key by an angle that depends on their position, so that the score between two positions depends directly on the distance between them. The papers course derives it.

A gated feed-forward network. A 2020 paper tested variants of the gated linear unit in place of the ordinary nonlinearity in the block's feed-forward half and found several of them better. Its conclusion is unusually candid, and worth quoting because it is a fair description of how a lot of this progress happened: "We offer no explanation as to why these architectures seem to work; we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence."

A cheaper normalisation. RMSNorm, published in October 2019, drops the re-centring step from layer normalisation on the hypothesis that it is dispensable, and reports comparable quality with running-time reductions between 7 and 64 per cent depending on the model.

Fewer key and value heads. Grouped-query attention, from May 2023, shares each key and value across a group of query heads, which shrinks the memory traffic that Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs shows is what actually limits generation.

Read that list again and notice what kind of list it is. A rotation instead of an addition, a gate instead of a plain nonlinearity, one fewer statistic in the normalisation, fewer copies of a tensor. Not one of them changes what the model is doing. They make it train more stably, run faster and use its context more sensibly, and they are properly called hygiene rather than insight. The architecture that carried the field from 2017 to 2026 is, in its essentials, the one described in The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked, plus a mixture-of-experts feed-forward half in the largest models and a selective attention step in the newest.

5. Distillation, stated carefully

Three different things are called distillation, and a reader who does not separate them will be permanently confused by news about them.

Matching a teacher's full output distribution. The original 2015 idea: instead of training a student on the correct answer alone, train it to match the whole probability distribution a stronger model produced, which carries far more information per example than a single label. This is used as a pre-training objective for the small members of several model families.

Training on a teacher's finished outputs. Generate answers from a strong model and fine-tune a weak one on them as if they were human demonstrations. This is cheap and it works: the DeepSeek-R1 report shows a 32-billion-parameter model fine-tuned on outputs from its own much larger reasoning model reaching 72.6 per cent on the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination questions and 94.3 per cent on MATH-500, and states that the reasoning patterns of the large model can be systematically harnessed to enhance smaller ones.

The student generates and the teacher corrects. A 2026 variant in which the student produces its own attempts and the teacher scores or repairs them, so the student trains on its own trajectories rather than on the teacher's.

The consequence for this lesson is worth stating bluntly. A small model that has been distilled from a frontier model is not a small model that learned all of this by itself. It is a lossy compression of a much larger artefact, and the compute that produced the knowledge inside it was spent on the teacher. Comparing it with GPT-3 as though both were trained from scratch under comparable conditions flatters it.

That is also where the legal question sits, and this course states it rather than settling it. In late January 2025 OpenAI said it had evidence that a competitor had used its model outputs in a way that violated its terms of use; the competitor denied it and said its models were independently trained; no public evidence has resolved the dispute. The framing this course prefers is the unresolved question rather than the verdict: a field that trained on the scraped public internet now has to say what it thinks it owns about the text its own models produce, and it has not said. Four axes of open is where this course takes up what a licence on a model actually controls.

6. How much does each one contribute?

Nobody knows, and it is worth being exact about why nobody knows.

To assign shares to those six causes you would need to train a series of models that differ in one cause at a time: the 2020 recipe with 2024 data, the 2024 recipe with 2020 data, the same base model with and without post-training, and so on, all evaluated on one harness on one day. That is many frontier training runs whose only product is an attribution study, and no lab has published one. What exists instead is a large number of single-variable ablations at small scale, from different groups, on different baselines, which do not add up to a decomposition.

So this lesson gives an ordering rather than a split, and labels it as a judgement. Data volume and data quality are almost certainly the two largest; post-training is the largest single contributor to what a user actually experiences as the difference, because it is the difference between a text continuer and an assistant; distillation matters enormously for the smallest models and not at all for the largest, since there is nothing above the frontier to distil from; answer-time compute is the newest and the one growing fastest; and the block changes are real, cumulative and last.

Do not carry away a number from that paragraph, because there is no number in it. Carry away the shape: the reason a small model is good now is mostly about what it was fed, what was done to it afterwards, and what it is allowed to do at answer time, and only marginally about how it is built.

Retrieval practice

Which of the six causes contributes least to the size gap?
A rotation instead of an addition and a gate instead of a plain nonlinearity make a model train and run better without changing what it does.
Why does calling a small model "distilled" change how you should read a comparison?
A distilled model is a lossy compression of a frontier artefact, so comparing it with a model trained from scratch compares one thing against another thing's compute.
Why does this lesson refuse to give each cause a percentage share?
An attribution would need a series of frontier runs differing in one variable each, evaluated on one harness on one day, and no such study exists.
What makes a published benchmark score a claim rather than a measurement?
The same model on the same benchmark can differ by several points depending on formatting and answer extraction, so a number without its harness is unattributable.
