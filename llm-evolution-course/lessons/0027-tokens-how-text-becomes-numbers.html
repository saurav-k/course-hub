Tokens: how text becomes numbers

A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This course keeps saying that a model reads text. This lesson says exactly what it reads. It assumes nothing beyond being able to follow a table of counts, and it explains a mechanism whose consequences reach the whole rest of the course.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Meaning as a direction in space Give every word a position in a few hundred dimensions, learn those positions only from the company each word keeps, and similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space.

The one tight idea

A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

2. A network reads numbers, and text is not numbers

A neural network is a very large pile of arithmetic. Numbers go in, numbers are multiplied and added, numbers come out. It has no facility for reading a letter, because a letter is not a quantity.

So before any of the machinery in this course can run, something has to convert a piece of writing into a list of whole numbers, and convert the list back afterwards. That converter is called a tokenizer, the pieces it produces are called tokens, and the numbered list of every piece it knows about is called its vocabulary.

The tokenizer is not learned along with the model. It is fixed before the first gradient step, it never changes afterwards, and every model in this course is stuck with whatever choice its authors made. That is why a preprocessing step gets a whole lesson: it is the one decision in the pipeline that cannot be revised by training harder.

3. Why not one number per word

The obvious design is a dictionary. Number every word in English, feed the model the numbers, and let it work.

The problem is that the list is never finished. Someone will write antidisestablishmentarianism, or a URL, or a surname, or a typo, or an emoji, and the dictionary has no number for it. The usual repair was a single catch-all symbol, conventionally written <UNK>, meaning a word was here and we do not know which. Every rare word collapses into the same number, and the information is simply gone.

This was not a small annoyance. Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch opened their 2015 paper on the problem by observing that neural translation systems of the day used a fixed vocabulary while translation is an open-vocabulary task, and that the standard fallback was to look the unknown word up in a dictionary afterwards. Proper nouns, compounds and loanwords are exactly the words a translator most needs to get right, and they are exactly the words a fixed vocabulary does not contain.

4. Why not one number per character

The opposite design never runs out. There are only so many characters, so number those instead.

Now nothing is unknown, and two new problems arrive. The first is length: the becomes three steps rather than one, so every sentence is four or five times longer, and the model must learn spelling before it can learn anything about meaning. The second is cost, and it is the harsher one. Attention, the mechanism at the centre of the Transformer described in The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked, compares every position with every other position, so its work grows with the square of the length. Making sequences five times longer makes that step twenty-five times more expensive.

5. Byte-pair encoding, ten merges by hand

The compromise that won is an algorithm from somewhere else entirely. Byte-pair encoding was published by Philip Gage in 1994 as a data compression technique: repeatedly find the most frequent pair of adjacent bytes in a file and replace it with a single unused byte. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch took that idea and pointed it at words instead, merging characters rather than bytes. Their description of the result is the clearest sentence anyone has written about tokenization: it "effectively interpolates between word level inputs for frequent symbol sequences and character level inputs for infrequent symbol sequences".

Here is the whole algorithm, worked by hand on the four-word corpus from the paper's own Algorithm 1. Every word starts split into single characters, with </w> marking where a word ends, and each word carries how often it occurs.

The starting corpus. Four words, split into characters, with their frequencies.
Word, as charactersTimes it occurs
l o w </w>5

w i d e s t </w>3

Count every adjacent pair across the whole corpus, weighted by how often each word occurs. The pair e followed by s appears six times inside newest and three times inside widest, so it appears nine times in total, and no other pair beats that. Merge it, write the merge down, and count again. Ten rounds of that produce the trace in Figure 1.

The output of training is nothing but that ordered list of merges. To handle a word the algorithm has never seen, split it into characters and apply the ten rules in order. Running the trace above and then tokenizing gives:

Applying the ten learned merges to words the training corpus never contained.
WordHow it comes out
lowestlow + est</w>
lowerlow + e + r + </w>
widerwi + d + e + r + </w>

lowest never appeared in training, and it still comes out as two familiar pieces rather than as an unknown. Nothing is lost, and nothing is guessed. That is what an open vocabulary means, and it is the whole reason the field adopted this.

Notice also what the algorithm did not do. wider splits after wi, which is not a prefix of anything. The pieces are artefacts of counting, not units of language, and it is worth carrying that forward: a token is a statistical convenience and nothing more.

6. The byte-level twist, and why nothing is ever unknown again

Starting from characters solves English. It does not solve writing. There are over a hundred and thirty thousand Unicode characters, and a tokenizer that wanted to guarantee it could represent any of them would have to spend its whole vocabulary on the alphabet before learning a single merge. The GPT-2 paper states the number and the objection plainly: a base vocabulary of over 130,000 is "prohibitively large compared to the 32,000 to 64,000 token vocabularies often used with BPE".

The fix in that paper is one sentence and it is genuinely elegant. Do not start from characters. Start from bytes.

Every piece of digital text, in every script, is stored as a sequence of bytes, and there are exactly 256 possible bytes. So the starting alphabet is finite, tiny and complete. Learn merges on top of it and a common English word still ends up as one token, while a character the tokenizer has never encountered falls back to its two or three raw bytes rather than to an unknown symbol. In the paper's words, "a byte-level version of BPE only requires a base vocabulary of size 256", and because the scheme can assign a probability to any Unicode string it lets a model be evaluated "on any dataset regardless of pre-processing, tokenization, or vocab size".

That is where GPT-2's often-quoted vocabulary size comes from, and it decomposes exactly:

One guard was needed on top. Left alone, a frequency-driven merger will happily spend separate vocabulary slots on dog., dog! and dog?, which the paper says it observed. So GPT-2 chops the text into runs of one kind of character before merging, and forbids merges that cross from letters into punctuation or digits. The single exception is a leading space, which is allowed to stick to the word after it, and that exception has consequences of its own.

7. Two consequences that outlive the algorithm

A leading space is part of the token

Because a space is allowed to attach to the word that follows it, hello and  hello are two different tokens with two unrelated identifiers: 31373 and 23748. The model has no way to know they are the same word except by having seen both a great many times. This is why a prompt that ends in a trailing space behaves oddly, and why capitalisation is not free either: Hello is one token, and HELLO is three.

The same effect makes indentation expensive. Four leading spaces are four separate tokens, so a Python file pays a tax on every line. This was not a theoretical worry: when OpenAI later fine-tuned this family on code, the Codex paper reports that "the largest source of inefficiency arises from encoding whitespace", and that adding tokens for whole runs of whitespace let them "represent code using approximately 30% fewer tokens".

The same sentence costs far more in some languages than others

Byte-level encoding guarantees that any language can be represented. It guarantees nothing about the price. The merges were learned by counting, on a corpus that was overwhelmingly English, so scripts that were rare in that corpus received almost no merges and fall back to raw bytes, at two to four bytes a character.

State that ratio carefully. It is six to one for this sentence, through this tokenizer. A different tokenizer trained on a more balanced corpus gives a different number, and quoting a language ratio without naming the tokenizer is meaningless.

8. What later tokenizers fixed

Several of the specific defects demonstrated above were repaired in the years afterwards, and knowing which ones were repaired is what stops this lesson becoming a permanent complaint.

Digits are the clearest case. GPT-2 splits a long number wherever its merges happen to fall, so 1234567890 becomes 123, 45, 678, 90: four pieces with no arithmetic meaning at all. OpenAI's later cl100k_base tokenizer, whose splitting rule is published in the tiktoken repository, caps a digit run at three characters, so numbers are at least cut into consistent chunks rather than arbitrary ones. Whitespace runs got their own tokens, as the Codex paper describes. Vocabularies grew, which buys back some of the cost for scripts other than English.

What has not changed is the shape of the problem. The tokenizer is still frozen before training, its merges are still frequency artefacts rather than units of meaning, and its corpus still shows through in who pays more. Where the data actually came from traces that corpus back to its source, and it is a shorter chain than most readers expect.

What this lesson does not claim

The token counts in Figure 3 were measured with GPT-2's own tokenizer, loaded from the encoder.json and vocab.bpe files OpenAI publishes. They describe that tokenizer. Do not carry the ratio over to a different model, and do not repeat a language-cost ratio you have seen quoted unless it names the tokenizer it was measured with.

Retrieval practice

Why is one number per word a bad way to feed text to a model?
Every rare word, typo and name collapses into a single catch-all symbol, and what it was is simply gone.
What decides which pair of pieces gets merged at each step?
It is a count and nothing else, which is why the resulting pieces so often cut across what a linguist would call a unit.
Why can a byte-level tokenizer never meet an unknown symbol?
The starting alphabet is the complete set of byte values, so the worst case is falling back to raw bytes rather than failing.
Why does one sentence cost far more tokens in Hindi than in English?
Nothing in the algorithm prefers English; the training corpus for the merges does, and the tokenizer carries that preference forever.
