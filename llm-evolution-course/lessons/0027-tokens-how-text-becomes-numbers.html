Tokens: how text becomes numbers

A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Meaning as a direction in space Give every word a position in a few hundred dimensions, learn those positions only from the company each word keeps, and similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space.

The one tight idea

A model never sees words: it sees pieces chosen by an algorithm that repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair, and once you start from raw bytes nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

2. What this lesson must cover

To write. Full prose, complete sentences, 1,400 to 2,000 words. Every bullet below is a beat the finished lesson owes the reader, in this order.

  • Why not words: an unbounded vocabulary and an unknown token for everything unseen.
  • Why not characters: sequences become far too long.
  • Byte-pair encoding worked by hand on a small corpus, merge by merge, so the reader can follow every step.
  • The byte-level twist: start from the 256 possible bytes and nothing can ever be unknown.
  • Two consequences worth remembering: a leading space changes a token's identity, and the same sentence costs many more tokens in some languages than others.
  • That later tokenizers fixed several of the specific problems demonstrated here.
Do not overclaim

Do not state a token-count ratio between two languages without measuring it with a named tokenizer and reporting which one you used.
