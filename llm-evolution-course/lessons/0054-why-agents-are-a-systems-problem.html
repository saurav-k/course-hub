Why agents are a systems problem

What a model can do on a single attempt keeps improving while how often it succeeds every time keeps not improving, and that gap is where the binding constraint now sits.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.
Where this lesson starts

Tools, protocols, and the agent loop draws a loop that works. This lesson is about why a loop that works most of the time is not the same as a system that works. It assumes you have seen that loop.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Tools, protocols, and the agent loop Tool use went from a prompting pattern to a trained capability to an industry protocol, and the loop it produces is simple enough to draw and hard enough to make reliable.

The one tight idea

What a model can do on a single attempt keeps improving while how often it succeeds every time keeps not improving, and that gap is where the binding constraint now sits.

2. Two words that get used as if they meant the same thing

Almost every confused argument about agents comes from collapsing two different questions into one word, so separate them first and keep them separate.

Capability is whether the system can do the task at all. The usual measurement gives the model one attempt and asks whether it succeeded, which is written pass@1.

Reliability is whether it does the task every time you ask. The usual measurement runs the same task several times and asks whether all of the attempts succeeded, which is written pass^k for k repeats.

Those are not two ways of saying the same thing, and a number reported for the first is structurally silent about the second. A system that succeeds on half its attempts and a system that succeeds on exactly the same half every single time score identically on one attempt and are completely different products.

The gap is easiest to see with one measurement. In September 2025 a benchmark called MCPMark measured tool-using agents on 127 tasks written by domain experts, each starting from a prepared initial state and each checked by a program rather than by a person, and each demanding real create, read, update and delete work rather than lookups. The best model of the day reached 52.56 percent when given one attempt and 33.86 percent when required to succeed on four attempts out of four. Other models widely regarded as strong at the time were below 30 percent on one attempt and below 15 percent on four. The tasks took an average of 16.2 turns and 17.4 tool calls.

Those two percentages describe the same models on the same tasks on the same day. The distance between them is the subject of this lesson.

3. Why length is the enemy

The arithmetic is the least surprising thing in this course and the most consequential.

Suppose each step of a task succeeds with probability p, and suppose for a moment that the steps are independent. Then a run of n steps succeeds with probability p multiplied by itself n times. That is the whole model, and it is enough to show why per-step improvements that sound trivial are not.

The 17-step column is there because it is roughly what the benchmark above measured in practice: about seventeen tool calls per task. At that length, a model that gets 95 percent of its individual steps right finishes about two tasks in five.

Notice what the table says about where effort should go. Moving from 90 to 95 percent per step is a large engineering achievement and it roughly doubles the end-to-end rate at seventeen steps. Moving from 95 to 99 doubles it again. Each additional nine buys the same multiple, and the length of task you can attempt at a fixed success rate grows accordingly.

Now the honest caveat, because the independence assumption is false in both directions. Real steps are correlated, and that helps when an agent notices its own mistake and repairs it, which the arithmetic gives it no credit for. It hurts far more when an early error poisons everything downstream, because then a single bad step does not cost you one factor of p, it costs you the run. Treat the table as a way of seeing why the exponent matters, not as a model of an agent.

4. What the measurements actually say

Two bodies of evidence matter, and they point in different directions, which is the finding.

Capability is rising, and there is a trend line for it

In March 2025 the research group METR proposed measuring an agent by the length of task it can complete, expressed in how long the same task takes a person with the relevant expertise. Their metric is the human task duration at which a model succeeds half the time. They timed people on a suite of software tasks, measured models against the same suite, and reported that this horizon had been doubling roughly every seven months since 2019.

Those are the 2025 figures and they have since been restated. On 29 January 2026 METR published Time Horizon 1.1, which grew the suite from 170 tasks to 228 and more than doubled the number of tasks estimated to take a person eight hours or more, from 14 to 31. Fitted over the whole period the doubling time barely moved, at 196 days, but the recent slope is steeper than the headline: 131 days measured from 2023, and 89 days measured from 2024. Read those three numbers together rather than picking one, because the disagreement between them is the finding. METR also states plainly that its own suite runs out before the frontier does: measurements above sixteen hours, it says, are unreliable with the current task suite.

Then METR audited itself, which is the part of this story worth carrying longest. On 3 March 2026 it corrected a modelling mistake of its own, an unmotivated regularisation term that had been flattening the fitted curves, and the fix lowered recent models' fifty-per-cent time horizons by up to twenty per cent. Seventeen days later it published a sensitivity analysis going considerably further, walking through every other modelling assumption in the metric and showing how much each one could move the answer. A laboratory publishing the ways its own headline number might still be wrong is a better worked example of the source discipline in When the record closed than any benchmark figure in this course.

Cite all of this as a trend and never as a current number, and the reason is that the authors say so themselves. INFERRED: the shape of the trend depends entirely on the task suite and on how the half-success point is estimated, both of which the authors have themselves changed, twice, within a year. Every figure above was read from METR's own pages on 22 August 2026; the live dashboard is the place to check them, not this page.

Reliability is not rising with it

Three separate pieces of work published in 2026 report the same thing from different angles.

  • February 2026. A framework paper proposes twelve metrics decomposing reliability into consistency, robustness, predictability and safety, evaluates fifteen models on two benchmarks, and concludes that recent capability gains have only yielded small improvements in reliability.
  • March 2026. A larger study runs ten models through 23,392 episodes over 396 tasks in three domains and four duration buckets. Four of its findings are worth carrying: capability and reliability rankings diverge, with models swapping several places once the tasks get long; the decay is domain-specific, so software engineering degrades sharply while document processing barely moves; the strongest models have the highest catastrophic failure rates, up to 19 percent, because they attempt ambitious multi-step strategies that sometimes spiral; and, awkwardly for a popular design pattern, memory scaffolds hurt long-horizon performance across all ten models they tested.
  • April 2026. A diagnostic benchmark collects more than 3,100 trajectories across four agentic domains specifically to characterise where long-horizon runs break rather than only how often.

Each of these is a preprint with a stated method and a public task suite, which is the standard this course asks of a measurement. None of them is a peer-reviewed settled result, and all three are recent enough that this page carries a review date rather than a conclusion.

The third finding is the one that should change how you read a launch announcement. It is not that weak models fail more, which would be unremarkable. It is that the models that can attempt more also fail worse when they fail, because attempting more is exactly what produces a long dependent chain with no way back.

5. The failure modes that only appear at length

Short tasks fail for boring reasons: the model did not know something, or misread the question. Long runs fail in ways that have no short-task equivalent, and the diagnostic work above exists to name them.

Drift. The goal was stated at the top of a context that has since grown by tens of thousands of tokens of tool output. What the agent is now optimising is its reading of the goal, filtered through everything since.

Looping. An action fails, and the agent tries it again, slightly rephrased, indefinitely. Nothing in the loop of goal, decide, call, observe forces progress, which is a point Tools, protocols, and the agent loop makes about the loop's structure.

Misreading the error. An error message is just text arriving in the context. An agent that reads it wrongly does not stop; it acts confidently on the misreading, and the next error is a compound of two.

Giving up early, or declaring victory. A run that stops and reports success is indistinguishable, to the calling system, from a run that succeeded.

Actions that cannot be undone. The deleted record, the sent message, the paid invoice. This one is not a property of the model at all: it is a property of the environment, and it is why sandboxes, dry runs and approval gates exist. An unreliable agent in a reversible environment is an annoyance; the same agent in an irreversible one is an incident.

6. Why the answer is engineering

Put the two halves together and the conclusion is unavoidable: the model is no longer the binding constraint on what an agent can attempt. The surrounding system is.

That system has a name in production, the harness, and its jobs are specific. Decide what stays in the context and what is summarised or dropped. Verify each step against something external rather than against the model's own opinion of it. Detect a loop and break it. Bound the tool surface so an injected instruction has fewer things to reach, which is the only currently deployed answer to the security problem described in Tools, protocols, and the agent loop. Checkpoint, so a failed run resumes rather than restarts. And put a person in front of anything irreversible.

One warning against reading that list as "add more machinery". The March 2026 study found that memory scaffolds hurt long-horizon performance in every one of the ten models it tested, which is a direct negative result about a popular addition. Harness design is a measured engineering discipline with real failures in it, not a pile of plausible components.

Two courses in this hub take it from here. Agent Engineering on durable state and degradation chains builds the machinery, and AI System Design on reliability as a design property treats reliability as something you design for rather than measure afterwards.

7. How to read a claim about an agent

The practical residue of this lesson is five questions, and they are worth asking of any number you are shown.

  • One attempt or all attempts? If the figure is a single-attempt score, it tells you what the system can do and nothing about what it will do.
  • How many steps? A score without a task length is uninterpretable, because the same per-step quality produces wildly different end-to-end numbers at ten steps and at fifty.
  • Which domain? Decay is domain-specific, and a figure from document processing does not transfer to software engineering.
  • Measured when, and with which harness? Agent scores are harness-dependent, so the same model with a different scaffold is a different measurement.
  • What happens when it fails? A 19 percent catastrophic failure rate and a 19 percent polite-refusal rate are the same number and opposite products.

Reading an eval table like a scientist is the general version of that discipline, and What is still open, and what would change it states the unresolved form of this question: whether reliability can be made to rise with capability, or whether the finding that the strongest models fail hardest is structural.

Retrieval practice

What is the difference between capability and reliability here?
A single-attempt score is structurally blind to whether the same half of the tasks succeeds each time or a different half does.
Why does a 95 percent per-step model finish few long tasks?
At about seventeen steps, which is what a realistic tool-use benchmark measured, 95 percent per step is roughly 42 percent end to end.
What did the 2026 studies find about the strongest models?
Attempting an ambitious multi-step strategy is what produces a long dependent chain, and a long dependent chain is what spirals.
Why is the fix described as engineering rather than a bigger model?
Context management, external verification, loop detection and approval gates all live outside the model, which is why they are the current binding constraint.
