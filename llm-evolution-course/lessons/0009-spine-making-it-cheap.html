Making it cheap

Once capability was possible, the binding constraint became price, and the answer was to stop paying for the whole model on every token.

Where this lesson starts

The open-weights turn left a large and growing set of models that most people still could not afford to train, tune, or serve. This chapter is about the four separate things the field did about that.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • The open-weights turn One set of leaked weights in early 2023 revealed enormous latent demand and created a parallel ecosystem, and the word open has been fought over ever since because it is really four separate questions.

The one tight idea

Once capability was possible, the binding constraint became price, and the answer was to stop paying for the whole model on every token.

2. Tuning a model you cannot afford to retrain

By 2023 the weights of capable models were downloadable, as The open-weights turn describes, and almost nobody could do anything with them. Adjusting a model in the ordinary way means updating every one of its numbers, which means holding all of them in memory along with a working copy for the training machinery. For a model of any size that is a rack of hardware, not a laptop.

Four separate ideas answered that, and this chapter takes them in order: cheap adaptation, cheap serving, sparse computation, and honest context length. None of them makes a model more capable. All of them change what it costs to have one.

The idea that broke this open is small and slightly surprising. Freeze the original numbers entirely. Alongside them, add a much smaller set of extra numbers, and train only those. At the end you have the untouched original plus a small patch, and the patch is a few tenths of a per cent of the size of what it modifies. This was published in June 2021 as LoRA and it works because the change a model needs in order to specialise turns out to be far simpler than the model itself.

A second paper in May 2023 added the other half. Store the frozen originals at reduced precision, roughly four bits per number instead of sixteen, using a scheme designed to lose as little as possible, and the whole job fits on one large card that a research group or a determined individual can rent by the hour. Between them, those two ideas turned fine-tuning from an industrial activity into a hobbyist one, and that is a large part of why the ecosystem described in The open-weights turn appeared as quickly as it did. The LoRA lesson next door works through the mechanism.

3. Serving is a memory problem before it is an arithmetic problem

Tuning is a one-off cost. Serving is the cost you pay forever, and it has a shape most people guess wrong.

A model generates one piece of text at a time, and each new piece has to attend to everything that came before it. Recomputing all of that from scratch for every new token would be absurd, so the intermediate results for each earlier position are kept and reused. That store is called the key-value cache, and it holds one entry per position per layer.

Two things follow. First, the cache grows linearly with the length of the conversation, and in practice it gets very large: at long context it can rival the model's own weights for memory. Second, generating each token requires reading the whole cache, which means the limiting resource is how fast memory can be read rather than how fast the arithmetic can be done. A graphics card that can perform enormous numbers of operations per second spends much of its time waiting for numbers to arrive.

Almost all the serving engineering since 2022 follows from that sentence. You can manage the cache in small blocks rather than one contiguous run, so that memory is not wasted on space reserved and never used. You can have several attention heads share one set of cached entries instead of each keeping its own. You can compress the cache into a smaller representation and expand it on use, which one 2024 design reported cut the cache by about 93 per cent. None of these change what the model knows. They change what it costs to let it speak.

4. Sparsity: what a model knows against what it computes

The deeper problem is that in an ordinary model, knowing more and costing more are the same thing.

Most of a model's numbers live in a block that sits after the attention in every layer, and every piece of text passing through is multiplied by all of them, every time. Want a model that knows more? Make that block bigger. Now every token costs more, forever. Parameters and cost are welded together.

Mixture of experts breaks that weld, and the mechanism is simpler than the name. Replace the one big block with many smaller ones, called experts. Add a small chooser, called the router, which is one modest table of numbers and a rule that picks the highest-scoring few. For each piece of text, score every expert, pick the top handful, send the text through only those, and blend their outputs weighted by their scores.

The consequence is that a model now has two sizes, and they are different numbers. There is the total, which is what it knows and what you must fit in memory. And there is the active count, which is what actually runs for each token and therefore sets the cost. A widely used 2023 model held 47 billion parameters and used 13 billion per token. By 2026 the ratio at the frontier of open weights had reached roughly thirty to one.

Read that trend carefully, because the surprising number is not the one people quote. Total parameters went up enormously between 2023 and 2026. Active parameters per token barely moved: around thirteen billion at the start of that period and around thirteen billion in a mainstream 2026 release, with only the very largest models reaching a hundred. The industry found it could keep expanding what a model knows almost indefinitely while what it computes per token grew very slowly.

The catch is memory again. All those parameters have to be resident somewhere even when they are idle, so the largest open-weight models of 2026 are simultaneously free to download and impossible for an individual to serve. Sparsity did not remove the bottleneck. It moved it from arithmetic to memory capacity.

5. Why the router is the hard part

A router is trained like everything else, which means nothing in the mechanism prevents it learning to send almost everything to the same three experts. If it does, those three get all the practice and improve, the rest get none and stay poor, and the router acquires an even better reason to prefer the three it already likes. That runaway is called routing collapse, and it was identified as far back as 2017. Worse, in a real cluster the experts live on different machines, so a router that favours a few of them turns hundreds of machines into one busy machine and a lot of idle ones.

Three generations of fix tell the story neatly, and each one is more elegant than the last.

The first, from 2021, adds a term to the training objective that penalises imbalance, along with a fixed capacity per expert; anything that overflows is simply dropped and skips the layer. It works, and it has an obvious flaw: you are degrading the model in order to keep the scheduler happy. The paper that introduced it says so, noting that too large a balancing penalty impairs performance.

The second, from January 2024, is about specialisation rather than balance: use many more, much smaller experts so the model can compose a more precise combination per token, and set aside a few shared experts that every token always uses, to hold the common knowledge that would otherwise be duplicated everywhere.

The third, from December 2024, is the one worth remembering. Keep a small bias number per expert. Add it to the scores only when deciding which experts to pick, never when weighting their outputs. After each training step, look at the actual load and nudge the bias down for overloaded experts and up for underloaded ones. That is a control loop rather than a penalty: it adjusts the queue without distorting the answer, and the laboratory that introduced it reported dropping no tokens at all.

6. The same trick, pointed at attention

Once the expert blocks are cheap, the expensive thing left in the layer is attention, whose cost grows with the square of the length as The Transformer turn sets out. And by 2025 everybody wanted very long contexts, because an agent reading a codebase needs them.

The answer of 2025 and 2026 is the same idea in a second location. Instead of every position attending to every position, a cheap index runs first and picks the few thousand positions most likely to matter, and full attention runs over only those. The clearest way to see this is in the models' own configuration files, where it is a single integer: at a context of one million positions, one 2026 model attends to 1,024 of them per query rather than to a million. Lab-reported reductions in attention computation at that length run to more than an order of magnitude. MARKETING

There is an honest caveat built into the design. The index has to consider everything in order to decide what to keep, so the index is itself the remaining quadratic cost. Later work attacks that by noticing that neighbouring layers pick almost the same positions, and reusing one layer's choices across several. This is a good general lesson: an optimisation usually relocates a cost rather than deleting it.

7. Why a small model now beats a very large old one

A model of a few billion parameters in 2026 outperforms the 175-billion-parameter model of 2020 on most things people measure. Six causes stack up, and it is worth stating plainly that almost none of them is a better architecture.

  1. Far more data. The 2020 model saw 300 billion tokens. A small 2024 model saw fifty times that, on a model twenty times smaller. The scaling bet explains why the older one was badly undertrained.
  2. Better data. Aggressive deduplication, filtering and curriculum, and in some lines deliberately synthesised material. Some of the strongest published claims here are contested on contamination grounds, so treat them carefully.
  3. Architectural hygiene. A dozen small improvements to position handling, normalisation and attention layout. Each is worth a few per cent; together they are worth a lot.
  4. Post-training. The 2020 model had none at all. Teaching a model to be useful shows how much of the usable gap sits here rather than in the base model.
  5. Distillation from a larger teacher. Many small modern models are lossy compressions of a frontier model rather than small models trained from scratch.
  6. It is allowed to think. A 2026 model can spend thousands of tokens of working before answering, where the 2020 model answered in one pass. The reasoning turn, and where we are is about that.

How much each of those six contributes is a judgement rather than a measurement. Nobody has run the experiment that would separate them, because it would mean training the same model six times with one ingredient removed each time, and nobody has done it in public. Anyone who gives you percentages for that split is estimating.

8. The number you are sold and the number you get

One cost reduction deserves separate treatment because it is routinely misread.

Advertised context length went from two thousand positions in 2020 to a million by 2026. That number is a capacity claim: how much text the model will accept without refusing. It is not a competence claim, and the two are routinely different by a large factor.

Two measured failure modes are worth knowing by name. In July 2023 researchers reported that performance is highest when the relevant information sits at the beginning or the end of the input and degrades significantly when the model has to use something in the middle, even for models built for long inputs. That is a positional bias, and it is the reason "put the important thing last" is real advice rather than folklore. In April 2024 a harder evaluation than the usual find-the-sentence test added multi-step tracing and aggregation, and found that although models claimed context sizes of 32,000 positions or more, only about half of them maintained satisfactory performance at that length.

Retrieval is not comprehension. The 2026 answer is that context is managed rather than merely enlarged: production systems summarise earlier turns, clear stale tool output, and give the model explicit memory to write to, spending the million positions deliberately instead of filling them.

By this point the field had capability it could afford to deploy everywhere. What it still had was a model that got exactly one pass of thought per question, however hard the question was. Changing that is the last new axis in this course, and it is The reasoning turn, and where we are.

Why is serving a token a memory problem rather than an arithmetic one?
The cache grows with the conversation and is read in full for every token, so the hardware spends its time waiting for numbers rather than multiplying them.
What does a mixture of experts actually decouple?
Total parameters are what you must store and active parameters are what you must run, and the router is what makes those two numbers different.
Why does a learned router need something to stop it?
Routing collapse is a runaway: practice improves the chosen few, which gives the router an even better reason to keep choosing them.
What does an advertised context length of one million tell you?
Capacity and competence are routinely a large factor apart, and measured failures include a bias against material sitting in the middle.
