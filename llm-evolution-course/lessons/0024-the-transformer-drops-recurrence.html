The Transformer: what dropping recurrence unlocked

Delete the recurrence and keep only attention, and every position in a sentence can be computed at the same time with a constant number of operations between any two words.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Attention fixed reach in Attention: stop compressing and left speed broken in The other problem: attention did not make it fast. This lesson is the paper that fixed speed by removing the part that was slow. It assumes you know what a query, a key and a value are.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Attention: stop compressing Do not squeeze the source sentence into one vector at all: keep every position, and at each output step learn a weighting that says which positions matter now.
  • The other problem: attention did not make it fast Attention fixed how far a model could reach, but the recurrence underneath it still forced one step per word, so training time grew with sentence length no matter how many machines you had.

The one tight idea

Delete the recurrence and keep only attention, and every position in a sentence can be computed at the same time with a constant number of operations between any two words.

2. The claim is in the title

"Attention Is All You Need" was posted to arXiv on 12 June 2017 by eight authors at Google. Its abstract makes a claim of subtraction rather than addition: it proposes an architecture based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely.

Nothing about attention was new in June 2017. It had been in use for nearly three years, always bolted on top of a recurrent network. The contribution is removing everything else, and then showing that what remains is better as well as faster.

The paper's own author-contribution footnote is unusually specific about how the idea arrived, and it is worth reproducing because it is rare to get this from a paper at all. Jakob proposed replacing recurrent networks with self-attention and started the effort to evaluate the idea. Ashish, with Illia, designed and implemented the first Transformer models. Noam proposed the scaled dot product, the multiple heads and the parameter-free position representation. The footnote also says that all eight contributed equally and the listing order is random, which is why this course names all eight rather than calling it Vaswani's paper.

3. One block, and then six of them

The architecture is a stack of identical blocks, and one block does two things.

First, self-attention: every position looks at every position, including itself, and replaces its own representation with a blend of all of them. This is the only place in the block where positions talk to each other.

Second, a small feed-forward network applied separately to each position, with the same weights everywhere. This is where a position does its own private processing on what it just gathered. In the base model this network expands each position's 512 numbers to 2,048, applies a nonlinearity, and comes back down to 512.

Two housekeeping devices wrap each of those. A residual connection adds the input of the sub-layer back on to its output, so information can pass through untouched if that is what helps, which is what makes a deep stack trainable at all. Then layer normalisation rescales the result so that its numbers stay in a sensible range, which stops the values compounding as they pass through the stack.

Six such blocks form the encoder. The decoder is another six, each with an extra attention step in the middle that looks at the encoder's output rather than at the decoder's own positions.

One detail here has an afterlife. The paper adds the residual first and normalises afterwards, which is called post-normalisation. It works, and it turns out to be fussy to train at large scale, so later models moved the normalisation to before the sub-layer instead. GPT-2 and the bet on scale is where that change shows up in this course. It is a small edit with a large effect on whether very deep stacks train, and it is a good example of how much of what followed was engineering rather than insight.

4. Self-attention is the same mechanism, pointed inwards

Attention as invented for translation, described in Attention: stop compressing, points from one sequence at another: the decoder writing French forms a query and compares it against the keys of the English source words. Self-attention points a sequence at itself. Each position forms a query, compares it against the keys of every position in the same sentence, and blends the values it finds.

The classic example is a pronoun. In "the animal did not cross the street because it was too tired", the word "it" must resolve to "animal" and not to "street". With self-attention, the position holding "it" issues a query, "animal" advertises a key that matches it well, and the blend that "it" receives is mostly made of "animal". Nobody wrote a rule about pronouns; the weights were learned because getting this right reduces the error.

The scoring is a dot product between the query and the key, divided by a fixed number to stop the scores growing with the size of the vectors, and then normalised into weights that add up to one. That divisor is the only formula this lesson mentions, and it exists for a mundane reason: without it, larger vectors produce larger dot products, the normalising step saturates, and training stalls. The papers course lesson on this paper derives it properly.

Several heads at once

One set of queries, keys and values gives one opinion about what each position should attend to. The paper found it works better to run several in parallel, each with its own smaller projection of the vectors, and then join their results back together. The base model uses eight of these heads, each working in 64 dimensions rather than 512.

Because each head works in a smaller space, eight heads cost about the same as one head using the full width, so the extra opinions are close to free. The stated reason for wanting them is that a single averaged attention blurs distinct kinds of relationship together; with several heads, one can track which noun a verb agrees with while another tracks which noun a pronoun refers to. The paper's own ablations show this is not a free parameter you can push in either direction: a single head is nearly a whole BLEU point worse, and too many heads is worse again.

5. Order has to be put back by hand

Here is the price of deleting recurrence, and it is stranger than the cost people usually quote.

A recurrent network knows what order its words are in because it reads them in order. Self-attention does not read in order; it compares everything with everything. Shuffle the words of the input and every query, key and value is unchanged, so every score is unchanged, so the output is the same set of vectors in a different arrangement. A model built only from attention is genuinely blind to word order. This is not a subtle limitation, and it was already known: the 2016 attention-only model described in The other problem: attention did not make it fast advertised working without any word-order information at all.

So order has to be added to the input as data. Before the first block, each position gets a distinctive pattern of numbers added to its word vector, encoding where it sits. The paper's scheme uses sine and cosine waves at geometrically spaced frequencies, so that every position gets a unique fingerprint and nearby positions get similar ones. Their stated hope was that the model could then learn to attend by relative position, since a fixed offset in position corresponds to a fixed transformation of the pattern, and that this might let a trained model cope with sentences longer than anything it was trained on.

It is an elegant scheme, and it is not the one that won, which the paper itself half-predicts. They also tried simply learning a vector for each position, as an ordinary parameter, and reported that the two versions produced nearly identical results. They kept the waves for the extrapolation argument rather than for a measured advantage.

Everything built on this architecture in the following year used the learned version instead, because it is simpler and performed the same. And a few years after that, both were displaced by a third scheme that encodes position as a rotation applied to the query and the key, which handles relative distance more directly. The papers course lesson on rotary position embeddings is where that is taught.

6. The bill: work grows with the square of the length

Self-attention compares every position with every position. Ten words is a hundred comparisons. A hundred words is ten thousand. A thousand words is a million. Work grows with the square of the sequence length, and so does the memory needed to hold the scores while they are computed.

The paper's own comparison, in its Table 1, is honest about when this is a good deal. A recurrent layer's work grows with the length of the sentence multiplied by the square of the vector size. A self-attention layer's grows with the square of the length multiplied by the vector size. So self-attention is cheaper exactly while the sentence is shorter than the vector, which the paper notes was true of the sentences it was translating. Translation sentences are tens of tokens long and the vectors were 512 numbers wide. The trade was excellent.

It stops being excellent the moment anybody wants to read a document rather than a sentence, and that is what everybody wanted within two years. Paying down this quadratic cost is a large fraction of all engineering work on these models from 2019 onwards: caching what has already been computed, restricting what each position may look at, rewriting the computation so it never stores the full square of scores at once. Serving reality: the cache, and what it costs and Long context: capacity is not competence are where this course takes that up.

Say it once here so the rest of the course lands: the Transformer bought constant path length and constant sequential depth, and it paid for them in quadratic work, and the field has been servicing that debt ever since.

7. What it was worth, in the paper's own numbers

The results are the reason the architecture took over within a year rather than within five.

From Table 2 of "Attention Is All You Need". BLEU is translation quality, higher is better. The cost column is the arithmetic used to train the model, and it is the column that persuaded people.
ModelBLEU, English to GermanTraining cost, floating-point operations
Google's production translation system24.62.3 × 1019
The convolutional alternative of May 201725.169.6 × 1018
An ensemble of the production system26.301.8 × 1020
Transformer, base27.33.3 × 1018
Transformer, big28.42.3 × 1019

Read the last two rows against the third. The base Transformer beat an ensemble of the best previously published system while using roughly one fiftieth of the arithmetic to train. In wall-clock terms, the base model trained in twelve hours on a single machine with eight graphics cards, and the big model took three and a half days on the same machine. The big model held 213 million parameters and the base model 65 million, which is small by anything that comes later in this course.

A result that is both better and an order of magnitude cheaper does not have to win an argument. Every research group with eight graphics cards could reproduce it in a day, and did.

8. It was a translation paper

The last thing to say about this paper is what it did not claim, because almost everything it is now credited with is in that category.

It is a machine translation paper. Its experiments are two translation tasks and, as a demonstration that the architecture generalises past translation, English sentence parsing. It contains no language model, no pretraining on unlabelled text, and no argument that making the model bigger will make it qualitatively better. Nothing in it proposes using half the architecture on its own. Nothing in it suggests that a model trained on enough text acquires abilities nobody trained it for.

All of those ideas belong to other people, in the following eighteen months, and this course covers them next: keeping one half of the architecture and pretraining it on unlabelled text is ImageNet for words: pretrain once, specialise later and The fork: GPT-1 and BERT, and betting that size alone changes the character of the thing is GPT-2 and the bet on scale.

The useful way to hold it is that the Transformer was the last paper of the machine translation era rather than the first paper of the language model era. It became the starting gun retroactively, and only because somebody else picked it up and pointed it somewhere its authors had not.

Retrieval practice

What did this architecture remove, and what did it keep?
Attention was three years old in 2017 and had always been bolted on to a recurrent network; the contribution is deleting everything around it.
Why does a model built only from attention need a position signal?
Attention compares everything with everything, so nothing in it distinguishes "the cat sat" from "sat cat the" unless order is added as data.
What did the architecture buy, and what did it pay?
Constant path length and constant sequential depth were the gains, and every position attending to every position is the bill.
What is the most common overstatement made about this paper?
It has no language model, no pretraining and no scaling argument in it; every headline number in it is a translation score.
