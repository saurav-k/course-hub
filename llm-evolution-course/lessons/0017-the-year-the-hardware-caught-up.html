2012: the year the hardware caught up

The ideas that won in 2012 were roughly twenty-five years old, and what actually changed was a large labelled dataset and a graphics card that could do the arithmetic in parallel.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

The methods in What a neural network actually is, Backpropagation, and the two winters and Sequences, memory, and the LSTM all existed by 1997 and did not take over. This lesson explains why they suddenly did, and it is about hardware and data rather than about mathematics.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • What a neural network actually is A network is a stack of two operations, multiply by a table of numbers and then bend the result, and training is nudging those numbers until a measured error gets smaller.

The one tight idea

The ideas that won in 2012 were roughly twenty-five years old, and what actually changed was a large labelled dataset and a graphics card that could do the arithmetic in parallel.

2. A benchmark big enough to be hard to cheat

Before 2009 there was no way to settle an argument about computer vision. The available labelled datasets held tens of thousands of images across tens of categories, which is small enough that a method can do well by memorising the quirks of the collection rather than by seeing anything. A field with no measurement it trusts cannot tell progress from fashion.

ImageNet was the answer, presented by Jia Deng, Wei Dong, Richard Socher, Li-Jia Li, Kai Li and Fei-Fei Li at a computer-vision conference in June 2009. Its construction is two existing things bolted together. The categories come from WordNet, a hand-built hierarchy of English concepts in which each meaning is a set of synonymous words, about eighty thousand of them for nouns. The images come from the open web, retrieved and then verified by people through Amazon Mechanical Turk, which is a marketplace for paying strangers small sums to do small tasks.

The 2009 paper describes twelve branches of that hierarchy, 5,247 categories and 3.2 million verified images, and states the ambition plainly: to reach the order of fifty million cleanly labelled full-resolution images, five hundred to a thousand for each of WordNet's eighty thousand noun categories. The annual competition run on it, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, used a slice of that: a thousand categories, about 1.2 million training images, fifty thousand for validation and a held-out test set.

The scoring convention matters for reading anything about this era. A system offers five guesses per image, and top-five error is the fraction of images where the true label was in none of them. Five guesses sounds generous until you remember there are a thousand categories, many of them fine distinctions between breeds of dog.

3. October 2012, and the two numbers

The 2012 results were announced at a workshop in Florence that October. A team from the University of Toronto, entering as SuperVision, won with a convolutional neural network that has been known ever since as AlexNet, after its first author Alex Krizhevsky, who wrote it with Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton.

Here is where nearly every retelling goes wrong, so it is worth being exact. There were two tracks, and SuperVision entered both. Using only the training data the competition supplied, its top-five error was 16.4 per cent. Using extra training data from a larger ImageNet release, it was 15.3 per cent. Both figures are on the official results page and both are real. The AlexNet paper quotes the second one, 15.3 per cent against a second-best entry of 26.2 per cent, and that pairing is the source of the famous ten-point gap. The comparison is fair, because the 26.2 per cent entry is the best of everyone else in the same table, but the number only means anything if you say which track it comes from.

Read the third column downwards. Two years of careful, expert, hand-engineered computer vision moved that number by 2.4 points. One neural network moved it by more than nine. And it kept going: by 2015 the winning entry, a residual network, reported 3.57 per cent on the ImageNet test set. A measurement that had been creeping fell off a cliff, and everybody in the field could see it happen on one slide.

4. Two gaming cards under a desk

The line from the AlexNet paper worth memorising is not about accuracy. It is this: the network took between five and six days to train on two GTX 580 3GB graphics cards.

Those were consumer gaming cards. Not a supercomputer, not a data centre, not a national facility. The network itself had sixty million weights and six hundred and fifty thousand units across five convolutional layers and three fully connected ones, and the paper is candid that the whole design was shaped by the three gigabytes of memory on a single card, which is why the model is split across two of them.

This is the fact that reframes the year. The method was not new. Backpropagation had been common practice since 1986, convolutional networks trained by it had been reading handwritten postal codes since 1989, and the LSTM had been published in 1997. What happened in 2012 was that two students could buy the hardware to run an old idea at a size where it started to work.

5. Wide, not fast

It is worth being precise about what a graphics card actually gives you, because this gets hand-waved constantly and the correct version explains the whole decade.

A graphics processor is not faster than a general-purpose processor. It is wider.

NVIDIA's own CUDA programming guide, version 1.0, dated 23 June 2007, says why in its opening pages. A graphics processor, it explains, is designed so that more transistors are devoted to data processing rather than to data caching and flow control, and it suits problems that can be expressed as the same program executed on many data elements in parallel. Because the same program runs for every element there is less need for sophisticated flow control, and because there are many elements with a lot of arithmetic each, memory delays can be hidden behind calculation rather than behind a large cache.

Training a neural network is exactly that shape of problem. It is dominated by weighted sums, which are thousands of independent multiply-and-add operations with no dependencies between them, all of which could in principle happen at the same instant. A processor of that era executed a handful at a time, very cleverly, with branch prediction and large caches, because it was designed for work where the next step depends on the last one. A graphics card executed thousands at a time, stupidly. For a job with no dependencies in it, stupid and wide wins, and it wins by a lot.

The date on that guide is the quiet hinge of this whole story. CUDA 1.0 is what made a graphics card programmable in ordinary code by people who were not writing graphics. Everything downstream in this course sits on top of it.

The two boxes at the bottom of figure 2 are real papers, and the comparison is the cleanest evidence there is. In December 2011 a team including Quoc Le, Marc'Aurelio Ranzato, Greg Corrado, Jeff Dean and Andrew Ng trained a network with a billion connections on ten million images, using a cluster of a thousand machines and sixteen thousand processor cores for three days. In 2013 Adam Coates, Brody Huval, Tao Wang, David Wu, Bryan Catanzaro and Ng trained billion-parameter networks on three machines with graphics cards in a couple of days, and reported scaling past eleven billion parameters on sixteen machines. Same scale of model, roughly a year apart, three orders of magnitude less hardware. The stated point of that paper is that such infrastructure is much more easily marshalled by others, and that is what actually changed: research at this scale stopped requiring a data centre.

6. Not one lucky result

It would be easy to read 2012 as one team getting lucky on one benchmark. November of that year rules that out.

A paper appeared in IEEE Signal Processing Magazine under the title "Deep Neural Networks for Acoustic Modeling in Speech Recognition: The Shared Views of Four Research Groups", with eleven authors from Toronto, Microsoft, Google and IBM. The title is the finding. Four separate laboratories reported, jointly, that deep networks had displaced the previous standard method in speech recognition. That does not happen because one method was lucky. It happens because a constraint that had been binding on everybody was released at the same moment for everybody.

7. The same lesson reaches language

Language followed within about two years, and the pattern is identical.

In January 2013 Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeff Dean published a much cheaper way to learn a numeric representation for every word, reporting that it took under a day on 1.6 billion words; that is the subject of Meaning as a direction in space. In August 2013 Alex Graves showed recurrent networks generating text and handwriting one character at a time. In September 2014 a recurrent network translated between two languages end to end, having been trained for about ten days on a machine with eight graphics cards.

None of those results required a new mathematical idea. They required text at scale and machines that could finish the training, and both of those had just arrived. This is why Sequences, memory, and the LSTM ends on a seventeen-year gap between a paper and its moment, and this lesson is what closed it.

8. A field can be constrained by compute rather than by ideas

That is the general lesson, and this course returns to it repeatedly, so it is worth stating flatly.

When a field stops making progress, the natural assumption is that it is short of ideas, and the natural response is to go looking for a new one. Between 1997 and 2012 that assumption was wrong. The ideas were on the shelf, correct and published, and what was missing was labelled data at scale and arithmetic that was wide enough and cheap enough. Both arrived from outside the field: WordNet was built by linguists, Mechanical Turk was built to sell labour, and the graphics card was built to sell video games.

Notice what this does to the question of who deserves the credit. Nobody in 2012 out-thought the people of 1997. They were standing at the moment the constraint moved, and they recognised it. That is a real and rare skill, and it is a different skill from having the idea.

9. What it owes the next step

2012 settled the method and immediately raised the price. From this point on, the interesting quantity in this story is not only what a model does but what it cost to train, and every later chapter has a hardware sentence in it somewhere: eight cards for ten days in 2014, then racks, then buildings.

The immediate debt is more specific. Vision had a representation that worked and language did not. A network can take a photograph as a list of pixel brightnesses without anyone deciding anything; there is no equally obvious answer for a word, and treating a word as an entry in a table is exactly the thing that had already failed. Meaning as a direction in space is the representation that fixed it, and it arrived three months after AlexNet.

Retrieval practice

Why must you name the track when quoting AlexNet's error rate?
Both numbers are real and both are on the official results page, so a figure without its track cannot be compared with anything.
In what sense is a graphics card better suited to training a network?
Weighted sums have no dependencies between them, so spending transistors on arithmetic rather than on caching and flow control is the right trade.
What actually changed between 1997 and 2012?
Backpropagation dates from 1986 and the LSTM from 1997, so the two things that moved were a large labelled dataset and programmable graphics cards.
Why does the speech result of November 2012 matter alongside ImageNet?
One team winning one benchmark can be an accident; four laboratories reporting jointly means a constraint had been released for everyone at once.
