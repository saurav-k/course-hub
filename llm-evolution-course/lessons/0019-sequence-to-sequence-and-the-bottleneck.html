Sequence to sequence, and the eight thousand numbers

Read a whole sentence into a single fixed list of numbers and then write another sentence out of it, and that single list is both the elegance and the flaw.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Word vectors give a representation for a word. This lesson is about representing a whole sentence, and about the specific failure that appears when the sentence gets long.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Sequences, memory, and the LSTM A recurrent network is one small network applied again and again with a carried-over memory, and the LSTM is that memory given a protected lane and gates that decide what enters it.
  • Meaning as a direction in space Give every word a position in a few hundred dimensions, learn those positions only from the company each word keeps, and similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space.

The one tight idea

Read a whole sentence into a single fixed list of numbers and then write another sentence out of it, and that single list is both the elegance and the flaw.

2. Translation has an awkward shape

A word on its own is not a task. Translation is a task, and its shape is the reason it was hard for a neural network to attempt at all.

The input is a sentence of some length. The output is a sentence of a different length. The two do not line up. German holds its verb back to the end of the clause. French needs two words where English needs one, and puts its adjectives on the other side of the noun. So you cannot walk down the source sentence emitting one target word per step, which is what a network that reads a sequence and writes a sequence would naturally do.

Every neural network before this point had a fixed job description. You handed it a thing of a known size and it handed you back a thing of a known size: an image in, a label out. The sequence-to-sequence problem breaks that on both ends at once, and that is the problem the encoder-decoder was invented to get around.

3. Read the whole thing, then write the whole thing

The design splits the job in two, and the split is the whole idea.

The encoder reads the source sentence one word at a time, keeping a running summary as it goes. When it reaches the last word, that running summary is taken to stand for the sentence. The decoder then starts from that summary and writes the target sentence one word at a time, each new word conditioned on the summary and on the words it has already produced. Read, then write. Nothing in the design needs the two sentences to be the same length, and nothing in it needs a grammar.

Be concrete about what the running summary is, because everything in this lesson turns on it. It is a list of numbers of a size the designer picks in advance, and it is overwritten after every word the encoder reads. Meaning as a direction in space introduced a word as a position in a space of a few hundred numbers; this is the same kind of object, standing for a whole sentence rather than for one word.

Nobody specifies how the meaning of a sentence should be encoded into that list. The training loop decides, because whatever encoding lets the decoder produce translations that match the human reference is the encoding that makes the measured error smaller.

Three groups reached this design inside a year, and it is worth naming all three, because the compressed version of this story usually names one.

  • Nal Kalchbrenner and Phil Blunsom presented recurrent continuous translation models at EMNLP in October 2013, with a convolutional encoder and a recurrent decoder. This is the first neural encoder-decoder for translation, and it is routinely left out.
  • Kyunghyun Cho and six colleagues posted the framing that stuck on 3 June 2014, along with a simpler recurrent unit called the GRU. Their paper still used the neural model as a component inside a conventional translation system rather than as the whole thing.
  • Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le posted the result that made the field pay attention on 10 September 2014: an encoder-decoder built from two four-layer LSTMs, translating English to French on its own, and beating the phrase-based statistical system it was measured against.

Convergent, not singular. Three groups pulled at the same thread within twelve months, and the third one pulled hard enough to move the state of the art.

4. What BLEU is, and why the field chased it

Every number in this lesson is a BLEU score, so it is worth one paragraph on what BLEU measures before any of them appear.

BLEU compares a machine translation against one or more human reference translations of the same sentence. It counts how many short runs of words the machine produced that also appear in the reference, for runs of one, two, three and four words, and combines those four counts into a single number between 0 and 100. It then applies a penalty if the machine's output is shorter than the reference, because a system that emits three safe words would otherwise score well by saying almost nothing. Higher is better. Nothing in it understands anything: it is word overlap with a length check.

The field chased it anyway, for a reason worth taking seriously. BLEU is automatic, so it costs nothing to run on ten thousand sentences overnight, and human judgement of translation quality costs a great deal and takes weeks. Over a whole test set it ranks systems in roughly the order human raters do, which is all a research group needs to know whether last night's change helped. A metric that is cheap and directionally right will be optimised, and this one was, for fifteen years.

Its authors knew what it was not. Cho and colleagues said so plainly in the very paper this lesson quotes for its main result, noting that BLEU is used as a de-facto standard and is not the perfect metric. Treat every BLEU number below as a measurement of a proxy: real, comparable within a table, and not the same thing as a good translation.

5. Eight thousand numbers

The seq2seq paper says the quiet part out loud, in one sentence in its training-details section. Its network is four LSTM layers with a thousand cells in each, and it carries two lists of a thousand numbers per layer, so, in the paper's own words, the deep LSTM uses 8,000 real numbers to represent a sentence.

Eight thousand numbers is a great deal of room for "the cat sat on the mat". It is not much room for a forty-word legal clause with three subordinate constructions inside it. Both get exactly the same allocation.

The result that made everyone look was worth looking at. On the standard WMT 2014 English-to-French test set, an ensemble of five of these models scored 34.81 BLEU against 33.30 for the phrase-based statistical system that had been the thing to beat. When the same ensemble was used only to re-rank the thousand candidate translations that the statistical system produced, the score rose to 36.5. This was not a toy. The model held 384 million parameters and took about ten days to train on a machine with eight graphics cards.

A neural network with no hand-written linguistic knowledge of any kind had just beaten the statistical machine translation tradition that began at IBM in 1990, on that tradition's own benchmark. That is why the next three years of the field are about this architecture and its problems.

6. What the squeeze cost, measured

A week before Sutskever's team posted their result, on 3 September 2014, Cho, Bart van Merrienboer, Dzmitry Bahdanau and Yoshua Bengio posted a short paper that did nothing but measure what the encoder-decoder was bad at. Their finding is the sentence this whole part of the course turns on: neural machine translation performs relatively well on short sentences without unknown words, but its performance degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence and the number of unknown words increase.

Their comparison is the part that stings. Restricted to test sentences of between ten and twenty words containing no unfamiliar words, which is the easy case, their neural encoder-decoder scored 27.81 BLEU and the conventional phrase-based system scored 33.08. Then they plotted score against sentence length for both, and the two curves go in opposite directions. The neural system falls away as sentences get longer. The phrase-based system, which never compresses anything because it assembles a translation out of matched phrase pairs, tends to get a higher score on longer sentences.

That opposition is the diagnosis. If the neural system were simply worse at hard sentences, both curves would sag together. Only one of them sags, and it is the one with a fixed-size summary in the middle of it.

7. The patches, and what they admit

People do not build workarounds for problems they have not noticed. The seq2seq paper contains one, and it is the clearest tell in the whole story.

Sutskever's team found that feeding the source sentence to the encoder backwards, while leaving the target sentence the right way round, improved the model a great deal. Test perplexity fell from 5.8 to 4.7 and BLEU rose from 25.9 to 30.6. Nearly five BLEU points from reversing a list.

Their own explanation is the interesting part. Reversing the source does not change the average distance between a source word and its translation. What it changes is the distance between the first few source words and the first few target words, which becomes very short. That gives the correction signal during training a short path to travel at the start of every sentence, and, as they put it, an easier time establishing communication between the source sentence and the target sentence.

A trick that helps by shortening distances is a trick that tells you the architecture was already losing a fight with distance.

Reversal was not the only patch. Groups deepened the network so the summary would hold more, enlarged the vocabulary so fewer words came through as unknown, and trained on sentences capped at thirty or fifty words so the hardest cases never appeared. Each helped a little. None of them touched the shape of the problem, because the shape of the problem is that the summary is one fixed-size list.

There is one honest complication to record. Sutskever's team reported that their own model, with reversal, did not suffer on long sentences, which directly contradicts what Cho's group measured on theirs. Both claims are in the record, a week apart, and the field did not settle the disagreement by arguing about it. It was settled by the paper that removed the fixed-size summary altogether and then measured the difference, which is Attention: stop compressing.

Retrieval practice

What is the bottleneck in an encoder-decoder without attention?
The summary is a fixed-size list of numbers chosen in advance, so a long sentence is squeezed into the room a short one needed.
Why does a rising curve for the phrase-based system matter here?
If long sentences were simply harder, both curves would sag; only the system with a fixed-size summary in the middle does.
What does the source-reversal trick reveal about the architecture?
Reversal carries no linguistic content at all; it only shortens paths, and it was worth nearly five BLEU points.
Why did the field trust BLEU enough to optimise against it for years?
A metric that costs nothing to run overnight and points in the right direction will be optimised, whatever its authors say about it.
