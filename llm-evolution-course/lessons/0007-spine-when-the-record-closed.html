When the record closed

The moment language models became commercially valuable, the papers stopped saying how they were built, so a reader of this field now needs a way to tell a measurement from a claim.

Where this lesson starts

Teaching a model to be useful ended with a research artefact becoming a product. This chapter is about what that did to the evidence, and it is the one chapter that teaches a habit rather than a mechanism.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Teaching a model to be useful A model trained only to continue text is not trying to answer you, and the work of 2021 and 2022 was inventing a training stage whose whole job is to make it try.

The one tight idea

The moment language models became commercially valuable, the papers stopped saying how they were built, so a reader of this field now needs a way to tell a measurement from a claim.

2. A technical report that reports no technique

OpenAI announced GPT-4 on 14 March 2023, and the accompanying document, called the "GPT-4 Technical Report", went up on arXiv the following day. It is a real document and anyone can read it. What matters is what it does not contain.

No parameter count. No layer count. No description of the training data. No hardware. No token budget. No training method beyond the sentence that it is a Transformer-style model pretrained to predict the next token in a document.

The report does not hide this. It states that it withholds those details, and gives two reasons: the competitive landscape and the safety implications of large models. Whether you find those reasons persuasive is a separate question from the historical fact, which is that a document titled "Technical Report" declined to report the technique.

That is a genuine rupture, and it is worth being precise about what broke. For most of the preceding decade a paper in this field told you how to rebuild the thing. The scaling bet quotes several of them doing exactly that, including a 2020 model that published a whole appendix accounting for its training computation. The trend was never perfectly smooth: the 2019 model in that same chapter never disclosed its compute, its hardware or its training time either. But from March 2023 onward the direction stopped wobbling. Frontier model architectures and training data are, as of the writing of this chapter, undisclosed as a matter of course.

This chapter is therefore the one chapter of the spine that teaches a habit rather than a mechanism. Once the primary sources become marketing documents with an evaluation appendix, the reader needs a way to sort claims, and that way has to be applied consistently rather than when something sounds suspicious.

3. Four labels worth carrying for the rest of the course

Sort every factual claim about a model into one of four boxes. The boxes are not about whether the claim is true. They are about what kind of evidence stands behind it.

KNOWN The laboratory published it, or the artefact itself proves it. The test is whether you can point at a paper, a model card, a licence file or a configuration file that states it. A number you can look up in a document is known even if the document turns out to be wrong, which is a different problem.

INFERRED A credible third party derived it from evidence, and the laboratory has neither confirmed nor denied it. The test is whether there is a named method you could re-run yourself. Inference is respectable. It is not the same as disclosure, and the two get merged constantly.

MARKETING The claim originates in a launch announcement and exists to sell something. It may well be true. The test is whether it arrives with an evaluation harness, a fixed prompt and a baseline you could reproduce. Almost no self-reported result at the frontier passes that test, which is a statement about the genre rather than about anyone's honesty.

UNVERIFIABLE Widely repeated, never confirmed, and no method exists that would settle it. This is the important one, because the honest position is not scepticism. It is that we do not know, and that the fact we do not know is itself the historically interesting thing.

Those four labels appear on this course's pages as small coloured tags, next to numbers that need them. Every lesson also carries a zone badge and an as-of date at the top, saying whether its interpretation is stable, whether it covers the last eighteen months and will be rewritten, or whether it is an open question. That is not decoration. It is the same habit applied to the course itself.

4. How a number becomes common knowledge

The worked example is the parameter count of the model announced in March 2023.

You have very likely met a figure of roughly 1.8 trillion parameters, arranged as sixteen expert sub-networks. Trace it back and it originates in unattributed industry conversation in mid-2023. It was repeated by people who were plausibly in a position to know, then reported, then cited, then treated as background fact. OpenAI has never confirmed it or denied it, and no method exists that would settle it from outside.

The label is UNVERIFIABLE, and the point of the example is not that the number is wrong. It might be right. The point is the mechanism: a claim can acquire the full social standing of a fact without ever acquiring any evidence, purely by being repeated by people who each assumed somebody upstream had checked. Once you have watched that happen once, you start noticing how often a confidently stated number in this field has no document behind it at all.

There is a discipline that goes with this, and it is the hardest sentence in this chapter to keep to. State the absence of evidence, never the motive. You can say a number was never confirmed. You can say a report declined to state something and quote the reasons it gave. You cannot say why it was really withheld, because you do not know, and a course that speculates about motive has stopped being about evidence.

5. Six questions to ask a benchmark number

What remains in a modern launch document is a table of scores, so a reader needs to know how to interrogate one. A benchmark number is a measurement made by somebody with a stake in the result. That is not an accusation; it is a description of who runs the evaluations. Six questions usually explain most of the gap between two competing claims.

  1. Which harness? The same benchmark run by two different pieces of evaluation software gives different numbers, because scoring free-form text against a reference involves choices.
  2. What scaffold? Was the model asked once, or asked repeatedly and allowed to run code, retry and check its work? Those are different systems and only one of them is the model.
  3. What prompt? Prompt wording moves scores by several points, and the prompt is frequently not published.
  4. Which baseline? A comparison against a competitor's model that the comparing team configured is not the same as one the competitor's team configured.
  5. Who ran it? Self-reported, third-party reproduced or independently audited are three quite different levels of evidence.
  6. Could the answers be in the training data? This is called contamination, and once a benchmark has been public for a few years it is nearly impossible to rule out.

Three worked examples, all of them settled history, show that none of this is new and none of it requires anyone to be acting badly.

The image-recognition result that opens the modern era, described in When meaning became geometry, is quoted in two different ways in the literature: 15.3% error in one place and 16.4% in another. Both are correct. They come from two tracks of the same competition, one of which allowed extra training data. Question four, in 2012.

The 175-billion-parameter model of 2020 states in its own paper that a bug in its contamination filtering caused some overlaps between training data and evaluation sets to be missed, and that retraining was not feasible because of the cost. Question six, admitted by the authors, and unfixable because at that scale you get one attempt.

In 2022 two laboratories published measurements of whether preference training degrades performance on academic benchmarks, and got opposite answers, as Teaching a model to be useful records. Neither was wrong. They measured at different scales with different evaluation sets, which is question one and question five at the same time.

6. One example with all four labels on it

In December 2024 a laboratory published a large model with an unusually detailed cost table, and what happened to that table afterwards is the whole chapter in miniature.

The paper states the training run consumed about 2.788 million graphics-card hours. That is KNOWN: it is a measured quantity, printed in the document, for a specific run.

The same table converts that to about 5.6 million dollars. That is INFERRED, and the paper is explicit about why: the conversion assumes a rental price of two dollars per card-hour. It is not an invoice and not what anybody paid. Change the assumption and the number changes proportionally.

The sentence that travelled the world was that a frontier model had been trained for under six million dollars. That is MARKETING and press compression, and the paper itself rules it out one paragraph later: the figure covers only the official training run, and excludes prior research, architecture and data experiments, failed runs, salaries and the cost of the hardware itself.

And the actual total cost of producing that model is UNVERIFIABLE. Nobody outside the laboratory can compute it, and the laboratory did not publish it.

One table, four labels, and every one of them defensible from the document. That is what this habit buys: not cynicism, but the ability to say precisely which part of a claim you are standing on.

The closing of the record had a second consequence, and it arrived almost immediately. If the laboratories would not tell you how their models were built, somebody was going to publish weights and let people find out for themselves. That happened within weeks, partly by accident, and The open-weights turn is what came of it.
