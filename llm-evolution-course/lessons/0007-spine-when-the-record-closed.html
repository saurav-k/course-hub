Where this lesson starts

Teaching a model to be useful ended with a research artefact becoming a product. This chapter is about what that did to the evidence, and it is the one chapter that teaches a habit rather than a mechanism.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Teaching a model to be useful A model trained only to continue text is not trying to answer you, and the work of 2021 and 2022 was inventing a training stage whose whole job is to make it try.

The one tight idea

The moment language models became commercially valuable, the papers stopped saying how they were built, so a reader of this field now needs a way to tell a measurement from a claim.

2. What this lesson must cover

To write. Full prose, complete sentences, 1,400 to 2,000 words. Every bullet below is a beat the finished lesson owes the reader, in this order.

  • The GPT-4 technical report and what it deliberately omits.
  • Four labels worth using for the rest of the course: known, inferred, marketing, unverifiable.
  • How a widely repeated number becomes common knowledge without ever being confirmed.
  • How to interrogate a benchmark claim: harness, scaffold, prompt, baseline, who ran it, contamination.
Do not overclaim

State the absence of evidence, never a motive. You can say a number was never confirmed. You cannot say why it was withheld.

4. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: The disclosure collapse Once models became commercially valuable the technical reports stopped containing technique, so from 2023 onward a reader has to sort every claim into what is known, what is inferred, what is marketing and what is unverifiable.
  • Deep dive: Reading an eval table like a scientist A benchmark number is a measurement made by someone with a stake in it, and six questions about how it was produced usually explain most of the gap between two competing claims.
