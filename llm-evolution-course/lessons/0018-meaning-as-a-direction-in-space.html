Meaning as a direction in space

Give every word a position in a few hundred dimensions, learn those positions only from the company each word keeps, and similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

Counting treats every word as unrelated to every other word, so a model that has seen dog learns nothing about cat. This lesson is about the representation that fixed that, and it is where this course teaches vectors and dot products.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • What a neural network actually is A network is a stack of two operations, multiply by a table of numbers and then bend the result, and training is nudging those numbers until a measured error gets smaller.

The one tight idea

Give every word a position in a few hundred dimensions, learn those positions only from the company each word keeps, and similarity in meaning becomes closeness in space.

2. You know a word by the company it keeps

Start with the problem, because the solution is only impressive against it.

A model that counts word sequences stores each word as its own entry in a table. Entries in a table have no distance between them, so in that model's arithmetic dog and cat are exactly as unrelated as dog and Tuesday. Having seen "the cat is walking in the bedroom" ten thousand times tells such a model nothing whatever about "a dog was running in a room", and any word run it has never seen has a probability of zero. With a vocabulary of a hundred thousand words there are, as the 2003 paper that first attacked this properly points out, potentially one followed by fifty zeros worth of ten-word sequences. You cannot collect your way out of that. This is called the curse of dimensionality, and The statistical turn: guessing the next word is where it bites.

The way out has been available as a slogan since the 1950s, from the linguist J. R. Firth: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Cat and dog both turn up after the, before barked or slept, near vet and food and walk, and Tuesday does not. Nobody has to define what a cat is; the pattern of contexts already separates them.

That is the distributional hypothesis, and turning it into something a computer can use requires one further move: stop storing a word as a name and start storing it as a position.

3. A word as a point, and a dot product as a similarity

Two numbers give you a point on a page and three give you a point in a room, and nothing stops you writing down three hundred numbers and calling that a point too. This course teaches vectors here because this is where the story needs them.

Suppose, in a made-up two-dimensional space, that cat sits at (0.9, 0.8), dog at (0.8, 0.9), and Tuesday at (-0.7, 0.6). To ask how similar two of them are, multiply the two lists position by position and add up the results. That single operation is called the dot product, and it is the same weighted sum that What a neural network actually is describes as the only arithmetic in the subject.

cat · dog = 0.9×0.8 + 0.8×0.9 = 0.72 + 0.72 = 1.44

cat · Tuesday = 0.9×(-0.7) + 0.8×0.6 = -0.63 + 0.48 = -0.15

A large positive number means the two point the same way, a number near zero means they are unrelated, and a negative one means they point apart. In practice the score is usually divided by the lengths of the two lists so that only the direction counts, which is called cosine similarity, but the operation underneath is the multiply-and-add above.

Notice what has been bought: in a table, "how similar are these two words" is not a question you can ask at all, and in a space it is one line of arithmetic.

4. Bengio 2003, and why almost nobody had these

The idea was set out properly by Yoshua Bengio, Rejean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent and Christian Jauvin in "A Neural Probabilistic Language Model", published in the Journal of Machine Learning Research in February 2003.

Their proposal is three steps and it is worth having in this order. Give every word in the vocabulary a list of real numbers. Express the probability of a word sequence in terms of those lists rather than in terms of the words. Learn the lists and the probability function at the same time, from the same loss. Nobody chooses the numbers; they are weights like any other, trained by the loop in What a neural network actually is. Their vocabulary was seventeen thousand words and the lists were thirty, sixty or a hundred numbers long: a word needs far fewer numbers than there are words.

The paper's own statement of why it works is the whole idea in one sentence: a sequence of words that has never been seen before gets a high probability if it is made of words that are similar to words forming an already seen sentence. Their worked example is the best one anybody has produced. Train on "The cat is walking in the bedroom" and, because dog sits near cat, was near is, running near walking and room near bedroom, the model has already raised the probability of "A dog was running in a room", and of a combinatorial number of other sentences it has never seen. One training sentence informs the model about an exponential number of neighbouring sentences.

It worked, too: against the best n-gram model they could build, the neural model's perplexity was about twenty-four per cent lower on one corpus and about eight per cent lower on another. And almost nobody used it for a decade, because training it over a large vocabulary meant an enormous amount of arithmetic per word, and in 2003 the machines described in 2012: the year the hardware caught up did not exist. The idea was correct, published, and unaffordable.

5. word2vec: throw away the part you do not need

On 16 January 2013, three months after AlexNet, Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeff Dean published "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space".

Their insight is an act of subtraction. If what you want is the word vectors rather than a good language model, you do not need most of the network that Bengio's design carried in order to be a good language model. Strip out the expensive middle and keep a very simple objective linking a word to the words around it.

They offer two shapes of that objective, and the two are mirror images. Continuous bag of words takes the words surrounding a position and predicts the word in the middle. Skip-gram takes the word in the middle and predicts the words surrounding it. Either way, the only training signal is a window of a few words sliding across ordinary text, and every step nudges the vectors of words that occur together closer to one another. No labels, no dictionary, no grammar, no annotation of any kind.

The result was cheapness, and cheapness is what changed the field. The paper reports that it takes less than a day to learn high-quality word vectors from a data set of 1.6 billion words, with the main models trained on a Google News corpus of about six billion tokens and a vocabulary of the million commonest words. Suddenly everyone could have word vectors for their own text, and within a year almost everyone did.

6. GloVe reaches the same place by another road

In October 2014 Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher and Christopher Manning published GloVe, for Global Vectors, at a conference in Doha, and it is worth knowing because it arrives at word vectors from the opposite direction.

Where word2vec slides a window and never looks at the corpus as a whole, GloVe starts from a single global count: for every pair of words, how often does one appear in the context of the other, across the entire corpus. Then it fits vectors to those counts directly.

Their motivating example is the clearest short argument for the whole approach. Take ice and steam, and ask how often each appears near four other words, in a corpus of six billion tokens.

From the GloVe paper: how often ice and steam appear near four probe words, and the ratio between them
Probe wordNear iceNear steamRatio
solid1.9 × 10-42.2 × 10-58.9
gas6.6 × 10-57.8 × 10-40.085
water3.0 × 10-32.2 × 10-31.36
fashion1.7 × 10-51.8 × 10-50.96

Read the last column, not the first two. Individually the raw probabilities are hard to interpret, but the ratio is startlingly clean: large for a word that belongs to ice and not steam, small for one that belongs to steam and not ice, and close to one for words such as water and fashion that relate to both or to neither. Nothing in that table is a definition of ice, and yet the column has separated a physical property from an irrelevance using nothing but counts.

7. The famous analogy, told accurately

The result everybody quotes is that king minus man plus woman lands near queen. It is real, and almost every retelling of it gets at least one thing wrong.

It is not in the word2vec paper. It appears in "Linguistic Regularities in Continuous Space Word Representations" by Tomas Mikolov, Wen-tau Yih and Geoffrey Zweig, presented at a conference in Atlanta in June 2013, and the sentence in the abstract is that the male-female relationship is automatically learned, and that with the induced vector representations "King - Man + Woman" results in a vector very close to "Queen".

Two details in that paper are routinely lost. It came out of Microsoft Research, not Google. And the vectors it analysed came from a recurrent neural network language model, not from word2vec at all: they were produced by a toolkit trained on 320 million words of broadcast news with an 82,000-word vocabulary. The analogy result is older than the tool it is usually credited to and was found in a different kind of model, which is a cheap and useful lesson in how folk history compresses.

The mechanism is simple enough to state without hedging. If the difference between man and woman is a particular direction and distance, and the difference between king and queen happens to be roughly the same one, then stepping from king by that offset lands near queen. Nobody built that in; it is a consequence of those four words having the contextual company they do.

Now the honest part, which the retellings skip. These papers all report accuracy on a test set of thousands of such questions, and the accuracies are nothing like perfect. The recurrent-network vectors in the 2013 analogy paper answered 39.6 per cent of the syntactic questions correctly, which its authors describe, fairly, as more than one in three and far better than the alternatives of the day. Skip-gram at three hundred dimensions scored 53.3 per cent overall on the word2vec paper's own test set of 8,869 semantic and 10,675 syntactic questions. GloVe at three hundred dimensions, trained on 42 billion tokens, reached 75.0 per cent.

So roughly a quarter of these questions were still answered wrongly by the best system in the literature. King and queen is a genuine result and it is also the example that works, chosen from a set where a great many do not. It is evidence that the space has usable structure in it, not that the model has learned what royalty or gender are.

8. What this actually dissolved

Return to the ceiling in section 2, because this is the payoff. The curse of dimensionality is not really about how many sequences there are. It is about the fact that a model storing words as separate entries cannot transfer anything it learned about one sequence to a similar one. Every sequence is an island.

Positions dissolve that. Similar words are now literally close together, so a small change to a sentence produces a small change in the numbers going into the model, and therefore a small change in what comes out. A model that has seen one sentence has been informed about all the sentences near it. Generalisation stops being something you hope for and becomes a property of the representation.

Be careful about what to call this. The model does not understand the words. What it has is a space whose geometry places words that behave alike near each other, learned from nothing but which words turn up together, and that turns out to be enormously useful. Anything more than that is a claim the evidence does not support.

9. What it owes the next step

Two debts, and the field has spent the years since paying both.

The first is that one vector per word is not enough. Bank gets a single position, so the river and the money have to share it, and whatever the space settles on is a blend of the two that is right for neither. A word's meaning depends on the sentence it is in, and a fixed table of positions cannot express that. Everything this course later calls a contextual representation exists to fix it.

The second is more mundane. A fixed vocabulary means a fixed list of words, and real text is full of things that are on no list: names, misspellings, compounds, other languages, code. A word that is not in the table has no position at all. Tokens: how text becomes numbers is how that was solved, by giving up on words as the unit entirely, and it is the layer that every model in the rest of this course actually reads.

Retrieval practice

What decides where a word ends up in an embedding space?

Why does representing words as positions dissolve the curse of dimensionality?
Because similar words sit close together, what the model learned from one sentence transfers to the sentences near it, instead of every sequence being an island.
