Who ships open weights now

The set of organisations publishing frontier-adjacent open weights has changed completely since 2023, and the change is a fact about release artefacts rather than a story about intentions.

Moving Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Next review due February 2027. The last eighteen months. Rewritten freely, and expected to be wrong before it is old.
Where this lesson starts

The leak that started an ecosystem and Four axes of open: weights, code, data, licence establish what open means and where the ecosystem came from. This lesson is a dated snapshot of who is actually shipping, and it is written to be replaced rather than amended.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • Four axes of open: weights, code, data, licence Open is not one question but four independent ones, almost nothing scores well on all four, and the word is now used for releases that would fail three of them.

The one tight idea

The set of organisations publishing frontier-adjacent open weights has changed completely since 2023, and the change is a fact about release artefacts rather than a story about intentions.

2. Measure it, do not assert it

"Who is publishing open weights" is the kind of question people answer from impression, and impressions about this field are usually eighteen months out of date. It is also, unusually, a question with a countable answer, because an open-weight release leaves an artefact in a public place with a timestamp on it.

The method this lesson uses is deliberately unglamorous. Query a public model host for everything a given organisation has published, sorted newest first, and read the dates. That is one request. It returns files rather than opinions, and anyone can repeat it.

Five things this measurement cannot tell you

A repository's creation date is not a launch date. Laboratories routinely create a repository days or weeks before making it public, so these dates run slightly early and are not comparable to press-release dates. Deciding what counts as a frontier-adjacent language model is a judgement. The filter below keeps general-purpose models and drops safety classifiers, speech models, vision encoders and quantized copies of models already counted. An absence of artefacts is only an absence of artefacts. It says nothing about why, and this lesson does not speculate: reasons are unverifiable in the sense The disclosure collapse sets out.

A publisher may hold models under more than one organisation name. Querying only the historically-known name misses a rename, and this page got that wrong before it was corrected: Meta's Llama releases sit under meta-llama and its Muse releases under meta-models, so a query for the first name alone reports a silence that the record does not contain. And the newest artefact is often a dated refresh rather than a new model. DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813 is an August 2026 refresh of a model announced in April 2026, so the date beside it is the date of that refresh. This table records the newest artefact by design, which is the right choice for a question about who is still shipping and the wrong one for a question about when a model first appeared.

Read the table as two separate findings rather than one.

Trillion-scale open weights are now published almost entirely by Chinese laboratories

Five organisations - DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot, Z.ai and MiniMax - published at the very top of the open-weight range through 2026, on a cadence of roughly one significant release each per quarter. Between February and August 2026 that group shipped GLM-5, GLM-5.1 and GLM-5.2; Kimi K2.5, K2.6 and K3; MiniMax M2.5, M2.7, M3 and H3; Qwen3.6 and Qwen3.8; and DeepSeek-V4 in two sizes with refreshes.

Nobody else is publishing weights at that scale. That is the finding, and it is narrow on purpose.

The Western laboratories did not leave - they moved down the size range

This is the part that the popular version of the story usually loses, and it matters because it changes what the trend means.

Meta is the sharpest case, because of who Meta is in this story. Its index, sorted newest first, runs Muse Glimmer on 10 August 2026, and below that two prompt-guard classifiers on 28 April 2025, a safety classifier on 23 April, and Llama 4 Scout and Maverick on 3 and 4 April 2025. There is no Llama 5. So the organisation whose accidental release created the open-weight ecosystem in the first place, and whose deliberate release of Llama 2 five months later made it legitimate - the sequence The leak that started an ecosystem tells - went sixteen months without publishing anything at frontier scale, and then returned with a 30-billion-parameter dense model under Apache 2.0. That is a return down the size range rather than back to the top of it.

Meta's flagship of that generation is a separate object and this table does not contain it. Muse Spark launched on 8 April 2026 as a closed model, and on the day Muse Glimmer shipped its founder committed to publishing open weights for Muse Spark 1.2. As of 22 August 2026 that commitment had not produced a release, so it is a statement rather than an artefact, and the distinction is the whole method of this page. Do not read Muse Glimmer's Apache licence as saying anything about Muse Spark.

Google shipped the Gemma 4 family in June 2026, in sizes up to roughly 31 billion parameters, with quantization-aware variants intended to run on a phone. Mistral shipped mid-size models through 2026. OpenAI shipped Apache-2.0 weights once, in August 2025, and its 120-billion-parameter release was post-trained in a 4-bit format specifically so that it fits on a single 80-gigabyte accelerator - a design goal about who can run it rather than about how well it scores.

So the accurate statement is not "the West stopped publishing open weights". It is that the open-weight market split by size: small and mid-size models come from everywhere, and the trillion-parameter open tier is Chinese.

4. How far behind is the open frontier?

Counting releases says who is shipping. It says nothing about whether what they ship is any good, and that needs a measurement with a stated method.

Epoch AI publishes one. It maintains a composite score called the Epoch Capabilities Index, built by aggregating public benchmark results, and it uses that index to compare the best open-weight model available at a given date against the best closed model.

Its finding, published on 29 May 2026: since January 2026 the most capable open-weight models have lagged frontier closed models by an average of four months, an average gap of 8 index points with a 90 percent confidence interval of 7 to 11. For scale, Epoch describes 8 points as "similar to the gap between GPT-5 and GPT-5.5" - which is to say, one point release rather than one generation. An earlier Epoch estimate covering the period to October 2025 put the lag at around three months, so on their own numbers the gap has widened slightly rather than closed.

That figure is inferred, not known, and Epoch says why themselves. Two limitations, both pushing the same way:

  • Open-weight models "tend to perform worse on private benchmarks compared to closed models, plausibly because they more aggressively hillclimb on public benchmarks" - which is the contamination and overfitting problem that Reading an eval table like a scientist works through.
  • Only models with enough public benchmark coverage can be scored at all, and "leading closed labs do not always release their most capable models".

Both mean the real gap is probably larger than four months, not smaller. Use the number, name the method, and do not round it to "open has caught up".

5. Check whether this page is still true

This lesson has a review date on it because it is a snapshot, and a snapshot of a fast-moving thing is wrong before it is old. So rather than asking you to trust it, here is the procedure to re-run it.

  1. Query the host, once per organisation name. Request one organisation's models from the public index, sorted by creation date, newest first. The index accepts an author name and a sort order, and returns identifiers and dates as data. The source list below gives the exact form of the request used for this page. Enumerate the names before you start, because a publisher may hold models under several: querying meta-llama alone misses everything under meta-models, which is where Meta's 2026 releases are.
  2. Filter. Keep general-purpose language models. Drop safety and moderation classifiers, speech and vision models, and quantized or format-converted copies of a model already in the list.
  3. Record the newest survivor and its date, for each of the nine publishers in the table above.
  4. Compare with the table, and check the scale as well as the date. If a publisher's newest entry is later than the one printed here, this page is out of date on that row. The findings in section 3 are about size rather than about dates, so what falsifies them is a Western laboratory publishing at the trillion-parameter tier, or the Chinese laboratories stopping - not a row moving by a month.
  5. Re-read the capability gap. Epoch's data insight carries its own update date and downloadable data, so check whether the four-month figure has moved and whether the limitations paragraph still says what section 4 quotes.

Two things to be careful about while doing it. A publisher can go quiet for six months and return, so a single gap is not a trend, and a return is not the end of a trend either - Meta's sixteen-month absence ended in August 2026 at a fifth of the size it left at. And an organisation may publish under more than one account name, so enumerate the names before you record a silence. An earlier version of this page reported that Meta had not shipped since April 2025, because it queried meta-llama and never asked for meta-models. A reproducible method that is reproducibly wrong is worse than an unsourced guess, because the author and the reader check it and both get the same answer.

The wider point is the one to keep once the numbers have moved on. The most reliable facts about this field are the ones that are properties of artefacts rather than statements by people, and a release date is about as artefactual as it gets. When you next meet a confident claim about who is winning at open models, the fastest way to test it is to go and look at what has actually been published, and when.

Retrieval practice

Why is counting release artefacts a better method than describing the trend?
The whole procedure returns timestamps on public files, so the page can be re-run by a sceptical reader in minutes.
What is the accurate way to state what happened to Western open-weight releases?
Google shipped Gemma 4 up to about 31B and Mistral shipped mid-size models, so the split is by scale rather than by geography until the very top.
Why does this lesson report an absence rather than a reason for it?
Sixteen months without a frontier release is a countable fact; why a company did not ship is unverifiable from outside.
Why are the stated limitations on the four-month gap important?
Public-benchmark hillclimbing and unreleased closed models each flatter the open side, which is why the figure must not be rounded to a claim of parity.
