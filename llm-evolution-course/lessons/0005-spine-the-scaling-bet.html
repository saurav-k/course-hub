The scaling bet

Between 2018 and 2022 the field discovered that the loss of a language model falls along a straight line as you spend more, which turned model building from a craft into a budgeting exercise, and then discovered the first version of that line was wrong.

Where this lesson starts

The Transformer turn left two architectures and no idea how big to make them. This chapter is about the four years in which the answer turned out to be a forecast rather than an intuition.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

The one tight idea

Between 2018 and 2022 the field discovered that the loss of a language model falls along a straight line as you spend more, which turned model building from a craft into a budgeting exercise, and then discovered the first version of that line was wrong.

2. Pay once, specialise cheaply

Before 2018 the economics of language work were the wrong way round. Every task had its own model, its own architecture and its own hand-labelled dataset, and labelled data was the scarce thing. If you wanted a system that classified support tickets and a system that answered questions, you paid twice, from scratch, and each one was only as good as the labels you could afford.

The idea that inverted this is called pretraining, and it has one moving part. Predicting the next word needs no labels at all, because the next word is already there in the text. So you can train one large model on an enormous quantity of ordinary text, for a long time, at great expense, once. Then, for each task, you take that model and adjust it slightly using a small number of labelled examples. The expensive step happens once instead of once per task, and the cheap step is cheap enough that a single graphics card will do it.

Jeremy Howard and Sebastian Ruder named the ambition in January 2018, arguing that language modelling is the ideal source task for text and the counterpart of what ImageNet had been for images. In June 2018 OpenAI applied it to the Transformer's decoder half, and in October 2018 Google applied it to the encoder half; The Transformer turn covers that fork and why the encoder half won at first.

One detail from the June 2018 model is worth carrying, because it is the first serious statement that data quality is not the same as data quantity. It was trained on over seven thousand unpublished books rather than on a same-sized collection of shuffled sentences, and the paper says why: the books contain long stretches of contiguous text, so the model can learn to depend on things it read a page ago. The shuffled corpus had the same number of words and destroyed that structure. Same quantity, different value.

3. GPT-2, and the bet that scale removes the second step

On 14 February 2019 OpenAI published a model called GPT-2 with a deliberately provocative title: "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners". The claim was that if you make the decoder-only model large enough and train it on enough text, the specialising step starts to become unnecessary. You do not fine-tune it to summarise. You write the article and then the word "TL;DR:" and it summarises, because documents on the internet that contain that string are usually followed by a summary. The task description has been smuggled into the text itself.

The paper reported four sizes, and the numbers it printed were wrong. It gives 117M, 345M, 762M and 1542M parameters, and every released checkpoint is named 124M, 355M, 774M and 1558M. OpenAI corrected it in the repository, writing that the original parameter counts were wrong due to an error in the previous blog posts and paper. Use the corrected numbers, and treat this as a small reminder that primary sources contain mistakes too.

The paper is also honest about something that usually gets dropped in retellings: all four models were still improving when training stopped. It says so twice. GPT-2 was not limited by how much text existed. It was limited by how long anyone was willing to wait.

And then OpenAI did not release it. Only the smallest model went out in February, with the larger ones following in May, August and November as the organisation ran partner studies and published a report on the trade-offs. This was the first time a major laboratory withheld a trained model on the grounds that releasing it might be harmful, and the argument it started has not ended. Note the wording carefully: OpenAI never said the model was too dangerous to release. That phrase is press shorthand. What they wrote was that they were not releasing the trained model, because of concerns about large language models being used to generate deceptive, biased or abusive language at scale.

4. Where the data actually came from

The usual mental picture is that there is a giant pile of internet text somewhere and the laboratories download it. By 2020 that picture is roughly right. In 2019 it was wrong in every particular, and the difference is worth getting exactly right, because it is one of the most commonly garbled facts in this whole story.

GPT-2's training set was called WebText and OpenAI built it themselves. They considered the obvious alternative and rejected it in print: Common Crawl, they wrote, has significant data quality issues, and they had seen the same problems in their own experiments with it.

So they built a filter out of other people's attention. Reddit is a link-sharing site where people post links and other people vote them up. OpenAI scraped every outbound link that had accumulated at least three net upvotes, on the reasoning that an upvote is a cheap signal that some human found the page interesting, educational or funny. That gave 45 million links. They pulled the text out of those pages, removed duplicates, removed every Wikipedia document so that later evaluations would not be contaminated by overlap, and ended with slightly over eight million documents totalling 40 gigabytes of text.

Three corrections fall out of that paragraph. GPT-2 was not trained on Reddit; OpenAI's own model card says WebText does not consist of data taken directly from Reddit itself. It was trained on the articles Reddit users linked to, with Reddit acting as the recommendation engine rather than the content. It was not trained on Common Crawl, which is a separate non-profit that has been archiving the web since 2008 and gives its archive away free; that organisation is real, and it fed the next model rather than this one. And it did not come from Hugging Face, which in early 2019 was a small company six months into maintaining a port of Google's encoder model to a different framework, and whose dataset library would not exist for another year. Hugging Face is the front door most people walk through to meet GPT-2 today, which is exactly why it gets credited with the house.

The reason this matters beyond pedantry is what happened next. When OpenAI came back to Common Crawl for the following model, they needed a definition of clean text, and they used WebText as that definition: they trained a classifier to tell WebText-like documents from raw crawl, and used it to re-weight 45 terabytes of compressed web pages down to 570 gigabytes. A Reddit upvote from 2018 is therefore still, at two removes, deciding what a 2020 model was allowed to read.

5. A straight line you can forecast with

Training a large model is a one-shot bet. You choose the size, spend the money, wait, and find out. Until 2020 there was no principled way to choose.

In January 2020 Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish and colleagues published a set of measurements that changed that. Train many models at many sizes on many amounts of data, plot the resulting error against the resources spent, and use logarithmic axes on both. What appears is a straight line: over more than six orders of magnitude, the loss falls as a smooth power of the model size, of the amount of data and of the computation spent.

A straight line on a log-log plot is a forecasting instrument. You can run a handful of small, cheap experiments, fit the line, extend it, and read off what a run a thousand times larger would achieve before you pay for it. The paper also found that this dominated everything else: within reasonable limits, performance depends very weakly on architectural choices such as depth against width, and very strongly on scale. That finding is why the field largely stopped inventing architectures and started buying hardware.

Be careful about what a scaling law is. It is an empirical fit over the range that was actually measured. It is not a law of nature, it promises nothing outside that range, and the authors said so themselves: their own paper contains a section noting that two of their curves eventually cross, and that where they cross is highly sensitive to the exact exponents. They flagged the weakness. The field read the headline. The scaling laws lesson in the papers course works through the actual relationships.

6. GPT-3, and learning that leaves no trace

In May 2020 OpenAI acted on the forecast. GPT-3 was 175 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens, at a cost the paper states as roughly three times ten to the twenty-third floating-point operations, which was an enormous number at the time. Architecturally almost nothing had changed since GPT-2. It was the same design, much larger, on more data.

What surprised people was not the fluency. It was a behaviour the paper calls in-context learning. Give the finished model a few worked examples inside the prompt itself, then ask your real question, and it does measurably better than if you had just asked. No weights change. Training is over. The examples are consumed and forgotten when the answer ends. And the effect grows with model size, which is what made it look like a property of scale rather than a trick.

Two weeks later, on 11 June 2020, OpenAI stopped releasing weights and launched a paid interface instead. Their post gave three reasons, and all three are worth stating because each one is separately arguable: commercial revenue funds the research, the models are too large and expensive for most organisations to run themselves, and access that goes through a service can be withdrawn from someone misusing it while released weights cannot. A reader who repeats only the first reason is being cynical, and one who repeats only the third is being credulous.

7. The first forecast held the wrong thing fixed

Kaplan's recipe told you how to spend a fixed budget: put almost all of it into a bigger model, and increase the data only slightly. The whole industry did exactly that for two years, building models of 175, 280 and 530 billion parameters.

In March 2022 a DeepMind team led by Jordan Hoffmann, Sebastian Borgeaud and Arthur Mensch trained over four hundred models and reported that the recipe was wrong. For a fixed budget, model size and training data should be scaled roughly equally: double the model, double the tokens. Their demonstration model, Chinchilla, had 70 billion parameters trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, used the same budget as a 280-billion-parameter model trained the old way, and beat it, along with everything else of that generation.

The reason the first answer was wrong is the best part, and it is specific rather than vague. Kaplan's experiments used one learning-rate schedule, set for a long run, and read the loss off at earlier points along that curve. But a model that was always going to stop early should have had its schedule set for the shorter run, and would have finished at a lower loss than the mid-run reading suggests. Every small-data measurement was therefore pessimistic, data looked less valuable than it is, and the fit tilted towards parameters. One mismatched schedule cost the industry two years and a great deal of money, and the correction is documented in the primary source rather than inferred.

The verdict on the preceding generation is stark when you write it as tokens per parameter, which is the number practitioners actually remember:

The largest model anyone had built was the worst offender. GPT-3 had been trained on roughly one twelfth of the data it should have had.

Once the correction landed, everything changed shape. Models got smaller and were trained for much longer, which is also cheaper to run afterwards, and the binding constraint moved from computation to text. The Chinchilla lesson next door works through the three separate methods that produced the same answer.

By mid-2022 the field could forecast a model's loss and knew how to spend a budget properly. It still had a model that was extremely good at predicting text and awkward to actually use, and closing that gap is a different problem entirely. Teaching a model to be useful is what closed it.

8. Where this hands off

This course owns chronology, causation, people and dates. It hands off for mechanism, maths and code rather than re-deriving them.

  • Deep dive: GPT-2 and the bet on scale Make the decoder much bigger and train it on much more text, and the fine-tuning step starts to become unnecessary: you describe the task in the prompt instead.
  • Deep dive: Where the data actually came from The dataset behind GPT-2 was scraped by its authors and filtered by a proxy for human approval, it was not a public crawl, and that filtering idea outlived the model that introduced it.
  • Deep dive: The model they would not release The first time a lab withheld a trained model on the grounds that releasing it might be harmful, it set the terms of an argument the field is still having.
  • Deep dive: Predicting the future with a straight line Model loss falls as a power law in size, data and compute, which means a straight line on a log-log plot, which means you can forecast the result of a run you have not paid for.
  • Deep dive: GPT-3, and learning without learning A model whose weights never change gets better at a task when you put a few worked examples in the prompt, which is a kind of learning that leaves no trace in the model.
  • Deep dive: Chinchilla, the correction, and data as the new constraint The first scaling recipe held the wrong thing fixed, so the models built on it were far larger than they needed to be and far less trained than they should have been, and once that was corrected the scarce resource became text.
