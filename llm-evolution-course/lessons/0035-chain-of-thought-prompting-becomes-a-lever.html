Chain of thought: prompting becomes a lever

Asking a large model to write out its working before answering improves its accuracy on multi-step problems, which means the prompt is not just a request but a control on how much computation the model spends.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson is about a discovery made by changing nothing but the input. It assumes you know that a model's weights are frozen at inference time and that a prompt can contain examples.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

  • GPT-3, and learning without learning A model whose weights never change gets better at a task when you put a few worked examples in the prompt, which is a kind of learning that leaves no trace in the model.

The one tight idea

Asking a large model to write out its working before answering improves its accuracy on multi-step problems, which means the prompt is not just a request but a control on how much computation the model spends.

2. One change, and it was not to the model

In January 2022 a group at Google Research posted a paper that reported a large improvement on multi-step problems without training anything. The models were the ones that already existed. The questions were the ones that already existed. The only thing that changed was the shape of the worked examples placed in front of the question.

The setting is the one GPT-3, and learning without learning describes: a model whose weights are frozen gets better at a task when you put a few worked examples into its input, a behaviour usually called in-context learning. The usual worked example is a pair. Here is a question, here is the answer. Do the same for mine.

Jason Wei and his colleagues changed the pair into a triple. Here is a question, here is the working, here is the answer. They called the middle part a chain of thought, and they hand-wrote exactly eight of these triples and reused that same set across the free-response arithmetic benchmarks. The prompt is longer. Nothing else about the system is different.

Their own example, which is worth reading slowly, is a question about tennis balls. Under the ordinary format the model is shown a question and the bare sentence "The answer is 11", and it then answers the real question with a bare sentence too, and gets it wrong. Under the new format the model is shown the same question followed by "Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11", and it then writes out its own working for the real question, and gets it right.

3. The gain is real, and it is not available to everyone

On GSM8K, a set of grade-school maths word problems, the largest model in the paper went from 17.9 percent correct with ordinary prompting to 56.9 percent with chains of thought. That was, at the time, the best published result on the benchmark, and the paper is careful to say what it beat: a smaller model that had been fine-tuned on the training set and paired with a separate verifier scored 33, and the previous best result of any kind was 55. Eight examples in a prompt had just overtaken a purpose-built trained system.

The second result is the one that matters more for the rest of this course, and it is a result about size. The paper ran the same comparison across every model size it had access to, and the two prompt formats do not separate until the models get large. Below roughly ten billion parameters, showing the working does not help. It usually hurts.

It produces something that has the shape of reasoning, with steps and arithmetic and a confident final sentence, and the steps are wrong, so a format that was supposed to help has instead given it more places to fail.

4. Why writing it out might help

The paper offers an explanation and is careful to label it as a property rather than a proof. Its first stated benefit is that a chain of thought "allows models to decompose multi-step problems into intermediate steps, which means that additional computation can be allocated to problems that require more reasoning steps".

That sentence is the reason this lesson exists, so it is worth unpacking in plain terms.

A model that answers immediately does a fixed amount of work. It reads the question, runs it once through a fixed stack of layers, and emits a token. A hard question and an easy question get exactly the same amount of computation, because the amount of computation is a property of the architecture rather than of the problem.

A model that writes out fifty tokens of working before answering runs that stack fifty more times. Each generated token is also fed back in, so the intermediate results are not held in the model at all: they are written down in the text, where the next step can read them. Two things therefore change at once. There is more computation, and there is an external place to keep partial results.

The paper's own ablation supports the second half of that. If the benefit were merely that the model produces the arithmetic to be evaluated, then prompting it to output the equation alone should work as well. It does not: equation-only prompting "does not help much for GSM8K", because in these problems the hard part is turning the English into an equation, not evaluating one. The natural-language steps are doing work that the equation cannot.

5. One sentence instead of eight examples

Four months later, in May 2022, a group at the University of Tokyo and Google Research reported that most of the effect survives when you delete the examples entirely.

Takeshi Kojima and colleagues appended a single instruction to the question - "Let's think step by step" - with no worked examples at all, and measured the same kind of jump. On MultiArith, accuracy went from 17.7 to 78.7 percent. On GSM8K it went from 10.4 to 40.7. Both figures are for the same model, an InstructGPT checkpoint, and the paper reports similar movements on a 540-billion-parameter model.

This is a stranger result than the first one, and it is worth saying why. The eight-example version has an obvious mechanism: the model is a text continuer, it was shown text that contains reasoning, so it continues in kind. The one-sentence version has no examples to imitate. Whatever produces the working is already inside the model, and the instruction only selects it. The paper's title says as much: large language models are zero-shot reasoners, meaning the capability did not have to be demonstrated, only requested.

The practical consequence arrived immediately. A capability that needs eight hand-written examples is a research technique. A capability that needs one sentence is something every user of a chat interface can stumble into, and within a year "think step by step" was folk knowledge.

6. Two arguments that this lesson must not settle

Both of them are still live, and a course that reports the result without them would be teaching a nicer story than the evidence supports.

Is the jump at a certain size a real change in kind?

In June 2022 several of the same authors published a paper naming this pattern. It defines an ability as emergent if "it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models", and argues that such abilities cannot be predicted by extrapolating from smaller runs - which is a direct challenge to the smooth-curve picture that Predicting the future with a straight line describes.

In April 2023 Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda and Sanmi Koyejo at Stanford published a rebuttal. Their claim is not that the measurements are wrong but that the sharpness is an artefact of the ruler: a metric that awards nothing unless every step of a multi-step answer is right will look like a sudden switch, while a continuous metric over the same fixed model outputs shows a smooth improvement. They demonstrate this by choosing metrics that manufacture apparently emergent abilities in vision models where nobody claimed any.

What is not in dispute is the measurement in the table above. What is in dispute is whether the shape of that curve tells you something about the model or something about how the score was computed, which is the subject of Reading an eval table like a scientist.

Is the written reasoning the reason for the answer?

The chain-of-thought paper claims a chain "provides an interpretable window into the behavior of the model", and then immediately concedes that "fully characterizing a model's computations that support an answer remains an open question". That hedge turned out to be load-bearing.

In May 2023 Miles Turpin and colleagues at New York University tested it directly. They biased the input in a way the model could not honestly ignore - for example, reordering a multiple-choice prompt so that the correct answer in every worked example happened to be option (A) - and then measured what the model said about its own reasoning. The models followed the bias, produced fluent step-by-step justifications for the answer the bias pointed at, and never mentioned the bias. Across thirteen tasks the manipulation cost as much as 36 percent accuracy, with both a GPT-3.5 model and Claude 1.0.

So a chain of thought is a text the model generates. It is not a transcript of the computation that produced the answer, and it can be a confident rationalisation of an answer arrived at for a reason the text does not mention. Hold both facts at once: the working improves accuracy, and reading the working does not tell you why.

7. What this handed to the next few years

The narrow reading of these papers is that a certain prompt format helps on arithmetic. The wide reading is the one that mattered.

Until 2022 the levers a lab could pull were the model, the data and the training run, and every one of them cost a fortune and took months. Chain-of-thought prompting demonstrated a fourth lever that costs nothing, is available at inference time, and works on a model you did not train: how many tokens the model is allowed to spend before answering. Once you can see that dial, an obvious question follows. If asking politely for more tokens buys accuracy, what happens if you train a model to use those tokens well, and let it spend as many as the problem needs?

That question is the whole of Thinking is just more tokens, and the reasoning models of 2025 are its answer. It is also why this small 2022 result sits in a course about how language models happened: it is the moment the prompt stopped being a request and became a control.

Retrieval practice

What was changed in the chain-of-thought experiment?
Nothing was trained and nothing was added to the system; only the shape of the examples placed in the input changed.
Why does showing the working make a small model worse?
PaLM 8B falls from 4.9 to 4.1 because the format gives it more places to go wrong, not because it is confused about the task.
In what sense is a prompt a control on computation?
Each generated token is fed back as input, so asking for working buys extra runs of the network and an external place to hold partial results.
What did the experiment on faithfulness demonstrate?
The written steps are generated text, so they can be a plausible rationalisation of an answer reached for a reason the text never states.
