Machines that talk without understanding

You can produce convincing language with pattern matching and no model of meaning at all, and people will supply the understanding themselves.

Settled Accurate as of 21 August 2026. Interpretation is stable. Corrected by appending, never rewritten to fit a new event.
Where this lesson starts

This lesson assumes nothing. It is about the first programs that handled human language, why they were built out of hand-written rules, and what those rules could never reach.

1. Where this lesson sits

What you need first

This lesson has no prerequisites in this course. It assumes only curiosity, and it states its own starting point above.

The one tight idea

You can produce convincing language with pattern matching and no model of meaning at all, and people will supply the understanding themselves.

2. A foreigner with a very good ear

In January 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum published a description of a program called ELIZA in Communications of the ACM. It ran on the time-sharing system at MIT, it held conversations in English through a teletype, and it contained no model of the world whatsoever.

Weizenbaum opens the paper with a warning about his own subject. "It is said that to explain is to explain away", he writes, and he notes that once a program's inner workings are set out plainly, its magic crumbles and the observer says to himself "I could have written that". He then explains ELIZA anyway, on purpose, because the reaction to it had already got out of hand.

His own way of describing the mechanism is the best one anybody has found, and it is worth taking at face value. Imagine a foreigner with a limited knowledge of English but a very good ear. He hears you say "I am very unhappy these days" and picks out only the first two words. Wanting to seem interested, he replies "How long have you been very unhappy these days?". He has not understood a word after "I am". What he has done is apply a template that matched "I am" and isolated the rest, and then a fixed frame that turns any sentence of the form "I am BLAH" into "How long have you been BLAH", independently of the meaning of BLAH.

That is the whole of ELIZA. It is not a simplification of ELIZA, it is Weizenbaum's own account of the design.

3. Keyword, decomposition, reassembly

The formal machinery has three parts, and all three are lists written by a person.

First, a keyword list. ELIZA scans your sentence from left to right and looks each word up in a dictionary of keywords. Only the rules attached to a keyword it found are worth trying, which is what keeps the program from testing every rule it owns against every sentence. Keywords carry a rank, so that when a sentence contains several, the program knows which one to build its reply around.

Second, a decomposition rule, which is a pattern that cuts the sentence into numbered pieces. Weizenbaum's own worked example in the paper is the sentence "It seems that you hate me", handled by the rule written (0 YOU 0 ME). The zeros mean "any number of words at all", so the rule reads "anything, then you, then anything, then me", and it splits the sentence into four numbered pieces: It seems that, you, hate, me.

Third, a reassembly rule, which is a fixed frame with numbered slots. The frame (WHAT MAKES YOU THINK I 3 YOU) throws away pieces one, two and four, drops piece three into the middle, and prints "What makes you think I hate you". Two of the four words in your sentence that the program did recognise are flipped on the way out, you becoming I and me becoming you. Of the word hate, the program knows only that it was in position three.

Everything else in the design is bookkeeping around those three lists: several reassembly frames per pattern so the same input does not give the same reply twice running, word tags so that a rule mentioning (/FAMILY) matches mother or father without naming either, and a memory stack so a remark can be dredged up later when nothing else matches.

One correction is worth making here, because most retellings get it wrong. The 1966 paper does not use the name DOCTOR. What it says is that the only serious scripts in existence were ones that made ELIZA answer roughly as a Rogerian psychotherapist would. ELIZA is the interpreter; the psychotherapist behaviour is a script that was loaded into it, and the two are not the same thing. Weizenbaum picked the psychotherapist pose for a coldly practical reason: it is one of the few forms of conversation in which one party can credibly know almost nothing about the real world. Tell a psychiatrist "I went for a long boat ride" and hear "Tell me about boats", and you assume they know about boats and have a purpose. The assumption is entirely yours.

4. The plan behind it was to write everything down

ELIZA was a demonstration rather than a serious attempt at language, but the method behind it was the serious plan for machine language for about thirty years. You write the rules down. A person who knows the language and knows the task sits and enumerates what the program should do, case by case, and the program is exactly as good as that list.

The same plan was pursued in three places at once: hand-built grammars, in which a linguist wrote out a language as a formal system for a parser to apply; expert systems, in which a specialist's judgement was turned into a few thousand if-then rules; and machine translation, in which a large bilingual dictionary was paired with hand-written transformation rules, each source word carrying a small program describing how to translate it.

Translation is where the plan was tried at industrial scale, and where it failed in public. Warren Weaver had proposed in 1949 that translation be attacked statistically, using the information theory that was then brand new. The field went symbolic instead. In 1954 a demonstration run by Georgetown University with IBM translated a set of Russian sentences into English and produced enormous press excitement. The decade that followed produced most of the architectural ideas the field still uses, and very little working translation.

5. Why the list never finishes

Hand-written rules do not fail because the people writing them are careless. They fail for a structural reason, and it is the reason this whole course exists.

A rule covers the sentences its author thought of. Real text keeps producing sentences nobody thought of, so each one needs another rule. Meanwhile the rules interact, so the thousandth rule is far more expensive to add than the tenth: before you can add it you have to know which of the existing nine hundred and ninety-nine it will fire ahead of, which it will steal input from, and which it will silently shadow.

ELIZA shows the interaction problem in miniature, in the paper itself. The rule (0 YOU 0 ME) handles "It seems that you hate me" perfectly. Hand it "It seems that you hate" and it fails outright, because there is no me to match, so a second rule has to be tried, and then a third, until something matches or the program gives up. Weizenbaum also notes that a tighter rule, (0 YOU 1 ME), would have worked equally well on the original sentence, since the 1 means "exactly one word". It would then have failed on "It seems you hate and love me", where the looser rule succeeds. Two rules, one sentence, and already the author has to reason about which is more general and in what order they should be attempted.

Language has a very long tail, and a list of rules does not. There is no point at which the list is finished, and no point at which the next rule is cheaper than the last.

6. The first winter was an argument that won, not an idea that failed

In 1966, the same year as the ELIZA paper, a committee of the United States National Academy of Sciences delivered the report that everyone now calls ALPAC, after the Automatic Language Processing Advisory Committee that wrote it. Its conclusion was that machine translation was not delivering: it would not produce an early cost reduction, it was not improving in performance, and it was not meeting an operational need. Funding in the United States was cut sharply. Machine translation lost academic respectability so thoroughly that the professional body of the field, then called the Association for Machine Translation and Computational Linguistics, dropped the words "Machine Translation" from its name.

The committee was chaired by John R. Pierce, a communications engineer at Bell Labs and a colleague of Claude Shannon. Three years later Pierce did the same thing to speech recognition, in a letter to the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America titled "Whither Speech Recognition?", in which he wrote that most recognisers behaved not like scientists but like mad inventors or untrustworthy engineers, and that performance would stay very limited unless the device understood what was being said with something of the facility of a native speaker. We know the wording exactly because Fred Jelinek, who spent the following decades proving Pierce wrong, quoted it back at an audience in 2004.

It is worth being careful about what this episode was. The first AI winter was not a discovery that the idea was impossible. It was an argument, made by serious people with real evidence, that the money was not buying results. They were right about the results. What they were wrong about was the reason, because the reason was not that the problem was too hard for machines. It was that the method was one that could only ever be as large as the patience of the people writing it down.

7. What Weizenbaum actually concluded

The historically interesting fact about ELIZA is not what the program did. It is what the people in front of it did.

Weizenbaum's observation, in the discussion section of the 1966 paper, is that his users completed the illusion themselves. A speaker sustains a conversation, he writes, by attributing to their conversational partner all sorts of background knowledge, insight and reasoning ability. When the partner is a program those attributions are still made, and they are still entirely the speaker's own contribution, showing up not in what the program says but in the interpretation placed on it.

He goes further, and the further step is the one worth carrying. A person will not defend the illusion against all odds, but they will work hard for it. As long as the replies can be read consistently with the assumptions being made, the image of the partner survives, and a difficult reply may even improve it, because the person invents a further rationalisation that makes it make sense. Only when those rationalisations become too massive and too self-contradictory does the whole image collapse and get replaced with a smaller one. Weizenbaum notes drily that some subjects were very hard to convince that ELIZA was not human, and calls that a striking form of Turing's test.

His closing worry is not about chat at all. It is that important decisions were increasingly being made in response to computer output, and that the person interpreting what the machine says is in the same position as the person typing at ELIZA, constantly having to judge how much credence the output deserves. ELIZA shows, he writes, how easy it is to create and maintain the illusion of understanding.

Two things follow from that for this course. The first is that a machine can produce convincing language with no model of meaning in it at all, so fluency is not evidence of understanding and never was. The second is that the demonstration is only half the story, because a listener will supply the understanding if you give them somewhere to put it. Both facts are older than every technique in the rest of this course.

8. What it owes the next step

The rule-writing programme leaves one debt behind it, and it is a precise one. If you cannot enumerate what language does, you need a method that measures how language behaves instead, and that method has to get better when you feed it something you can go out and collect. The statistical turn: guessing the next word is that method: stop describing language, start counting it, and let a pile of text do the work that a list of rules could not finish.

Retrieval practice

What did ELIZA hold in memory that let it answer you?
A keyword scan chose which patterns to try, a decomposition rule cut your sentence into numbered pieces, and a reassembly frame reprinted one of those pieces inside fixed words.
Why does the thousandth hand-written rule cost more than the tenth?
Adding coverage is editing a system whose parts already shadow and steal from one another, and that argument grows with every part.
What did Weizenbaum conclude from how people treated ELIZA?
He observed that a speaker attributes background knowledge, insight and reasoning to their partner, and that with ELIZA all of it was the speaker's own contribution.
What did the 1966 ALPAC report conclude about machine translation?
The committee judged the results rather than the idea, and the funding cut that followed is why the field spent the next decade elsewhere.
