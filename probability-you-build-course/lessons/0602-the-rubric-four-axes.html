Four axes, twelve points, one honest conversation with yourself

10 min the rubric Correctness, communication, honesty about uncertainty, craft - each scored 0 to 3, weighted equally. Self-assessment first, peer second. The scorer below applies the pass bar while you move the sliders.

The one-minute version

  • Four axes, each 0–3, equally weighted: correctness, communication, honesty about uncertainty, craft.
  • Pass bar for portfolio use: at least 8 of 12 total and no axis at zero. A brilliant analysis with a dead honesty axis still fails.
  • Score yourself first, honestly; a peer scores second. The gap between the two scores is itself information about your communication.
  • Level 3 on every axis means "survives an adversarial reader", not "went further". Depth is level 2's job; robustness is level 3's.

Why these four and not "impact"

Portfolio rubrics usually smuggle in an impact axis, and it quietly rewards whatever was already famous. This rubric refuses the category. The four axes measure only what you controlled: does the maths do what the prose claims, can a non-specialist follow it, is uncertainty treated as a subject rather than a footnote, and does the artifact behave like something maintained by a professional. A modest project can score 12; a grand one cannot buy its way past a zero.

The week-6 audit card doubles as the checklist for the correctness and honesty axes: if your project shipped with a completed audit card, those two axes are mostly bookkeeping. If it did not, that absence is the score.

Correctness: does the maths do what the prose claims?

ScoreWhat the project looks like
0A metric contradicts the narrative or the code somewhere - the text says calibrated, the reliability diagram says otherwise.
1Metrics right, but thresholds, splits, or denominators go undeclared somewhere. The reader meets a number and cannot reconstruct where it came from.
2Everything declared and recomputable from the repository: another person runs your code and lands on your numbers.
3Also correct under adversarial reading - edge cases handled and stated: empty calibration bins, the rare class with three positives, the threshold where denominators vanish.

Notice the ladder. Level 1 is where most coursework stops; level 2 is reproducibility, which is ordinary professional hygiene; level 3 is the habit week 4's adversarial suite trained - assuming a hostile reader and being right anyway. Nothing above 3 exists because nothing beyond adversarial survival is needed for trust.

Communication: can a non-statistician follow it?

ScoreWhat the project looks like
0A wall of numbers. Charts without captions, tables without units, a README that assumes the reader wrote the code too.
1Numbers explained but jargon undefined - "ECE of 0.14" arrives with no translation of what 0.14 buys or costs.
2A plain-language walkthrough with figures that carry the story. A manager reads top to bottom and can retell it.
3A stranger could repeat the analysis from the page alone, and every chart is labelled so it survives being screenshotted away from the site - axes named, thresholds stated, sample sizes printed on the plot.

The screenshot test is the sharpest instrument here. Your figures will travel without you - into slide decks, chats, other people's write-ups. A chart that needs its surrounding page to be understood has already failed wherever it landed.

Honesty about uncertainty

ScoreWhat the project looks like
0Point estimates only, or intervals quoted with no method attached - decoration, not statistics.
1Intervals exist; method unclear. The reader believes the width but cannot defend it.
2Bootstrap method stated, reproducible seed committed, limitations paragraph present and specific.
3Also separates aleatoric from epistemic residual error, and states in advance what evidence would change the conclusion. The falsifier is written down before the results arrive.

This is the axis where the course stakes its identity. Every week drilled some piece of it: the planner priced uncertainty explicitly, the garden made distributions drawable, the chamber put intervals on a point estimate, the Audit Bench turned critique into a document. A capstone that quotes single numbers as if they were facts has spent six weeks learning nothing it did not already believe.

Craft

ScoreWhat the project looks like
0Broken links, dead demo, an unreadable notebook dump exported as PDF.
1Works locally, messy repository - the author's machine is the only environment where truth lives.
2Clean repository shape, working browser demo, consistent style throughout.
3Review-grade: a reproducibility note, attribution and licences correct, and the artifact is pleasant - someone enjoyed using it.

"Pleasant" is doing real work at level 3. Reviewers - hiring managers, open-source maintainers, future collaborators - calibrate their trust of your statistics partly through their experience of your interface. A demo that stutters, mislabels, or dies offline teaches them to distrust the numbers too.

How scoring happens

  1. Self-assessment first, against the tables above, written down before anyone else sees the project. Score the work you shipped, not the work you intend to finish tonight.
  2. Peer assessment second: one fellow learner scores the same axes cold, from the public repository only. They may ask questions after scoring; not before.
  3. The gap is the lesson. If your peer scores communication two levels below yours, your README failed for exactly the reader it was written for. Fix that before polishing anything else.

Where does the line between 2 and 3 really sit? On every axis it sits at the same place: would this survive a hostile expert? The hostile expert recomputes your numbers (correctness), reads only your README aloud to their team (communication), hunts for the claim your evaluation cannot touch (honesty), and tries to break your demo on a train with no wifi (craft). Prepare for that reader and all four threes take care of themselves.

Check yourself

Project A scores correctness 3, communication 2, honesty 1, craft 2. Project B scores correctness 3, communication 3, honesty 0, craft 2. Which passes the portfolio bar?
A: 3+2+1+2 = 8, no zeros, passes. B: 3+3+0+2 = 8, but the zero-axis rule fires - an unaudited claim cannot be rescued by brilliance elsewhere. Set B's honesty to 1 in the scorer above and watch it flip to passing: the entire difference between fail and pass is one honest interval with a stated method.
Your bootstrap interval is quoted as [0.31, 0.52] with no statement of how many resamples were used or what seed. Which axis takes the damage, and at what level does the fix land?
The honesty table is explicit: intervals with no method score 1, because the reader cannot defend a width whose origin they cannot see. The climb to 2 costs one sentence (resample count, statistic, seed) and one committed constant. Quoting intervals is not the achievement; quotable-on-demand provenance is.
Your teacher. Run the self-scoring now, on whatever you currently imagine building - a half-formed idea scores fine. Write the four numbers down and keep them; you will rescore at ship time, and the distance travelled is the truest progress report the course can give you.

Where this came from

The audit-card framing that doubles as the correctness and honesty checklist follows the evaluation sources week 6 trusts: Guo et al. (2017) for calibration measurement, ProPublica's Machine Bias for the public-audit standard a capstone imitates, and fairmlbook.org for what a stated fairness position must contain. The equal weighting and the zero-axis rule are this course's own design choices, argued in part 1.
