The Distribution Garden: artwork where every layer is a random variable

9 min map One canvas, five toggleable layers, six milestones - and by the end of the week you can prove to a stranger that your picture was sampled, not drawn.

The one-minute version

  • This week ships a generative artwork: starfield, nebula glow, splat clouds, lightning walks, orbital rings - every layer driven by a named distribution with visible parameters.
  • The design rule every layer had to pass: the picture comes out wrong if you misunderstand the distribution. Nothing here decorates.
  • You will make uniform dots, rejection-sampled brightness, inverse-transform rings, Box-Muller splats, and walk histograms that go bell-shaped in front of you.
  • A live caption names each layer's distribution and parameters, so the piece doubles as its own specification - and Regenerate proves it is a sample.
  • Six milestones, each runnable on its own page: stop mid-week and you still have something working.

1. What you are building

The finished artifact is below. Five layers compose onto one canvas. Every layer is a random variable in the strict sense you will formalise on the next page: a fixed function applied to a seed. Toggle layers off and on, change the seed, press Regenerate, and watch which parts of the picture move together. The caption under the canvas is generated live from the actual parameters - it is not decoration either.

2. The five layers, and what each one teaches

Every visual choice in the garden earned its place by surviving one question: does the picture break if the distribution is misunderstood? A uniformly-filled ring looks wrong the moment you expect centre-dense rings; a splat cloud at the wrong sigma contradicts its own histogram overlay. That is what makes the garden an instrument rather than a screensaver.

3. The milestones

Each milestone lives on its own page and runs the moment the page loads. Nothing is scaffolding for later: if you stop at any point, everything so far still works.

  1. M1 - Function, not picture. Starfield plus seed control; regenerate changes everything, same seed reproduces exactly. Lesson 0101.
  2. M2 - Make it glow. Rejection sampling under f(r); acceptance readout as Bernoulli and binomial counters. Lessons 0102 and 0103.
  3. M3 - Variance you can see. Box-Muller splats with histogram overlay and live mean/variance against theory. Lesson 0105, with marginals at 0106.
  4. M4 - The u-machine. Exponential rings via inverse CDF; the u-slider inspector. Lesson 0104.
  5. M5 - Walks and the bell. Endpoint histograms going bell-shaped as N grows; the sqrt(N) cone; momentum breaking independence. Lesson 0107.
  6. M6 - Gallery. Compose, caption, export PNG, permalink. Lesson 0108, then problems at 0109.

4. Where this week comes from

The week models the second week of Stanford's Probability for AI (PAI1), whose public table pairs the topic "random variables" with the build "Distribution Garden - probabilistic artwork". The lineage goes back further: CS109, PAI1's ancestor course, has run a "make your own art!" problem set for years, and Chris Piech's open textbook works the same idea as a worked algorithmic art example that generates circle art from Pareto radii in JavaScript - the direct ancestor of the ring layer you will build on page 0104.

Provenance of the data: there is none. Everything on these pages is generated in your browser from a seeded generator, so nothing can go stale, nothing needs a network, and any two readers who type the same seed see byte-identical pictures. That is deliberate, and page 0101 makes it the first lesson.

Reading alongsideWhere a derivation deserves more care than a build page can spare, the week links to Statistical Foundations of Machine Learning, which owns the formal treatment of this material - expectation as a weighted average, the CLT stated precisely, confidence machinery. This course writes code first and links out for rigour; that course derives first. Use them together.

5. How to work through it

Three habits, used on every page. First, predict before you move a slider: say aloud what the picture should do, then check. Second, watch for the derive-code-simulate pattern: an analytic result is worked on the page, computed by the build, and shown beside its Monte Carlo twin within a stated tolerance - the agreement is the proof you watch happen. Third, when a quiz appears, answer from memory before revealing; the point is retrieval, not recognition.

When you finish the milestones, the consolidated problem set at 0109 carries ten problems from estimating a cell probability to writing a generic inverse-transform sampler, all with hidden worked solutions.
