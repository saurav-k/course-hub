Scores you cannot game

12 min Brier, Murphy decomposition, log loss Some scores pay only for honesty: your best strategy is to state your true beliefs. Others can be gamed by bravado. Last lesson you fitted a temperature with one of them - here is why that worked.

The one-minute version

  • A proper scoring rule is one where stating your true probabilities maximises your expected score. Bravery is not rewarded; information is.
  • Brier score = mean squared error of probabilities. On this page's population: 0.1232.
  • Murphy's decomposition: Brier = uncertainty 0.2368 - resolution 0.1219 + reliability 0.0088. The floor is set by the world; skill is resolution earned on top of honesty.
  • Accuracy at t = 0.5 is exactly constant in temperature - scan T from 0.05 to 6 and its minimum equals its maximum, 0.8255. There is nothing to optimise. Log loss has a unique minimum, at T = 0.69 on this population's full rows.

What "proper" buys you

Last lesson you minimised log loss to find a temperature and moved on without asking why that target was legitimate. The answer is a property called properness. A scoring rule assigns each prediction p a penalty when outcome y arrives - log loss charges -log(p) when y = 1 and -log(1-p) when y = 0; the Brier score charges (p - y)². The rule is proper when your expected penalty is minimised precisely by predicting the true probability of the outcome. Under a proper rule, the optimal strategy is honesty: hedge exactly as much as your uncertainty, no more and no less. Gneiting and Raftery (2007) built the general theory; for this week you need only the consequence - a proper rule cannot be improved by bluffing, so optimising it cannot push a model toward false confidence.

Contrast accuracy. It looks at the thresholded decision and ignores how close p-hat was to certainty. A model that says 0.999 before a wrong answer loses nothing extra compared with one that said 0.51. Accuracy cannot see confidence at all - which, next section, makes it flat under rescaling and useless as a fitting target.

Murphy's three-part story

The Brier score of a forecaster decomposes into three interpretable pieces (Murphy 1973). Uncertainty is pi(1-pi) from the base rate pi - the Brier any constant climatology pays, the floor nobody beats. Resolution is what you claw back by making predictions that separate outcomes - it enters NEGATIVE, rewarding information. Reliability is the calibration penalty - how far your promised frequencies sit from delivered ones. On the default population:

Brier 0.1232 ~= uncertainty 0.2368 - resolution 0.1219 + reliability 0.0088

Add the right-hand side and you get 0.1236, against a measured Brier of 0.1232: the identity closes to 0.0005, five parts in ten thousand. It is approximate only because the terms are computed over ten bins rather than in full generality - within a bin the decomposition treats every row as though it carried the bin's mean forecast, and the discarded within-bin spread is exactly that residual. Narrow the bins and the residual shrinks; the figure prints it beside the waterfall so the approximation is never invisible. The decomposition explains last lesson's result mechanically: scaling attacked the reliability term (0.0088 → smaller) and physically could not touch resolution or uncertainty, because those live in the ranking and the world respectively.

The constant forecaster is the cleanest demonstration of why ECE alone never crowned anyone. Outputting this population's base rate 0.385 for every row is exactly calibrated - the single occupied bin promises 0.385 and observes 0.385, so ECE prints 0.0000 and reliability prints 0.000000 - yet its resolution is 0.000000, its Brier sits at the full uncertainty floor 0.2368, and its log loss is 0.6665, which is the binary entropy of 0.385 in nats to four decimals. That equality is not a coincidence: scoring a constant forecast p against outcomes drawn at rate p costs -[p ln p + (1-p) ln(1-p)] per row by definition. Calibrated AND useless, with a perfect ECE to prove that ECE alone proves nothing. The weather app that always says "30% rain because it rains 30% of days here" passes a calibration audit forever and helps no one packing an umbrella today.

Why nobody fits temperature by accuracy

Now the punchline you have been owed since lesson 5. Scan every temperature from 0.05 to 6 on this population and measure accuracy at t = 0.5 after each rescale: the minimum and the maximum are the same number, 0.8255, with no spread at all. Every value of T is "optimal". This is not luck, and it is not an approximation - it is lesson 5's midpoint argument read as a function of T. The decision at t = 0.5 is z ≥ 0 whatever T is, so accuracy is a mathematically constant function of T, and an optimiser pointed at it has zero gradient everywhere, no preference between 0.05 and 6, and no reason to land anywhere in particular.

Notice the shape of the failure, because it generalises past this one fitting problem. Accuracy is not a noisy or weak target here; it is blind. It reads only which side of the line each row fell on, and T cannot move any row across that line, so accuracy has nothing to report. A proper rule reads the value, not just the side, which is why it can tell 0.51 from 0.999 and therefore has something to say about T. Point the same grid at log loss and there is exactly one minimum, at T = 0.69 on these rows, taking Brier from 0.1232 to 0.1198 and reliability from 0.0088 to 0.0056 while resolution holds at 0.1220 - the fit bought honesty and could not have bought skill. Properness is not a nicety; it is the difference between a target you can fit and a target that cannot see the question.

Why fit T by minimising log loss rather than maximising accuracy?
Properness is the licence: minimising a proper rule pulls toward truth instead of toward bravado. And accuracy is worse than a noisy target here - at t = 0.5 it is exactly constant in T, so the objective is flat and every value of T ties.
A weather app always outputs exactly the city's historical rain frequency (30%) regardless of today's clouds. It is:
Roughly 30% of its rainy-day-30% calls see rain, so calibration is nearly perfect; but it separates no day from any other, so resolution is zero and its Brier sits on the uncertainty floor. Reliability and resolution are different virtues - that is why Murphy's decomposition exists.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice P5working

Prevalence pi = 0.3. Forecaster A outputs 0.3 for everyone. Forecaster B splits the population in half: it outputs 0.1 to a subpopulation whose true frequency really is 0.1, and 0.5 to the rest, whose frequency really is 0.5. Compute both Brier scores, identify uncertainty, reliability and resolution for each, and check the identity Brier = uncertainty - resolution for B.

Solution

Forecaster A: E[(0.3 - y)²] = 0.3 x 0.49 + 0.7 x 0.09 = 0.147 + 0.063 = 0.210 - exactly the uncertainty term pi(1-pi); reliability gap zero, resolution zero.

Forecaster B: within the low half, contribution = (0.1-0)² x 0.9 + (0.1-1)² x 0.1 = 0.009 + 0.081 = 0.090. Within the high half, (0.5-0)² x 0.5 + (0.5-1)² x 0.5 = 0.125 + 0.125 = 0.250. Mean = 0.5 x 0.090 + 0.5 x 0.250 = 0.170.

Both halves are internally calibrated, so reliability stays zero; the resolution gained is 0.210 - 0.170 = 0.040, and indeed 0.210 - 0.040 = 0.170. The moral in one line: the score paid B precisely for information that changes decisions - which is what "proper" means when it grows up.
