Add logs: the peak stays put, and your floats stay alive

12 min build inside Independent evidence multiplies, multiplied probabilities vanish off the number line, and the logarithm fixes both at once.

The one-minute version

  • Independent bins combine by multiplication: the likelihood of all 24 counts is the product of the 24 per-bin probabilities.
  • This dataset's full likelihood is about 10-88 - and a year of such exposures drives it past what a 64-bit float can represent at all. Logs turn the product into a sum that always computes.
  • The log is monotone, so it rearranges values without reordering them: the logged surface peaks at exactly the same hypothesis as the raw one.
  • Milestone 4 lands here: the score over every (x, w) drawn as a heatmap, and your own twelve-line grid search marking its argmax.

1. Multiplication is the honest combination

If bins were somehow correlated you would need to model how, but this instrument's bins are independent: whether one muon survives has no bearing on its neighbour's fate. Independence has a precise combining rule from Week 1 - multiply. So under any hypothesis, the probability of observing all 24 counts is the product over bins of each count's Poisson probability:

L(hypothesis) = P(k1) × P(k2) × ... × P(k24) Each factor P(ki) is the Poisson probability of observing count ki when the bin's expected count is mi: mke-m/k!. The hypothesis enters only through the 24 expected counts it predicts.

2. The per-bin term, derived once

Substituting the Poisson formula into the product and logging gives, per bin, three terms: k·ln(m), then -m, then -ln(k!). The last depends only on what was actually observed - the same for every hypothesis in the comparison - so scores can drop it without changing who wins:

score(bin) = k · ln(m) - m Read as: reward for predicting what arrived (k grows, so does the pull toward larger m), minus one unit of penalty per expected muon (predicting more than arrived costs). The dropped constant is ln(k!), the observation's own complexity.

3. Why raw products genuinely die

It is fair to ask whether the underflow story is real or folklore, so here is this week's own dataset, measured from the build's code. The full likelihood of the 24 observations at the best-fit hypothesis - constants included this time - works out to about 10-88. Your browser's 64-bit float still represents that (its floor is near 10-308). But likelihoods compound across data: a full year of exposures multiplies roughly 365 more copies of that product into one number near 10-32,000, which no float holds - the computation silently returns zero, and zero looks identical for every hypothesis. The log of the same quantity is about -203 today and -74,000 next year: always finite, always comparable, always pointing at the same winner.

4. Run milestone 4: the surface, seen all at once

Your slider trips through lesson 0202 sampled this surface one line at a time. The upgraded build evaluates all of it - every x from -120 to 120, every w from 0 to 80 - and draws the whole landscape. Then it hands you the optimiser. This is the actual loop, and it is yours to read line by line:

let best = { x: null, w: null, ll: -Infinity };
for (let w = 0; w <= 80; w += 5) {
  for (let x = -120; x <= 120; x += 5) {
    const ll = PYBMuon.poissonLogLik(obs, x, w);
    if (ll > best.ll) best = { x, w, ll };
  }
}

On the heatmap, note where the brightness varies slowly: along the width direction the ridge is soft - several widths score within a couple of units of the best - while misplacing x by 20 pixels falls off the bright region quickly. Lesson 0202 promised this asymmetry a picture; there it is. And note what the heatmap is mathematically: 833 sums of 24 log terms. No magic, just the arithmetic from section 2, everywhere.

Check yourself

Why compare LL = ln L rather than L itself across 24 angular bins?
Multiplication becomes addition under the log, and the log's strict order preservation means the argmax cannot move. Nothing is smoothed - the noise is exactly where it was - and nothing about the peak changes except the ruler measuring it.
Doubling the exposure time doubles every bin's expected count m. What happens to the argmax of the Poisson log-likelihood?
More exposure adds information, not position: the same placement still explains the data best, but each bin carries twice the evidence, so wrong placements lose far more log units and the peak narrows. Precision improves; the estimate itself stays put.
Your teacher. In the heatmap, find the brightest cell with w = 15 and compare its shade to the overall best. That gap - small - is why lesson 0206 will refuse to report width to three significant figures even though the fit prints them.

Where this came from

The IID product form, the log transformation, and Bernoulli worked examples follow Piech, Maximum Likelihood Estimation. Float limits quoted above are IEEE-754 double precision facts, demonstrated arithmetically on this page's own dataset rather than cited from a benchmark.
