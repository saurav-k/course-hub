Normal splats: variance made visible, Box-Muller beside its histogram

12 min working One slider moves a cloud, its histogram, its theory curve and two readouts together - because they were never four separate things.

The one-minute version

  • The Normal distribution N(μ, σ²) is defined by two numbers: centre μ and standard deviation σ, whose square is the variance.
  • σ is spread you can see. Doubling σ doubles the visible width and multiplies the variance by four - the square is the whole subtlety.
  • Box-Muller turns uniforms into normals with sines, cosines and a logarithm - the famous pre-computed transform your u-machine from page 0104 stands beside.
  • About 68% of a normal population sits within one σ of the mean - a claim this build lets you weigh by eye and by readout.

1. Two numbers own the shape

The glow needed a whole function, exp(-k r), to describe its fall from brightness. The normal needs only two constants. Its density is fixed in shape; the mean μ slides it along the axis and the standard deviation σ stretches or squeezes it:

X ~ N(μ, σ²),   f(x) proportional to exp( -(x-μ)² / (2σ²) ) Read as: X is normally distributed with centre mu and standard deviation sigma; sigma squared is the variance. The constant of proportionality exists to make the total area exactly 1, and nothing on this page will make you compute it by hand.

Variance deserves its definition stated once, in words: it is the average of the squared distance from the mean,

Var(X) = E[ (X - μ)² ],   σ = sqrt( Var(X) ) Read as: take every outcome's distance from centre, square it so that left and right both count positively, average those squares - that is the variance. Its square root returns to ordinary units and is called the standard deviation.

Squaring is why doubling σ multiplies variance by four, and halving σ takes variance to a quarter. The build's readout shows both numbers side by side precisely because mixing them up is the classic slip - watch for it in your own reading of dashboards: "variance" figures that look like reasonable spreads are usually standard deviations wearing the wrong label.

3. Run the sigma slider through its paces

Predict before dragging: set σ = 40, note the variance readout, then move to σ = 80. If you predicted the cloud roughly doubles in width while the variance lands near 6,400, you have the square relationship working for you. The curve meanwhile halves in peak height - it must, since its area stays pinned at 1 and its width doubled.

Check yourself

Doubling sigma in the splat layer does what to the distribution?
Variance is sigma squared, so doubling sigma multiplies variance by four - the same square that turned a distance into an area. Doubling alongside confuses the deviation with its square; a fixed variance contradicts the widened picture; and narrowing contradicts the premise. Watch the build's readout perform all three wrong answers' arithmetic for you if you drag halfway instead of fully.
With sigma = 40 the variance readout shows 1,637 against a theoretical 1,600. You should conclude:
A finite sample's variance estimate wobbles around the true sigma squared, and 2.3 percent off at these sample sizes is ordinary breathing, inside the stated tolerance. Variance equalling sigma confuses the two labelled columns the readout deliberately separates; histograms may legally overshoot a curve anywhere; and reseeding changes nothing about estimation error being expected.
Your teacher. Set n to its minimum, sigma to 40, and regenerate a few seeds. Some seeds will show gaps well above five percent - that is small-sample noise doing something honest. Now raise n slowly and watch the gap shrink like a taut argument: the law of large numbers is not a slogan here, it is a readout falling.
