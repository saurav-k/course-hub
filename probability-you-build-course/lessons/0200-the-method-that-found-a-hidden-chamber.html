Three detectors, counted muons, and the argument that found a hidden chamber

12 min week map In 2017 physicists found a large void inside the Great Pyramid without drilling a single hole. This week you rebuild their method three times, in code you write yourself.

The one-minute version

  • Muons rain through the pyramid. Rock eats some; air eats almost none. Count muons coming through each angle and the angles that crossed a void arrive in surplus.
  • The analysis compares what was counted against simulations with and without a hypothesised structure, and picks the hypothesis that makes the observed counts most probable.
  • That pick is called the maximum likelihood estimate, and it is this week's entire subject: write down P(data given hypothesis) for every hypothesis, then let the data choose.
  • A likelihood is not a probability that the hypothesis is true. Confusing the two has sent innocent people to prison.

1. The discovery, briefly and accurately

In November 2017 the ScanPyramids collaboration announced, in Morishima et al., Nature 552, 386-390 (2017), the existence of a large void above the Grand Gallery in Khufu's Pyramid. The paper's method ran like this. Cosmic-ray muons arriving from the sky were counted by detectors placed in the Grand Gallery and outside the pyramid. Because rock absorbs muons while air barely does, the count arriving along any direction tells you how much stone stands in the way of that direction. The team compared the observed counts per direction against simulations of what the counts would be for a pyramid with no hidden structure, and found an excess - directions that saw less stone than the no-void model predicted. Fitting those excess slices from two viewing positions triangulated the void, which the paper reports as having a minimum length of 30 m, the same cross-section as the Grand Gallery, and lying directly above it. Three independent detector technologies - nuclear emulsion films, scintillator hodoscopes, and gas detectors - agreed.

The free arXiv version of the same paper is worth skimming for its Extended Data figures, which show exactly the shape of argument this course will make runnable: observed data plotted against simulations with and without structures, an excess located by fitting slices, and the fit turning counts into a position. A later paper on the North Face Corridor, Sercombe et al., Nature Communications 14 (2023), states the underlying idea in one sentence: measuring the muon flux in a given direction provides a direct estimate of the mean density along that path.

Honesty labelThis course's pyramid is a simulation built from teaching constants. Its geometry follows the published description, and its physics uses the standard qualitative facts (near-vertical arrival, absorption through stone); every flux number it produces was generated by this course's own code, not measured by ScanPyramids. The lessons cite the Nature paper for the method and never quote simulated counts as if they were the collaboration's data.

2. The move that finds things: run probability forwards, compare hypotheses

Nothing in the analysis computes "the probability that the void exists". Instead, over and over, it computes something much easier: if such-and-such structure were in the pyramid, what counts would the detectors probably see? That is a forwards calculation, world to observation, and it is exactly the kind of thing this course's first two weeks taught you to build. The analysis runs it once per candidate world - no void, void here, void there - and then asks which candidate makes the counts that actually arrived least surprising.

3. Likelihood is not probability - and the difference imprisons people

Write L(hypothesis given data) for the number the scoring step produces: the probability of the observed data assuming that hypothesis. It is called the likelihood of the hypothesis. Two hypotheses' likelihoods may be compared directly - if the data are twice as probable under A as under B, then A explains the data twice as well. But the likelihood of A is not the probability that A is true. That statement also depends on how plausible A was before the data arrived, and Bayes rule is what combines the two - treated properly in Statistical Foundations' lesson on Bayes rule.

The failure mode has a name: the prosecutor's fallacy. A defendant matches a DNA profile carried by one person in three million. The prosecutor says "the probability of this evidence if he is innocent is one in three million, so the probability he is innocent is one in three million." The first clause is a likelihood; the second claims a posterior. They differ enormously when the alternative hypothesis - somebody else did it - covers millions of people: in a city of four million, expect more than one matching innocent, and the match evidence is far weaker than "one in three million" suggests. The Statistical Foundations problem set works this exact arithmetic in its Bayes-rule problems. This week's builds score hypotheses constantly; every score they print is a likelihood, and every lesson will hold the line on what that means.

Check yourself

Two hypotheses score likelihood 0.002 under A and 0.006 under B. What have you learned?
Likelihoods compare explanations: B makes the observed data three times more probable than A does. Whether B is true additionally depends on prior plausibility (Bayes territory, deliberately out of scope this week). A is weakened, not ruled out. And 0.006 is the probability of the data assuming B - not a property of the data alone.
A news report says: "the accused matches a profile carried by one person in three million, so he is almost certainly guilty." What is wrong?
This is the prosecutor's fallacy. One-in-three-million is P(evidence given innocent); the claim needs P(innocent given evidence), which also depends on how many other people could have committed the crime. In a large population several matching innocents are expected, and the posterior looks nothing like the likelihood.
Your teacher. Before lesson 0201, say aloud what the detector measures in one sentence with no statistics in it. If you said "how much stone is in each direction", you have the instrument; the rest of the week is only about turning that sentence into a number and trusting the number correctly.
