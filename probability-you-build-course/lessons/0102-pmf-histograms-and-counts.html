Sticks for atoms: the PMF, and what a histogram is converging to

11 min foundation Count dots in grid cells, watch the count's histogram grow bars that lean on exact binomial sticks - the empirical learning its theory by repeated contact.

The one-minute version

  • A discrete random variable takes isolated values, and its PMF assigns each value its probability: p_X(x) = P(X = x).
  • "This dot landed in my cell" is a Bernoulli indicator; "how many of the n dots landed there" is a Binomial count.
  • An empirical histogram from finite data is noisy. Raise n and the bars settle onto the theoretical sticks - the picture converges, the distribution was already there.
  • The build reports total variation distance, one number for how far the bars still are from the sticks.

1. From placement to counting

The last page placed uniform dots and stopped. Now draw a grid over the plate and ask a sharper question about any one cell: how many dots does it hold? Each dot, independently, either lands in that cell or misses it. That single-dot yes/no quantity is the atom every count is built from:

P(B = 1) = p,   P(B = 0) = 1 - p Read as: B is a Bernoulli indicator - it equals 1 when the dot lands in the chosen cell and 0 when it does not. Here p is one cell's share of the plate: with m equal cells, p = 1/m.

Sum n such indicators - one per dot, all independent, all with the same p - and you have the cell count X. Its distribution has a name and a formula:

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 - p)n-k,   E[X] = n · p Read as: the probability the cell holds exactly k of the n dots is the number of ways to choose which k dots land there, times the probability those k arrive and the other n-k do not. The expected count is n dots times p, one cell's share.

This is the binomial distribution. It matters here because acceptance counters later this week - kept proposals out of attempts - are the same object wearing different clothes.

2. The PMF, and what the bars are approximating

For a discrete random variable, the probability mass function answers one question per value: how much probability sits exactly here? The histogram your build draws is an estimate of that function from finite data - with 64 cells you have 64 samples of the count, so the bars wobble. The sticks behind them are the truth the wobble is circling.

3. Run it until the bars find the sticks

Two failure modes live exactly here, so name them now. First, the histogram is not the distribution: at n = 100 the bars visibly miss the sticks, and nothing is wrong - the sample is just small. Second, the mean readout compares the sample average against n·p, not against whatever the current picture suggests; if those two numbers disagree wildly at large n, suspect the code, not the coin.

The total variation distance deserves thirty more seconds because you will meet it again twice: here as a convergence dial, and in Week 6 as part of judging whether two models' score distributions differ. For distributions P and Q it is half the sum of |P(x) - Q(x)| over all values x - the total amount of probability that would have to be moved to turn one into the other. Zero means identical; below roughly 0.05 at these settings means the bars have found the sticks for practical purposes.

Each dot contributes an independent Bernoulli(1/m) indicator, and a sum of n identical independent indicators is Binomial(n, p) - that is the definition. The observed counts vary cell to cell, but they are data, not the law. A normal shape emerges only as an approximation for large n, and the Poisson appears when n is huge and p tiny, as a limit rather than the exact answer.
