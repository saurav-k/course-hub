Week 4 · Logistic regression · Lesson 301

Logistic regression is maximum likelihood

working 10 min The MLE recipe instantiated · Math for ML, maximum likelihood · CS109 Lecture 24 (PDF)

The one-minute version

  • The model assumes each label is a Bernoulli coin whose heads probability is σ(θ·x).
  • Multiplying the per-point probabilities gives the likelihood of the labels; its log is the quantity training maximises.
  • This log-likelihood is exactly Week 3’s object: same recipe, different coin.
  • The loss everyone calls cross-entropy is minus that log-likelihood, averaged. Two names, one number.
  • On separated data the likelihood has no finite best value - the cliffhanger lesson 0304 measures.

1. The assumption: a coin per data point

Logistic regression is one modelling assumption plus Week 3. The assumption: for every point x, the label y is produced by a Bernoulli trial - a coin flip - whose probability of coming up 1 is σ(θ·x), with θ the same three numbers as last lesson (θ₀, θ₁, θ₂). Conditional probability supplies the reading “p(y = 1 given x)”; the formal derivation lives in Statistical Foundations 0020 and Bayes’ rule in 0023.

P(y given x) = σ(z)y (1 − σ(z))1−y,    z = θ·x Read as: if the label y is 1, keep the factor σ(z) (sigma of the score); if the label is 0, keep the factor 1 − σ(z). The exponents pick the right branch without an if-statement: anything to the power 0 is 1.

2. Multiply the coins, then take the log

A dataset is many independent flips. Independence means multiply, and multiplying many terms near zero is numerically miserable, so - exactly as in Week 3 - we take logs and add:

LL(θ) = Σi [ yi log σ(zi) + (1 − yi) log(1 − σ(zi)) ] Read as: the log-likelihood LL adds up, over all points i, the log-probability the model gave the label that actually occurred. Training logistic regression means finding the theta that makes LL as large as possible.

Work it on the smallest separated dataset there is: two points, ((−1,), 0) and ((1,), 1), with the bias fixed at 0 so only the weight w remains. The first point contributes log(1 − σ(−w)), which equals log σ(w) by symmetry, and the second contributes log σ(w). So LL(w) = 2 log σ(w):

  1. No weight at all. w = 0: both points sit at σ(0) = 0.5, LL = 2 log 0.5 = −1.386.
  2. Pull apart. w = 2: scores land at ±2, σ(2) = 0.881, LL = 2 log 0.881 = −0.254.
  3. Pull further. w = 5: σ(5) = 0.9933, LL = 2 log 0.9933 = −0.013.

Sanity check. Every term is a log of a probability, so LL is always negative - it climbs toward 0 but can never reach it. The three values rise monotonically, as they must when the weights separate the labels further.

On perfectly separable data the fit never converges, because there is nothing to converge to: the likelihood keeps climbing as the weights grow. Lesson 0304 measures what that does to confidence, and where the standard industrial fix - L2 regularisation - trades.

3. Cross-entropy is this exact number, negated and averaged

Training libraries minimise a loss, and the loss they call cross-entropy is

CE(θ) = −LL(θ) / n Read as: cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood divided by the number of points n. Minimising CE and maximising LL are the same act - the minus flips climbing to descending, and the division keeps the step size meaningful as datasets grow.

That is the entire relationship. When a framework reports “cross-entropy loss 0.31”, it is reporting the average per-point negative log-probability of the truth under your model - the Week 3 quantity with a sign flip. The codebook reading of cross-entropy as expected message length is derived in Math for ML 0181; this course states the identity and moves on. Likewise the general MLE recipe lives in Math for ML 0162.

The banked intuition Every instinct Week 3 built about likelihood transfers now: peaks worth climbing, curvature telling you how confident the peak is, unbounded objectives telling you the data cannot pin the answer. Nothing about logistic regression is a new optimisation problem - it is the old one wearing a sigmoid.

How does the cross-entropy loss used in training relate to Week 3’s log-likelihood?
CE = −LL/n exactly: same curves, flipped sign, rescaled axis. Squared error answers a different model altogether, the continuous/coin distinction runs the wrong way (Bernoulli is discrete), and a constant offset moves no optimum anywhere.
A colleague minimises plain accuracy instead of cross-entropy. What breaks?
Accuracy is a count of thresholded labels: moving a probability from 0.51 to 0.99 changes nothing it can see, so most directions give zero signal and the optimiser stalls. Unbounded growth is the separated-data pathology of lesson 0304, not an accuracy defect, and speed was never the issue.
