A hypothesis is a setting of two sliders, and the data grade every setting

12 min build inside The unknown becomes two dials, each dial gets a score, and "estimating" means finding the dials' best setting.

The one-minute version

  • A hypothesis about the hidden void is exactly two numbers: its centre offset x and its half-width w. This lesson's build wires both to sliders.
  • Each slider setting produces predicted counts per bin, and the Poisson log-likelihood scores how ordinary the observed counts look under that prediction.
  • The best setting is the argmax: the input where the score peaks. The score value itself is not physical - the placement is the answer.
  • Dragging reveals an asymmetry you will spend lesson 0206 explaining: the score punishes wrong positions hard and shrugs at wrong widths.

1. Parameters, met as controls

Lesson 0201's comparison used one fixed hypothesis: nature's own void. Now the hypothesised void becomes yours to move. Its two free quantities are called parameters from here on - the centre offset x, measured from the pyramid's axis, and the half-width w. Every pair (x, w) is one complete candidate world, and the machinery from last lesson runs unchanged for any pair: trace the rays through a pyramid containing that void, compute expected counts per bin, compare with what arrived.

2. Run milestone 3: feel the surface before seeing it

Three things are worth noticing while the sliders are still warm. First, the score is enormous and negative-ish even at good settings - it is a log of a probability of 24 specific observations, not a probability of anything. Second, most settings do not merely score badly, they score obviously badly: the indigo boxes visibly miss the grey bars. Third, some quite different-looking settings score almost identically - try x = 40 with w = 15 versus w = 35. Those are nearly tied, which should bother you slightly. It will get a name (weak identification) and a whole lesson (0206).

4. Naming the target: max versus argmax

The game the build is playing now has a precise vocabulary. The maximum of a function is its highest value; the argument of the maximum, shortened to argmax, is the input that achieves it. When the build prints a fit, the placement is the argmax and the score is the maximum - and confusing them is confusing the answer with the grade. The general vocabulary of argmax-as-training lives in Mathematics for ML lesson 0005; here it stays concrete.

From this lesson forward the week's real protagonist exists: a function over hypotheses, a pair of hands (yours, then code's) hunting its peak. What is still missing is why the score adds logarithms, which settings deserve your trust, and what to do when no formula for the peak exists. The next four lessons are those three questions in order.

Check yourself

The finished search prints "grid argmax: x = 40, w = 25, LL = 11,759.9". Which part is the estimate?
The argmax - the input that achieved the top score - is the estimate: a position and a width. The score is the quality of the explanation, useful for ranking hypotheses but not a physical quantity. Reading the big number as "how correct" is misconception one wearing a calculator.
Dragging x away from 40 drops the score steeply, but sweeping w between 15 and 35 barely moves it. What does the asymmetry tell you?
A parameter matters exactly to the degree that changing it changes the score. Rays crossing the void fix where the surplus begins and ends along their paths, so position is nailed - while several widths produce nearly the same depth of surplus, and tie. Lesson 0206 gives this asymmetry its proper name and consequences.
Your teacher. Find a second slider setting that scores within 1 unit of the best, and one that scores 10 units worse. State both as sentences about stone ("a slightly narrower void explains the counts almost as well"; "a void on the wrong side of the axis explains them far worse") - that translation from score-differences to physics is the skill.
