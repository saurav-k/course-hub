When the peak has a formula, algebra finds it in four lines

12 min build inside The grid search works everywhere but answers only where you thought to look. When the likelihood is simple, calculus hands over the exact answer - and its limits are worth learning too.

The one-minute version

  • Interior maxima of smooth functions have zero slope, so "set the derivative to zero and solve" recovers many estimates exactly, with no scanning at all.
  • Worked in full for failures counted over orders: the maximum-likelihood failure rate is always k/n - observed failures divided by attempts.
  • The same three-step recipe produces lambda-hat = sample mean for Poisson counts and x-bar for a Normal's centre. It is one tool, not three memorised results.
  • Then the recipe hits a wall it cannot handle: k = 0 puts the peak at the boundary of p's allowed range, where slopes never vanish. That wall is build 3's subject and lesson 0207's destination.

1. The recipe

Every analytic estimate this week follows the same three moves, so learn them once:

2. Worked in full: failures over payments

A payment pipeline processes n orders and fails on k of them. Model each order as Bernoulli: failed with probability p, independently. The probability of witnessing this exact record is proportional to pk(1-p)n-k, so the log-likelihood drops the constant combination term and keeps two lines:

  1. Write the score. With 3 failures in 12 orders as the running example: LL(p) = 3 ln(p) + 9 ln(1-p), dropping the constant ln C(12,3).
  2. Differentiate. dLL/dp = 3/p - 9/(1-p). The first term rewards raising p; the second punishes it as successes start failing.
  3. Set the slope to zero and solve. 3/p = 9/(1-p) gives 3(1-p) = 9p, then 3 = 12p, then p-hat = 3/12 = 0.25.
  4. Confirm it is a peak. The slope is positive just below 0.25 (try p = 0.2: 3/0.2 - 9/0.8 = 15 - 11.25 = +3.75) and negative just above (p = 0.3: 10 - 12.86 = -2.86), so the function climbs in and falls out. The endpoints score lower by inspection: L(0) = L(1) = 0 here.

3. The same three moves elsewhere

The recipe is not specific to coins and failures. Run it once more yourself in problem 4 for Poisson counts, where it delivers lambda-hat = the sample mean - and note what happens in build 1 when all bins share their expected count: the best hypothesis predicts the average of what arrived. For a Normal's centre mu the identical moves produce x-bar again. Three distributions, one habit: write the total log-probability of the data, differentiate, solve. The general treatment lives in Mathematics for ML lesson 0162; here the derivations stay two parameters wide and concrete.

4. Where the recipe hits a wall: run build 3

Step 3 assumed the peak sits strictly between 0 and 1, where slopes exist and can vanish. Push the data to the edge of what business reality supplies - a perfect run - and that assumption dies. The week's third instrument is deliberately tiny so nothing distracts from the crash:

Sit with the k = 0 case before moving on. The fit says p-hat = 0, and the next lesson will show why taking that literally - certifying a zero percent failure rate from forty clean payments - is the most expensive misreading of a likelihood in industry. The curve itself already whispers the truth: it is high across a whole neighbourhood of small p values, not just at zero.

Check yourself

Twelve trials, three successes. What is the maximum-likelihood estimate of the success probability?
The derived answer is exact: p-hat = k/n = 3/12 = 0.25. The shrunk variants are other estimators (add-one-style smoothing) with different goals, and 3/9 confuses the denominator with the count of non-successes. Under pure maximum likelihood there is nothing to argue about.
Why does setting dLL/dp = 0 locate the peak?
Rising on one side, falling on the other: a smooth interior peak must flatten exactly at its top, and solving for that flattening finds the candidate. Concavity is the consequence checked in step 4, not something the equation enforces - and boundaries, lesson 0207's topic, break the argument entirely.
Your teacher. Derive p-hat = k/n once from scratch without looking - the whole chain is four short lines. If week 4's logistic regression is the first time you re-derive it, the derivation was borrowed, not learned.
