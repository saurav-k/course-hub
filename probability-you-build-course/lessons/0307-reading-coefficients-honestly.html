Reading coefficients honestly

working 12 min Odds ratios and the full-suite report · Piech, ch. Logistic Regression

The one-minute version

  • A coefficient of 0.7 means: odds multiply by e0.7 ≈ 2 per unit of that feature, other features held fixed.
  • It does not mean the probability rises by 0.7 - probabilities compress as odds explode.
  • “Holding others fixed” is doing real work, and lesson 06 showed when it quietly fails.
  • The week’s artifact completes here: the nine-case report, yours included, fit to screenshot.
  • The design problem - the metric that lies - is assigned on the problems page.

1. What a coefficient actually says

Take the fitted weight w₁ = 0.7 on a feature called minutes exercised. The wrong reading - “each extra minute raises P(fit) by 0.7” - fails twice over: probabilities are bounded by 1, and the model never claimed linearity in probability anyway. The score z is what moves linearly, and z lives in log-odds:

log(odds) = θ₀ + θ₁x₁ + θ₂x₂,    odds = p / (1 − p) Read as: the model is linear in the log-odds - the natural log of odds, where odds are the probability of success divided by the probability of failure. One unit increase in feature x₁, holding all other features fixed, adds θ₁ to this log; equivalently it multiplies the odds by e raised to θ₁.

The same reading worked numerically, for a person whose current probability is 0.8:

  1. Odds today. p = 0.8 gives odds = 0.8/0.2 = 4.
  2. In log space. log(4) = 1.386.
  3. Add the coefficient. One more unit of the 0.7-feature: 1.386 + 0.7 = 2.086.
  4. Back to odds. e2.086 = 8.06 - the odds multiplied by e0.7 ≈ 2.01.
  5. Back to probability. p = 8.06/9.06 = 0.889.

Sanity check. The probability rose only 0.089 while the odds doubled - the compression the quiz warned about. Near certainty, even doubling the odds barely dents the probability; near zero, the same doubling barely lifts it off the floor. Effects live in odds space, displays live in probability space, and the honest sentence names both.

“Holding other features fixed” carries the rest of the weight. It is a genuine property of the model - the score adds contributions - but lesson 06 showed its limit: with correlated features, the credit for the shared signal is split arbitrarily across refits. So the protocol for reading any coefficient out loud:

The protocol Name the unit and say odds: “one more unit of x multiplies the odds of the positive label by ew, with every other feature held fixed.” Then check stability before believing the number itself: if the features correlate, quote the combination the data identifies rather than either twin alone. A coefficient without those two qualifiers is a rumour wearing a decimal point.

A colleague reads w₁ = 0.7 on “minutes exercised” as “each extra minute raises P(fitness) by 0.7”. The correction is...
The score is linear in log-odds, so the multiplicative statement is the faithful one - and the holding-others-fixed clause is part of the claim, not decoration. Adding 0.7 to raw odds is neither the model nor the arithmetic, and standardisation changes units, not interpretability itself.
A fitted weight comes out negative, w = −0.5, on hours of television. What does one more hour do?
Negative coefficients shrink odds by the same exponential rule positive ones grow them: each hour multiplies odds by about 0.61. Halving the probability confuses scales, and sign changes grammar, not meaning.
