Eight projects worth doing

12 min the menu Seven fully specified proposals graded by ambition, plus a four-question gate that qualifies any idea of your own. Each entry names its data, its probabilistic core, and the bar that makes it portfolio work rather than homework.

The one-minute version

  • Ambition here means judgement on display, not compute or novelty. The highest-signal project on the list re-analyses data thousands of people have already touched.
  • Proposals 1–4 are moderate: one probabilistic core, one fitted model, one audit, all achievable in two to three focused weeks alongside a job.
  • Proposals 5 and 6 are high: they join a public controversy or bind model to policy, which is where junior portfolios start to read like professional work.
  • Proposal 8 is yours, admitted through a four-question gate. If every question has an answer, the topic qualifies - any topic.

The gate first, because it also grades the menu

Before choosing, hold every candidate - including the seven below - against the same four questions:

  1. What is random here, and which distribution?
  2. Where do fitted probabilities appear in the output?
  3. What audit will the README contain?
  4. What single observation would falsify the approach?

If all four have concrete answers before you write code, the project qualifies regardless of topic. If any answer is "I'll figure that out later", shrink the project until it is not. These four questions are the brief from part 1 compressed into interview form, and interviewers will ask them in exactly this register.

The menu

Moderate ambitionProposals 1 to 4 each carry a complete arc - probability core, trained component, audit, runnable demo - at a size you can finish well.

1. The spam filter that knows when it is guessing moderate

Data. The UCI SMS Spam Collection: about 5,500 short messages, labelled ham or spam, small enough to ship derived extracts inside your repository. The Enron email corpus (~0.5M messages, released by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission during its investigation) is the ambitious alternative - larger and messier, and carrying real people's privacy with it; treat it accordingly.

Probabilistic core. A Bernoulli naive Bayes classifier as the baseline - word present or absent, independence assumed, the multiplication rule doing visible work - then a logistic regression like Week 4's for comparison. Calibration curves for both, because naive Bayes is famously overconfident and the comparison is where the lesson lives. Choose your operating threshold from stated false-positive economics: what does a ham message sent to the junk folder actually cost?

Portfolio bar. The week-6 audit card applied to a model you trained from raw text, with a demo showing live p-ham / p-spam on typed input, confidence shading moving as the reader edits the message. Everyone has built a spam classifier; almost nobody ships one that admits when it is guessing.

2. The weather-forecast auditor moderate

Data. Public forecasts versus observations for your own city, collected by you over two weeks or more. Open-Meteo's forecast API serves hourly precipitation probability on its free non-commercial tier without an API key, and archives past forecasts through its historical endpoint; national weather services publish CSVs too. Keep the raw rows upstream and ship only your small derived table.

Probabilistic core. Are the days the station calls "70% rain" actually rainy about 70% of the time? Bin each promised probability, compare promised frequency against observed frequency, draw the reliability diagram, compute expected calibration error on real public promises. Then do the same for temperature after rescaling, where the interesting failure modes hide.

Portfolio bar. Auditing someone else's probabilities is rarer than training your own model and reads as judgement rather than homework. It is also the cheapest project on the menu: no training pipeline, just honest counting - which is precisely why it stands out.

3. Dungeon-drop loot with provable rates moderate

Data. None needs collecting - you design the drop tables yourself, as published game systems implicitly do. That makes this the only proposal with zero licence exposure.

Probabilistic core. Categorical distributions over loot tiers, geometric waits until the rare drop. Monte Carlo verification that shipped rates match advertised ones: run millions of simulated kills with a seeded generator and put bootstrap intervals on the empirical rates. Where a "guaranteed drop within 90 pulls" pity system exists, derive its true effect on the distribution instead of taking the marketing at its word.

Portfolio bar. Game designers are a real audience for exactly this skill, and the demo animates drops while a live tally converges to the promised distribution - probability made watchable. Rigour aimed at a community that rarely receives it.

4. The A/B test simulator that shows why n matters moderate

Data. Entirely synthetic, seeded, generated in the browser - nothing to license, nothing to clean, reproducible by every reader.

Probabilistic core. Binomial sampling under two conversion rates. Power curves drawn live as the sample-size slider moves; the false-positive factory runs 1,000 A/A tests at the 0.05 level so the reader watches roughly fifty "wins" appear out of pure noise; intervals drawn beside point estimates throughout. This is week 1's sampling-fluctuation lesson and week 6's interval discipline turned into an explainer other people link to.

Portfolio bar. An interactive explainer that demonstrates fluency plus craft - the artifact teaches, which is a harder and more visible skill than the analysis alone. Every product team you might join argues about experiment size; hand them the tool that ends the argument.

High ambitionProposals 5 and 6 add a second dimension: joining a public argument, or binding probabilities to policy. Both demand more writing, and both repay it.

5. COMPAS re-audited with this course's tools high

Data. ProPublica's compas-analysis repository, published alongside their 2016 Machine Bias investigation: defendant records with risk scores and two-year rearrest outcomes.

Probabilistic core. Reproduce the false-positive-rate and false-negative-rate asymmetry with the Audit Bench's methods; fit the base-rate term; show the fairness impossibility result of Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) and Chouldechova (2017) holding on the real numbers - with different base rates across groups, equal calibration and equal error rates cannot coexist. Then write the paragraph most analyses skip: which criterion would you bind, and why, given what the score is used for.

Portfolio bar. Joining a public controversy with primary-source numbers and a stated value position is the strongest signal of judgement a junior portfolio can send. The bar is honesty under scrutiny: your reproduction will be checked against numbers people have argued about for years, so declare every threshold and denominator.

6. The triage queue simulator high

Data. Synthetic arrivals from a Poisson process you parameterise; severity scores from any Week 4 or Glass-Network-style classifier, or from a designed scoring function while you develop.

Probabilistic core. Decision thresholds under a capacity constraint: expected waiting times and expected false-prioritisations as the threshold moves, with the residual harm split into aleatoric (irreducible case-to-case variation) versus epistemic (your model's ignorance) - the split week 6 taught as "price the claim". The queue animation makes the cost of a threshold visceral in a way no table manages.

Portfolio bar. Probability, model, and policy in one artifact. This is the closest the menu comes to the decisions real ML teams make weekly, and the write-up - who waits, who is misranked, what would have to be true to justify a different threshold - is the part reviewers remember.

Very high ambitionOne entry, with a precondition attached.

7. Calibration dashboard for a public ML API very high

Data. Your own prompts, plus logged token log-probabilities from a free-tier LLM endpoint - the same softmax-over-tokens object week 5's Sampling Bench studied, observed in the wild. Check the provider's terms of service before collecting anything; free tiers vary in what logging and reuse they permit, and a capstone built on a violation fails craft outright.

Probabilistic core. Empirical frequency of the claimed top token against the stated log-probability mass, binned and plotted as reliability curves; repeat across temperature settings and watch the calibration move exactly as the temperature lesson predicts. Almost nobody audits frontier-model confidence with their own harness, which is exactly what makes the result worth reading.

Portfolio bar. Novel enough to feel risky, but the method is entirely week 6. The care requirement is real: document the terms check, freeze your prompt set, state the date range, and treat every provider number as a dated snapshot.

8. Your own proposal any level

Any topic qualifies that passes the four-question gate above. Past the gate, the design constraints are the same as everyone else's: a stranger must be able to run it, the README must carry the audit, and the failure catalogue applies with full force. Bring the idea to the rubric early - scoring a half-formed idea takes ten minutes and routinely saves weeks.

Check yourself

A learner wants to build "a sentiment analyser for my favourite game's reviews". Run the four-question gate. Which response shows the gate doing its job?
The gate asks four questions, not whether the domain sounds serious. Star ratings give a noisy but real label; a classifier reporting probabilities satisfies requirement 2; calibration per game is a natural group analysis; and "reviews describing crashes rated as positive sentiment" is a falsifier anyone can check. Hobby domains produce excellent portfolios precisely because the author cares enough to finish.
Why does auditing the weather service's published probabilities count as a full capstone when there is no trained model anywhere in it?
Requirement 2 asks for a trained component in the general case, but the deeper contract is the set of habits: naming what is random, comparing promised probabilities with observed frequencies, binning honestly, quoting intervals. The forecast auditor exercises every week-6 muscle on someone else's output - which is rarer in portfolios than yet another trained model, and reads as judgement. If you choose it, say in the README how you satisfy each of the five requirements rather than hoping the reviewer infers it.
Your teacher. Pick today, not this month. Write the four answers for exactly one proposal, in sentences, before you allow yourself to open an editor. Projects die in the gap between choosing and starting, not in the code.
