One table, six dials

10 min confusion matrix and thresholds as policy Every metric you will argue about this year is one slice of a two-by-two table. Learn to see the slices and the arguments become arithmetic.

The one-minute version

  • A decision converts each probability into yes or no at a threshold t. Crossing the four combinations of decision and truth gives the confusion matrix.
  • The six famous rates - sensitivity/TPR, specificity/TNR, FPR, FNR, PPV, NPV - are just cell counts divided by the right row or column total.
  • Moving the threshold trades cells against each other live. On this page's population at t = 0.5: TP 642, FP 221, TN 1009, FN 128.
  • Choosing t is a policy act: it decides who absorbs the errors before anyone computes a single rate.

Four cells, then nothing new

Your Week 4 logistic regression outputs probabilities; the world supplies true labels. To grade the pair you need one more ingredient, and it is not a metric - it is a decision rule: flag when p-hat is at least t. That rule splits every row into one of four fates. Flagged and truly positive (TP). Flagged and truly negative (FP). Released and truly negative (TN). Released and truly positive (FN). The four counts are the whole story; everything with a Greek-sounding name in the metrics literature is one of these four divided by the correct total:

sensitivity = TPR = TP/(TP+FN)        FNR = 1 - TPR
specificity = TNR = TN/(TN+FP)        FPR = 1 - TNR
PPV = precision = TP/(TP+FP)          NPV = TN/(TN+FN)

The left column conditions on truth (how good is the test given a case really is positive or negative); the right column of pairs conditions on the decision (how good is a flag once issued). Keeping the conditioning straight is the entire difficulty of metric talk. A model can be a magnificent detector by the top pair and an unusable alarm by the bottom one, and both statements are honest at once.

Do it once by hand on the page's own counts, because a rate you have divided yourself stops being a word. At t = 0.50 the two-group population gives TP 642, FP 221, TN 1009, FN 128, so 770 rows are truly positive and 1230 truly negative:

TPR = 642/770  = 0.8338      FNR = 128/770  = 0.1662
TNR = 1009/1230 = 0.8203     FPR = 221/1230 = 0.1797
PPV = 642/863  = 0.7439      NPV = 1009/1137 = 0.8874

accuracy = 1651/2000 = 0.8255   selection rate = 863/2000 = 0.4315

Notice which totals each denominator came from. TPR and FNR share the 770 true positives and therefore sum to 1; TNR and FPR share the 1230 true negatives and sum to 1. PPV and NPV share nothing - their denominators are the 863 flagged and the 1137 released rows - so they are free to move independently, and they do. That is the whole reason a model can look excellent on one pair and useless on the other.

The threshold is a policy, not a property

Nothing about the model changed as you dragged the slider. The scores sat where they sat; what moved was the line the organisation draws through them. At t = 0.50 on the two-group population the model burns 221 false flags to catch 642 of the 770 true positives. Slide to t = 0.80 and the false flags nearly evaporate - FP falls to 25, PPV climbs from 0.7439 to 0.9378 - while the misses pile up from 128 to 393 and recall halves to 0.4896. Accuracy moves by three and a half points, from 0.8255 to 0.7910, which is nothing beside what happened underneath it: the burden slid wholesale from one population to another. A cancer screen and a spam filter differ less in their models than in where they are willing to draw that line.

This is why the audit card you build in lesson 9 forces a single declared threshold into its headline section. Rates quoted without the threshold that produced them cannot be reproduced, compared, or defended - and quietly moving t until the numbers flatter you is threshold-shopping, which lesson 7 turns from a temptation into a visible offence.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You raise the flagging threshold t. Which pair moves in opposite directions?
A higher bar makes surviving flags purer (precision up) but misses more true positives (recall down). TPR and FPR do both fall together - attractive, but they move in the same direction, so they cannot be the answer. Accuracy has no forced direction at all.
A model has sensitivity 0.95 and PPV 0.10 on a screening task. In plain words:
Sensitivity 0.95 says nearly every true case is caught. PPV 0.10 says only one flag in ten is real. The pairing is typical at low prevalence - which is exactly why quoting one number without the other misleads.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice P1working

An evaluation set of 500 rows: the model flags 90; of those flagged, 60 are truly positive; there are 150 true positives in total on the set. Compute TP, FP, precision, recall, FNR and FPR, then read the numbers back as one sentence about cost.

Solution

TP = 60. FP = 90 - 60 = 30. Precision = 60/90 = 0.667. Recall = TP/(all positives) = 60/150 = 0.400. FN = 150 - 60 = 90, so FNR = 90/150 = 0.600. Negatives: 500 - 150 = 350; TN = 350 - 30 = 320; FPR = 30/350 = 0.0857.

The sentence: this model catches 40% of the real cases while burning 8.6% of all honest ones - a recall problem first and a precision problem second, so any fix should start below the threshold, not above it.

Where this came from

The confusion matrix and its derived rates are classical signal-detection theory; no primary paper is needed for arithmetic this old, though Swets' work on discrimination versus criterion is the deep root if you want it.
