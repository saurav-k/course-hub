Zero failures does not mean a zero percent failure rate

14 min build inside Maximum likelihood answers three kinds of question badly - peaks on the boundary, peaks in the clouds, and peaks that run away to infinity. This page hands you the tool for each.

The one-minute version

  • Forty payments, zero failures. The likelihood peaks hard against p = 0, so the MLE of the failure rate is exactly zero - an artefact of the boundary, not a measurement.
  • The honest summary is the likelihood interval: every p whose score sits within about 2 log units of the peak. Here that runs from 0 up to 4.9 percent.
  • The conventional exact 95% bound, solving (1-p)40 = 0.05 by hand, reaches 7.2 percent. Nobody should certify anything near zero off forty clean runs.
  • Worse than boundaries exist: on perfectly separated data the likelihood has no summit at all. That cliff is week 4's opening scene.

2. The zero-failure paradox, quantified

Your payment pipeline processed 40 orders today and failed on none of them. Lesson 0204's recipe says the maximum-likelihood estimate of the failure rate is k/n = 0/40 = 0, and lesson 0204 ended with the warning: the curve slams into the boundary. Would you ship the pipeline with an SLA promising a 0% failure rate? Before answering, read what the whole curve - not just its tip - is willing to say:

Here is the band's right-hand end computed by hand, so you can defend it without the browser:

  1. Write the score. With k = 0 failures the binomial likelihood collapses: L(p) = (1-p)40, peaking at p = 0 with LLmax = 0.
  2. Define the band. Keep every p whose score is within 2 log units of the peak: 40 ln(1-p) ≥ -2.
  3. Solve for the edge. ln(1-p) ≥ -2/40 = -0.05, so 1-p ≥ e-0.05 = 0.9512, giving p ≤ 0.0488.
  4. Say it in words. Forty clean payments are consistent with any true failure rate from zero up to about 4.9%. A rate of 2% scores only 0.81 units below the best - barely distinguishable.
  5. Cross-check conventionally. The classical exact 95% upper bound solves (1-p)40 = 0.05: p = 1 - 0.051/40 = 1 - 0.928 = 7.2%. Different rule, same verdict: nowhere near zero.

Notice what would actually move the band: more orders. At n = 400 the same arithmetic gives p ≤ 0.005 - ten times tighter, because exposure multiplies evidence (lesson 0203's sharpened peaks, wearing business clothes). Zero failures in forty runs is not knowledge about the rate; it is a promise about how wide your ignorance currently is.

4. The week's design problem: choose your instrument

Choose your instrument

You must estimate the daily error rate of a payment pipeline and decide whether a rare-but-catastrophic failure mode (rate possibly below 1 in 10,000) exists. You may instrument the system with exactly one of:

  1. (a) full logging for one day;
  2. (b) sampled logging (10% of traffic) for thirty days;
  3. (c) targeted verbose logging, triggered by a cheap heuristic, for thirty days.

Pick one. Defend it with a likelihood argument - what counts do you observe under each option, what is the Poisson mean, and which schedule gives the tighter upper bound on a tiny rate? Then state what observed result would falsify your choice.

Rubric, for self-assessment:

  • Essential. Names the count statistic and its Poisson mean under each option.
  • Strong. Recognises that total exposure - rate multiplied by window - controls how small a rate you can bound, not the sampling fraction alone.
  • Strong. States the falsifier as an observable: "if sampled logging yields zero errors while the heuristic trigger fires more than k times, my choice was wrong."
  • Exceptional. Says what the likelihood cannot tell you here: failure modes the heuristic never triggers on are invisible to option (c) no matter how long it runs.

Write it as prose a reviewer could check line by line - this is the week's portfolio artefact for the reasoning skills, alongside the three builds.

Check yourself

k = 0 failures in n = 40 payments; the MLE of the failure rate is 0. Best next action?
The data speak through their whole curve: forty clean runs leave any rate up to about 4.9% (7.2% at the exact 95% level) standing. Certifying zero mistakes the boundary artefact for a measurement; waiting for failures wastes information you already have; 0.5 ignores the data entirely.
Two hypotheses tie exactly on likelihood. What would a Bayesian reach for that pure maximum likelihood deliberately leaves out?
Likelihood measures explanation quality only; when it ties, prior belief breaks the tie, and Bayes rule is the machinery that combines them properly - treated deck-faithfully in Statistical Foundations lesson 0023. Grids, bins and seeds all act on the likelihood side, which is exactly the side that just declared a draw.
