Why one line is not enough

9 min a geometric failure Two moons meet every straight boundary you can draw and beat it. The plateau you are about to watch is not an optimisation problem, and no amount of training will move it.

The one-minute version

  • The trainer gains a dataset switch today. Blobs stay separable by a line; two moons never are.
  • On moons, one neuron stalls at 0.870 accuracy and stays there for twenty thousand epochs at every learning rate from 0.5 to 10. A brute-force sweep of every line, at 3600 angles and every threshold, tops out at 0.875. The neuron is not under-trained; it is within half a point of the best line that exists.
  • "Just train longer" and "just retrain" are the two reflexes this page exists to kill. The model class, not the training loop, is what failed.
  • The fix - stacking neurons so their votes compose - is lesson 3's subject. Today you collect the evidence that makes it necessary.

1. The switch that breaks the toy

Last lesson's trainer was pinned to blobs because blobs are kind: each class lives in one convex lump, and some straight line always separates them well enough. The dataset picker below adds the two datasets the rest of the week runs on. Two moons interleaves two crescents, each shifted half a period out of phase. Two spirals winds the classes around each other, which is the hardest case we will use. Both are generated from seed 42 exactly like the blobs were, so your points match everyone else's down to the pixel.

2. The plateau is geometry, not effort

Here is the argument, made once in algebra so the picture cannot bluff you. A single neuron's boundary is the set of points where z = w1x + w2y + b = 0 - a straight line, every time, whatever weights you pick. So the question "can one neuron fit two moons?" is exactly "is there any straight line separating the crescents?". There is not: each crescent wraps around the other's tip, so any line placing the upper moon's mass on the positive side also places part of the lower moon there. The best achievable line slices off a corner of each crescent and nothing you do to w and b changes that.

The two reflexesYou have now watched both fail on purpose. Reflex one: "train more" - the loss curve flattens and the epoch counter runs away with nothing to show for it. Reflex two: "retrain, maybe bad luck" - reset gives a fresh line, and every fresh line hits the same wall, because every possible line is wrong in the same place. Week 3 taught you that maximum likelihood over a fixed model class finds the best of what the class contains; it never promised the class contained anything good.

3. What a fix must therefore do

Saying "use a network" too early sounds like magic. Say instead what property the fix needs: the model must be able to draw boundaries that are bent - specifically, bent around each moon's tip. One way to get a bent boundary from straight pieces is to let several lines vote: each hidden neuron contributes its own straight edge, and a final vote combines the edges into a region with corners. You can already verify the idea by hand: problem P2 in this week's problems proves no single line separates the four XOR corners, and problem P3 builds a working two-layer fix from just two threshold voters. The network in lesson 3 is that trick made smooth and trainable by gradient descent.

You do not have to take the ceiling on trust. There are only two free directions in a line's orientation and one in its offset, so the whole space of straight boundaries can be searched by hand: sweep the angle through 3600 steps, and for each angle project all 200 points onto it and try every threshold between neighbouring points. That is 720,000 candidate lines, which is every line that classifies the dataset differently from its neighbours, and the best of them gets 175 of 200 points right. Here are all four datasets, each measured that way and beside the number the trained neuron actually reaches:

Read the gap column, because it is the whole diagnostic in one number. Gradient descent is doing its job everywhere: on the first three datasets it lands within a single misclassified point of the theoretical best line, and on spirals its shortfall is the loss surface being awkward rather than the class being small. What varies enormously is the second column - the best the class can do at all. Blobs: a line suffices. Moons: a line loses a quarter of one crescent no matter what. Spirals: a line is nearly worthless. Nothing in the training loop can move column two, which is why tuning it is wasted effort here.

One caution before you move on, because it protects you later in real work: the ceiling number itself is an accident of where the crescents sit, not a law. Raise the noise and it drops - noisy moons cap at 0.855 rather than 0.875, as the table shows. What does not move is the shape of the argument. For any fixed placement, either the class contains a good boundary or it does not, and training length appears nowhere in that sentence.

4. Check yourself

On two moons, a single neuron plateaus at 0.870 accuracy and stays there forever. The most likely cause?
The failure is architectural, not optimisational: the boundary of a single neuron is the line z = 0, and every possible line misclassifies part of at least one crescent. The brute-force sweep in section 3 puts the best line at 0.875, so the trained neuron is already within one point of the ceiling. A smaller rate changes the path, not the destination; more epochs only walk the flat part of the loss surface more thoroughly; and re-seeding hands you a different wrong line. This is why the diagnostic in section 2 asks about the model class before anything else.
