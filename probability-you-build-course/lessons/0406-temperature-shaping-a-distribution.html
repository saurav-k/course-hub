Temperature: shaping a distribution

10 min the knob every API exposes One number divides the logits before softmax: below one sharpens the distribution toward argmax, above one flattens it toward uniform. The same slider sits in every large-model API you will ever call.

The one-minute version

  • Temperature T divides every logit before softmax. Small T exaggerates differences; large T shrinks them toward equal.
  • On the bench's defaults: Paris takes 94.6% of mass at T = 0.5, 67.7% at T = 1, 40.2% at T = 2. As T → 0 sampling becomes greedy; as T grows the bars crawl toward uniform 16.7% each - at the slider's maximum of 3 Paris is still on 31.2%, so flattening is far slower than sharpening.
  • The token strip animates repeated samples so you can see the reshaped distribution behave.
  • Temperature is not creativity, not hardware, and not speed - it is one division. And a sharper distribution is not a more correct one; week 6 drives that wedge all the way in.

1. One division, three regimes

Last lesson's bench fixed T = 1. The general recipe is softmax(s / T): divide every logit by the same positive T, then exponentiate and normalise exactly as before. Because softmax responds only to differences, dividing by T rescales those differences: at T = 0.5 the gap between Paris's 3.2 and cheese's −0.5 reads as 7.4 instead of 3.7; at T = 3 it reads as 1.23. 2. Drive the bench

Watch what the slider does not do. It does not reorder the candidates, ever. Dividing every logit by the same positive T preserves their order exactly, so the ranking at T = 0.05 is the ranking at T = 3 and the ranking of the raw logits; only the spacing changes. It does not change the model: the logits are whatever they are, and T is applied after. And it does not make the model better or worse in any sense the loss can measure - which is precisely why choosing T is a product decision about behaviour, not a training correction. Problem P7 of the problem set walks both limits formally: as T → 0 all mass lands on argmax s, and as T →∞ the distribution approaches exact uniformity.

3. What real stacks expose

Everything you just drove exists, parameter for parameter, in production text generation. Model providers expose temperature in their completion APIs, and the standard documentation of sampling strategies describes exactly this loop - compute next-token logits, divide by temperature, softmax, draw - with T set per request by the calling engineer. When a product sets "temperature 0.7" for its summariser, it is doing the slider above over fifty-thousand candidates instead of six. Two honest caveats keep the mapping clean: real stacks add extra knobs on top (top-k and top-p truncate the tail before sampling), and temperature interacts with logit scale - two models with different calibration need different temperatures to show equally sharp behaviour. The second caveat is big enough that week 6 gives it its own lesson.

Sharp is not correctIt is tempting to hear "T = 0.05 puts 100% on Paris" and conclude low temperature makes a model right. What it makes the model is confident. Confidence and correctness are separable properties: a miscalibrated model at low temperature is wrong loudly instead of softly. Guo et al. showed modern networks are systematically miscalibrated (arXiv:1706.04599); the audit toolkit that separates sharpness from correctness arrives in week 6. Until then, hold the distinction in vocabulary: temperature tunes behaviour; calibration measures trustworthiness.

Dividing logits by a small T stretches their differences, softmax exaggerates them, and sampling concentrates on the argmax - at the limit it is pure greedy decoding. Creativity folklore has the direction backwards: flattening is high temperature. Speed is untouched, every candidate still evaluates; and calibration - whether stated probabilities match reality - is a separate property that temperature scaling addresses only when fitted on held-out data, which is week 6's machinery.
