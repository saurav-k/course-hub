A random variable is a function, and the seed plays the outcome

10 min foundation Press Regenerate and everything moves; retype the same seed and nothing does. That asymmetry is the entire definition, running in your browser.

The one-minute version

  • A random variable is not a wiggly number. It is a function from outcomes to numbers: fixed machinery, different answers per input.
  • In this week's builds the seed is the outcome omega, and the drawing procedure is the function X. The canvas shows X(omega).
  • Same seed means same outcome means the identical picture - pixel for pixel, verifiable by a checksum, not by squinting.
  • The raw material every sampler this week will transform is the uniform: Math.random() is U(0,1) and nothing else.

1. What the Regenerate button actually does

Type a seed into the build below, note the picture and the print it reports, then press Regenerate a few times. Every press draws a new starfield. Now retype your original seed exactly, with the same star count, and watch the canvas snap back - not to something similar, but to that picture, confirmed by the checksum in the readout. Nothing about this is vague enough to require trust in your eyes: the print is a checksum of every dot position, and it will match to the last hex digit.

2. The definition, read aloud

A random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers. Formally:

X : Ω → ℝ Read as: X is a rule that takes each outcome omega in the outcome space Omega and returns one ordinary number. Omega is fixed; X is fixed; which outcome arrives is what chance decides.

In this week's builds the mapping is concrete. The outcome space Omega is the set of seeds - all the whole numbers the seed field accepts. The function X is the drawing procedure: place n dots, at positions determined by the seed. The value X(omega) is one particular starfield. When you pressed Regenerate, the page sampled a fresh omega from the seed field; when you typed the seed back, you named the old outcome again, and a function cannot answer the same question twice with different answers.

This is the fix for the most common misconception in the subject. If you leave this course remembering only that "random variable" names a thing that varies, you will mistrust every formula downstream, because the formulas all treat X as something with stable properties - a mean, a spread, a shape. Those properties live in the function. They hold for seed 42 and seed 9137 alike, which is precisely why the two canvases, for all their pixel differences, are instances of one distribution.

3. The raw material: uniforms

Every dot position in the build came from a call to a seeded uniform generator. The continuous uniform distribution U(0,1) spreads its probability evenly over the interval from 0 to 1:

Hold on to that last point. The glow layer will want centre-heavy brightness, the rings will want long-tailed radii, the splats will want bell-shaped spread - and none of these shapes exists in the generator. The whole technical story of Week 2 is transforming uniforms into the distribution you actually want, by rejection on page 0103, by inverse transform on page 0104, and by Box-Muller on page 0105.
