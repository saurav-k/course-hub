Two curves, two questions

12 min PR and ROC, and the perfect score that proves nothing One curve asks what your flags are worth. The other asks how well you sort. Confusing the two questions is how a model with a flawless AUC ships uselessly.

The one-minute version

  • Precision-recall conditions on flags: of the things I raised, how many mattered, and how much did I catch? It degrades gracefully when positives are rare.
  • ROC conditions on truth: true-positive rate against false-positive rate across every threshold. Its area has one exact meaning: the probability a random positive outranks a random negative.
  • This week's population: AUC 0.9126, average precision 0.8766. Both honest, neither sufficient.
  • The degenerate toggle shows a scorer with AUC exactly 1.0000 whose probabilities all sit between 0.505 and 0.515: at any sane threshold it flags everyone, and its numbers carry no information.

The same slider, swept

Lesson 2's threshold slider produced one confusion matrix per position. Sweep it from 0 to 1 and each position leaves a dot. Connect the dots twice - once in the precision-recall plane, once in the ROC plane - and you have the two most quoted figures in machine learning evaluation. They contain identical information about the scores; they frame it for different questions.

Precision-recall frames the operator's question. Precision answers "of my flags, how many are real"; recall answers "of the real cases, how many did I flag". When positives are 2% of the world - fraud, disease, outage - recall can look heroic while precision drowns, which is why rare-event work lives on the PR curve. ROC frames the separation question. True-positive rate against false-positive rate ignores prevalence entirely: it measures whether the score ranks positives above negatives. That prevalence-independence makes ROC portable between problems and quietly misleading inside one.

Reading AUC without lying to yourself

AUC has exactly one definition worth memorising: the probability that a uniformly chosen positive receives a higher score than a uniformly chosen negative. 0.5 is coin-flip sorting, 1.0 is a perfect sort, and everything else is a statement about ranking and nothing more. Four misreadings account for nearly every misuse in the wild: that AUC is the fraction classified correctly (it is not; that needs a threshold), that calibrated probabilities follow from it (they do not; lesson 4 gives you the tool to check), that some best threshold inherits the glory (no threshold is privileged by the integral), and that AUC improvements mean deployment improvements (lesson 4's degenerate case gains AUC while gaining nothing deployable).

The degenerate population on this page is built to be impossible to forget. Every row's rank is essentially correct, so the ROC curve pins itself into the ideal corner and AUC prints 1.0000. But the probabilities were then squashed into a band barely ten thousandths wide around 0.51 - so at t = 0.50 every single row is flagged, precision collapses to the prevalence (0.497), and no threshold separates anyone from anything. Ranking was perfect; the system was useless. If a vendor ever leads with "AUC 0.99", this page is your counter-question: at what thresholds do the probabilities actually live?

Average precision - the area under the PR curve, computed as the step-sum of precision weighted by recall gained - is the PR-flavoured cousin: 0.8766 here against the same model's 0.9126 AUC. Neither subsumes the other; they answer their own questions, and an audit quotes the pair with the threshold policy stated beside them.

Where this came from

The ROC predates machine learning by decades - it was built for radar receiver operators, hence the name - and the ranking-probability reading of AUC is standard signal-detection theory. Average precision follows the step-integral convention used by common evaluation libraries. AUC 0.9126, AP 0.8766 and every number in the degenerate case were recomputed mechanically from this page's seeded generators before publication.
