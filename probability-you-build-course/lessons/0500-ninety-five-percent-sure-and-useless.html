Ninety-five percent sure and useless

10 min the base-rate trap, live A fraud scorer that is right about 82% of transactions and has still lost money to a policy that never flags anything. This week you stop building models and start judging them.

The one-minute version

  • Every panel you build this week serves one document: the probabilistic audit - a printed critique of a set of predictions.
  • Accuracy answers to prevalence. On this week's rare-event population (prevalence 0.0195) the strategy "flag nobody" scores 0.9805.
  • The trained model scores 0.8230 at t = 0.5 - fifteen points below the baseline that never thinks. When it does flag, only 8.4% of its flags are real.
  • The fix is not a better single metric. It is refusing to read any metric without its baseline, threshold and costs attached.

The fraud desk

You are handed a deployed fraud scorer and its evaluation run: 2000 transactions, of which 39 turned out to be fraudulent, a prevalence of 0.0195. The vendor's deck quotes accuracy 0.8230. Before reading another slide, compute the accuracy of the dumbest possible competitor: a policy that says "not fraud" every single time. It scores 1 - 0.0195 = 0.9805. The vendor loses to a policy with no model at all, by fifteen points.

This is the trap the whole week exists to make impossible to fall into again. Accuracy measures agreement with reality, and when one outcome is fifty times more common than the other, agreeing with reality is easy without ever detecting the interesting part. The vendor's model does catch 82% of the true frauds at its default threshold - and simultaneously places false accusations on honest transactions so freely that fewer than one flag in twelve is real (precision 0.084). Both facts live inside 0.8230. Neither is visible from outside it.

The populations are generated in your browser from fixed seeds, so what you see matches what every other reader sees, byte for byte. The rare-event generator draws each row's label as fraudulent with probability 0.02 and gives positives a stronger latent signal than negatives; the two-group generator, which later lessons need because its groups carry different base rates, draws group 0 with prevalence 0.30 and group 1 with 0.55, then scores both groups with one shared model.

Demote accuracy, do not delete it

After the demotion it is tempting to throw the number away. Resist. The honest position is that accuracy is a witness, not a judge: it testifies fully only once you supply the context that makes it interpretable - the prevalence it was measured against, the baseline it must beat, and the threshold that produced it. Written as a decomposition,

accuracy = P(decision = no) x specificity + P(decision = yes) x sensitivity

always-no accuracy = 1 - prevalence

every term except sensitivity belongs partly to the world. Two evaluators quoting "82%" and "98%" about the same model are not disagreeing; they are standing at different baselines. When you assemble the audit card in lesson 9, the accuracy line always prints beside its always-no twin, because a headline without its baseline is how dashboards lie politely.

The same arithmetic wears a Bayesian coat. The probability that a flagged transaction is truly fraudulent - the quantity the fraud desk actually acts on - is a posterior, and at low prevalence the prior crushes every likelihood. That derivation is done carefully in Bayes rule in Statistical Foundations of ML; here you only need to recognise that the vendor quoted you a likelihood while your business runs on posteriors. For the formal sibling treatment of the trap itself, see the base rate trap in Math for ML.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A screening test runs on a population with 1% prevalence. Its sensitivity is 90% and its false-positive rate is 5%. Compute the positive predictive value exactly - what fraction of flagged people are true positives - and write one sentence a manager could read about what the result means.

Solution

Work per 10,000 people. True positives: 100 carry the condition, and the test finds 90% of them, so TP = 90. False positives: the 9,900 healthy produce 5% flags, FP = 495. PPV = 90 / (90 + 495) = 90 / 585 = 0.1538.

The sentence: even with excellent-looking operating characteristics, a flagged case is more than five times as likely to be clean as dirty at this prevalence - the alarm is mostly false, and no threshold tweak inside the test can change that while sensitivity stays at 90%.

This PPV-from-prevalence computation is Bayes' theorem; the full derivation lives in Bayes rule.
