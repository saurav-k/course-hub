Week 1 · Consolidated problems · paper and code

Nine problems that make you produce every number the planner prints

working 4 min Reading time only - allow about 90 minutes to work the set. Graded from warm-up to hard, two are code deliverables, and the design problem at the end is the week's exam. Every solution stays hidden until you ask.

How to work this set

  • Attempt on paper first - or in your editor for the two code problems - before opening any solution.
  • Every problem ends with a sanity check line so you can catch your own arithmetic before it misleads you.
  • Problems 1 to 7 reuse the frozen catalogue: nano $0.00054, frontier $0.012 per request, pf = 0.97, a = 0.95, b = 0.15 unless stated otherwise.
  • The design problem closes the week: pick a policy family for an SLA and defend it with numbers your own planner produces.

Practice about 90 minutes, with paper and editor

Practice 1warm-uplesson 0001-0002

One nano call, ps = 0.8. Write the sample space and both event probabilities; verify the three axioms on them; then compute P(wrong) twice - once directly, once through the complement rule.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the space. S = {correct, wrong}, with P(correct) = 0.8 and P(wrong) = 0.2.
  2. Check non-negativity. Both probabilities are ≥ 0.
  3. Check normalisation. 0.8 + 0.2 = 1, so the whole space carries probability exactly 1.
  4. Price wrongness twice. Directly: the event {wrong} has probability 0.2. By complement: P(wrong) = 1 - P(correct) = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2. They agree because the two events partition S.

Sanity check. 0.8 + 0.2 = 1 must hold, and the two routes to P(wrong) must agree - either mismatch means a partition error.

Practice 2easylesson 0002

Five independent nano calls, ps = 0.8, policy takes the request if any sample is correct. Compute P(success) exactly, and the policy's expected cost per request at $0.00054 per call.

Show the worked solution
  1. Invert to the miss. All five miss with probability (0.2)5 = 0.00032, by independence.
  2. Subtract from certainty. P(any correct) = 1 - 0.00032 = 0.99968.
  3. Price certainty in cost. You always pay for five calls whether they hit or not: E[cost] = 5 x 0.00054 = $0.0027.

Sanity check. Success must beat the single-call 0.8, and cost must be exactly five times a single call - here 0.99968 > 0.8 and 0.0027 = 5 x 0.00054.

Practice 3workinglessons 0003-0005

Cascade numbers: ps = 0.8, pf = 0.97, a = 0.95, b = 0.15, c-nano = $0.00054, c-frontier = $0.012. Compute P(ship), P(final answer correct), and E[cost] per request - deriving each by conditional probability, path products, and total probability rather than quoting them.

Show the worked solution
  1. Mix the universes for P(ship). P(ship) = 0.8 x 0.95 + 0.2 x 0.15 = 0.76 + 0.03 = 0.79.
  2. Add the two success paths. Ship-and-right contributes 0.76; refusal happens with probability 0.21 regardless of hidden correctness, and the frontier lands there: 0.21 x 0.97 = 0.2037. Total 0.76 + 0.2037 = 0.9637.
  3. Ride the same branches for cost. Nano is certain: $0.00054. Frontier rides refusal: 0.21 x 0.012 = 0.00252. E[cost] = 0.00054 + 0.00252 = $0.00306.

Sanity check. Quality must sit between nano-alone (0.80) and frontier-alone (0.97): 0.9637 does. Cost must sit between their costs ($0.00054 and $0.012): $0.00306 does.

Practice 4workinglesson 0007

Correlated failures: suppose the probability that all five samples of Practice 2 fail together is 0.05 - not the independence product 0.25 = 0.00032. For the any-correct policy, what is the true P(success), and how much did dependence take?

Show the worked solution
  1. Invert the new miss mass. P(any correct) = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95.
  2. Compare against the promise. The independence formula printed 0.99968; the gap is 0.99968 - 0.95 = 0.0497.
  3. Name the thief. Nearly five points of success rate vanished into correlated failure without one input changing except the independence assumption.

Sanity check. The truth must sit below the independent promise, since clumping concentrates misses onto outcomes where overlapping failures waste samples - 0.95 < 0.99968 confirms direction.

Practice 5workinglesson 0008

Majority vote arithmetic: n = 9, ps = 0.75. Compute P(majority correct) by summing k = 5 to 9 of C(9,k)(0.75)k(0.25)(9-k), showing the C(9,5) term's full chain.

Show the worked solution
  1. Build k = 5 in full. C(9,5) = 9!/(5!4!) = 126 patterns; each pattern costs (0.75)5(0.25)4 = 0.23730 x 0.00391 = 0.000927; contribution 126 x 0.000927 = 0.11680.
  2. Tally upward. k = 6: 84 x 0.002780 = 0.23360. k = 7: 36 x 0.008341 = 0.30034. k = 8: 9 x 0.025028 = 0.22525. k = 9: 1 x 0.075085 = 0.07508.
  3. Add the winners. 0.11680 + 0.23360 + 0.30034 + 0.22525 + 0.07508 = 0.95107.

Sanity check. The total should sit just above 0.95 for p = 0.75 with nine ballots - plausible, since voting amplifies a solid edge without reaching the any-correct ceiling.

Practice 6workinglesson 0006

Bayes on a coarse grid: H = {0.5, 0.7, 0.9} with a uniform prior; observe 7 correct in 10. Likelihoods are proportional to h7(1-h)3 - the constant C(10,7) cancels in the normalisation. Compute the posterior over all three hypotheses and E[h | data].

Show the worked solution
  1. Weight each hypothesis. 0.510 = 0.000977; 0.77 x 0.33 = 0.002224; 0.97 x 0.13 = 0.000478.
  2. Normalise by the evidence. Total weight = 0.003679. Posteriors: 0.000977/0.003679 = 0.266; 0.002224/0.003679 = 0.604; 0.000478/0.003679 = 0.130.
  3. Average with the posterior. E[h | data] = 0.266 x 0.5 + 0.604 x 0.7 + 0.130 x 0.9 = 0.673.

Sanity check. Posterior mass should concentrate near the observed rate 0.7 and starve the extremes - 0.604 on h = 0.7 versus 0.130 on h = 0.9 is exactly that shape, and the three posteriors must sum to 1: 0.266 + 0.604 + 0.130 = 1.000, right within rounding.

Practice 7hardlesson 0005

Threshold design: find the largest false-alarm rate b at which the cascade can still match always-frontier (P(final correct) = pf = 0.97), holding ps = 0.8 and a = 0.95. Solve algebraically, then interpret the answer against the shipped default b = 0.15.

Hint

Write P(final correct) as a function of b using Lesson 5: psa + (1 - (psa + (1-ps)b))pf. Set it equal to pf and solve for b. Watch which terms cancel.

Show the worked solution
  1. Expand the ship rate. P(ship) = 0.76 + 0.2b, so P(final correct) = 0.76 + (0.24 - 0.2b) x 0.97.
  2. Set parity. 0.76 + (0.24 - 0.2b) x 0.97 = 0.97 requires (0.24 - 0.2b) = 0.21/0.97 = 0.21649.
  3. Solve. 0.2b = 0.24 - 0.21649 = 0.02351, so b = 0.1175.
  4. Interpret. At b = 0 the cascade peaks at 0.9928, comfortably above frontier parity; the shipped default b = 0.15 overshoots the threshold, which is why Lesson 5's cascade reads 0.9637 < 0.97. Tightening the check below 0.118 buys frontier-matching quality at a quarter of the price.

Sanity check. The threshold must lie strictly between 0 (where quality exceeds parity) and the value where escalation vanishes... check both ends: b = 0 gives 0.9928 > 0.97 and b = 0.15 gives 0.9637 < 0.97, so a crossing between them is guaranteed and 0.1175 sits in (0, 0.15).

Practice 8codelesson 0008, milestone M4

Implement pMajority(n, p) for odd n with the multiplicative binomial loop - no factorials anywhere. Validate it against a 40,000-trial simulator for n = 9 at p in {0.5, 0.6, 0.75}, and report the worst absolute deviation.

Hint

Accumulate r = r * (n - i) / (i + 1) for i from 0 to k-1 to get C(n,k); keep the running product interleaved so intermediates never explode.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the counter. The loop form from lesson 0008: multiply by (n-i), divide by (i+1), never leaving the neighbourhood of 1.
  2. Sum the winners. For k from (n+1)/2 to n, accumulate choose(n,k) x pk(1-p)(n-k).
  3. Fuzz against simulation. At 40k trials the simulated frequency differs from the analytic value by roughly 0.002-0.004 at these settings; the worst deviation across the three p values should land under 0.01.
  4. Know the scaling. Monte Carlo error shrinks like 1/sqrt(trials), so quadrupling trials halves the gap - useful when a reviewer asks why your tolerance is what it is.

Sanity check. Deviations must shrink as trials grow, and pMajority(9, 0.75) should print 0.95107 to five places - matching Practice 5's hand sum.

Practice 9codelesson 0006, milestone M3

Extend the Bayes updater to 200 observations without underflow, using log-space accumulation: add log-likelihoods, subtract the maximum, exponentiate, normalise. Show it matches the naive product computation through about 50 observations and stays finite at 200 where naive multiplication has underflowed.

Hint

The log of a product is a sum of logs: log w(h) = log prior + k log h + (m-k) log(1-h). Subtracting the largest exponent before exponentiating keeps every remaining value in double range.

Show the worked solution
  1. Accumulate in logs. Per observation add log(h) or log(1-h) to hypothesis h's accumulator, starting from log(prior).
  2. Shift before exponentiating. Let m = max accumulator; compute exp(acc - m) per hypothesis; normalise. The shift subtracts out in the division, changing nothing mathematically while rescuing everything numerically.
  3. Demonstrate agreement, then divergence. Through 50 observations naive products still representable: posteriors match log-space to several decimals. By 200, naive weights have hit 0 (underflow) while log-space posteriors stay finite and sum to 1.
  4. Watch the drift. As m grows, E[h | data] converges toward the true observed rate - with more data the grid sharpens around reality whatever the prior said.

Sanity check. Posteriors must still sum to 1 at every length, and E[h | data] must move monotonically toward the observed hit rate as evidence accumulates.

The design problem the week's exam - allow 45 minutes plus planner time

Design problemcapstonethe SLA ladder

Your coding-assistant feature must hold 98 percent correct answers on 50,000 requests per month, inside a $600 monthly inference budget. Three tiers from the frozen catalogue are available ($0.00054 / $0.00480 / $0.01200 per request). Choose a policy family - single-tier, n-sample, cascade, or a hybrid ladder with a second check - justify it with numbers your own planner produces, and state what evidence would falsify your model.

Hint

Start from the budget: $600 / 50,000 = $0.012 per request - exactly one frontier call, so the naive plan spends the whole budget on the top tier. Then ask whether a cheaper family clears 98 percent, and under which assumptions.

Show one defensible answer
  1. Read the budget as a unit price. $0.012/request permits anything up to frontier-always. Any-correct nano sampling clears 98 percent at ps = 0.8 with n = 3 (1 - 0.23 = 0.992) for $0.00162 - roughly seven times under budget - if answers verify themselves and samples run independently.
  2. Own the assumption. That plan lives or dies on independence. If clumping runs at c = 5 percent, the ceiling 1 - c = 0.95 falls short of the bar, and the plan collapses without any parameter changing.
  3. Hedge with structure, not hope. A cascade - nano with a strong check, escalating to frontier on refusal at a = 0.95 - reaches 0.9637 at $0.00306, still under half the unit budget, and degrades gracefully: its guarantee survives clumping because it makes one cheap attempt, not a batch.
  4. State falsification concretely. Example: "if validation shows over 2 percent of batches failing entirely together, the sampling guarantee is void and I fall back to the cascade; if validation drives E[ps] below 0.62 under Bayes, even the cascade misses 0.98 and I move to hybrid laddering." Numbers, owners, triggers.

Sanity check. Whatever family you pick, its P(correct) derivation must come from total probability with simulation agreeing within noise, and its E[cost] times 50,000 must clear $600 with visible headroom - a plan landing within 5 percent of the budget is a plan one pricing change breaks.

Show the self-assessment rubric

Score yourself 0 to 2 on each; 8 or above is a pass, and any zero names your next study session.

  • Priors explicit. Every probability in your argument traces to the catalogue, to measurement, or to a declared assumption - none arrived by vibes.
  • Maths checks. P(final correct) and E[cost] were derived by total probability, not asserted; your simulation column agrees within stated noise.
  • Sensitivity owned. The plan was re-derived at ps shifted ±0.1, and your write-up says whether the policy choice flips (fragile) or holds (robust) across that range.
  • Independence named. The write-up states where independence is assumed and what clumped failure would do to the SLA if it failed.
  • Falsification concrete. A specific observable - "over 2 percent fully-failed batches", "E[ps] below 0.62" - with the fallback it triggers, not a vague vow to monitor.
Your teacher. Do not grade yourself on speed. This set deliberately reuses one catalogue everywhere so that by problem 7 the numbers feel like furniture - and furniture is the point: a professional who can price a cascade from memory in a meeting is dangerous in the best way. If any solution surprised you, its linked lesson is where the surprise lives; go poke the build until intuition and arithmetic agree again.
