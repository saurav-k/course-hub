Do the probabilities mean anything?

12 min reliability diagrams, ECE and its fragility A model can rank flawlessly and flag sensibly while its numbers are fiction. Calibration is the promise that when it says 70%, things happen 70% of the time - and modern models break that promise by default.

The one-minute version

  • Calibration: among cases scored about 0.7, about 70% should be positive. The reliability diagram checks it bin by bin against the diagonal.
  • This week's two-group scorer is miscalibrated: ECE 0.0843. Its curve sits below the diagonal for eight bins and crosses above it in the top two - one direction is not the whole story.
  • The honest control scorer scores ECE 0.0169: same task shape, honest numbers.
  • ECE has a dirty secret: change the binning and you change the number. Same control model, same rows: 0.0154 at 5 bins, 0.0169 at 10, 0.0290 at 20.

The promise frequencies make

AUC graded the sort. But your Week 4 model outputs something richer than ranks: numbers claiming to be probabilities, which downstream code will multiply, threshold and price. Those numbers carry a testable promise, first stated in the forecasting literature by DeGroot and Fienberg: among all cases given p-hat near 0.7, the observed frequency of positives should be near 70%. If that holds at every confidence level, the model is calibrated - its probabilities mean what they say.

The reliability diagram tests the promise directly. Partition predicted probabilities into equal-width bins; within each bin plot the observed positive frequency against the mean predicted probability. A perfectly calibrated model traces the diagonal. Points below it mark overconfidence - the model promised more than reality delivered. Points above it mark underconfidence.

Summarising the bend, honestly

The expected calibration error collapses the diagram to one number: the weighted mean absolute gap between observed frequency and mean predicted probability across bins,

ECE = sum over bins b of (n_b / n) x | observed_b - mean-p-hat_b |

On the default population that is 0.0843. The shape matters more than the size, and this shape has two halves. Across the bottom eight bins the scorer promises more than reality delivers - its lowest bin promises 5.8% and sees 1.4%, its 0.7 bin promises 74.8% and sees 68.7% - so those points sit below the diagonal. In the top two bins it reverses and promises less than it delivers: 85.1% against an observed 89.6%, then 94.4% against 98.0%. The curve crosses the diagonal at roughly 0.82.

Read that as a shape rather than a label. A score whose probabilities were merely too extreme would sit below the diagonal above 0.5 and above it below 0.5, pivoting at the middle; a score whose probabilities were too timid would do the opposite. This one pivots near 0.82, which is what a curve looks like when it is both too flat and shifted. Keep the picture rather than a slogan: lesson 5's single-parameter fix pivots at 0.5, so it can straighten most of this bend and provably cannot straighten all of it - which is exactly why that lesson ends by naming what a second parameter buys.

Two cautions before you trust any ECE printout. First, the binning arbitrariness you just watched: the same honest control produces three defensible numbers depending on bin count, because fine bins leave sparse tails whose gaps swing wildly. An audit states its binning next to its ECE, always. Second, ECE says nothing about resolution - a forecaster that outputs the overall base rate for everyone is nearly perfectly calibrated and perfectly useless, which deserves its own lesson (6, with the Murphy decomposition).

This is the modern default, not your bug

If your deep model's diagram bends, you have reproduced one of the most robust findings in the field. Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger ("On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017) documented that modern networks - unlike their pre-AlexNet ancestors - tend to be badly calibrated out of the box, typically overconfident, and that the problem grows as accuracy improves through greater depth and width rather than going away. Their explanation is training dynamics: the negative log-likelihood reward is won by confident correct answers, so optimisation pushes magnitudes far past what frequencies justify. The paper also supplied the repair - temperature scaling - which is exactly where lesson 5 goes. The practical reading for you: a bent reliability diagram is not evidence someone erred; it is the expected state of the art arriving on your desk unexamined.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice P3working

Three bins from an evaluation run: [0, 0.5): n = 100, mean p-hat 0.30, observed frequency 0.20. [0.5, 0.8): n = 60, mean 0.62, observed 0.50. [0.8, 1]: n = 40, mean 0.92, observed 0.75. Compute ECE, then write the one-line calibration verdict an audit card would carry.

Solution

Gaps weighted by bin share: |0.20 - 0.30| = 0.100 at weight 100/200 gives 0.050; |0.50 - 0.62| = 0.120 at weight 60/200 gives 0.036; |0.75 - 0.92| = 0.170 at weight 40/200 gives 0.034. ECE = 0.050 + 0.036 + 0.034 = 0.120.

The verdict: every bin promises more than it delivers, and the gap widens with confidence - the pattern temperature scaling exists to soften. Note that the largest gap, 0.170, sits in the lightest bin and contributes 0.034, less than the smallest gap does from the heaviest bin. Weights matter as much as gaps when you argue from ECE alone, which is also why an ECE computed with different bins is a different number.

Where this came from

The reliability-versus-discrimination split goes back to DeGroot and Fienberg (1983) and Murphy's forecast verification work (1973); the modern statement of network miscalibration and the term "temperature scaling" come from Guo et al., arXiv:1706.04599. Every gap, count and ECE value quoted here - the bin table (5.8% against 1.4%, 74.8% against 68.7%, 85.1% against 89.6%, 94.4% against 98.0%), the pooled ECE of 0.0843, and the control's 0.0154 / 0.0169 / 0.0290 at 5, 10 and 20 bins - was recomputed mechanically from this page's seeded generators under node before publication.
