Fixing the numbers without touching the model

12 min one scalar, fitted on held-out rows, provably harmless to accuracy Temperature scaling is the most useful twenty lines in this week: it makes probabilities honest while leaving the ranking and the default decision untouched - and this page proves exactly how much it leaves alone, which is not quite what most write-ups claim.

The one-minute version

  • The fix is one scalar: divide every logit by a temperature T before the sigmoid. Nothing inside the model changes.
  • T is fitted by minimising log loss on held-out rows only. On this page's population, fitting on half the rows gives T* = 0.73.
  • On the untouched test half: ECE 0.0925 → 0.0671, Brier 0.1215 → 0.1173, log loss 0.3886 → 0.3691.
  • The invariance, stated exactly: scaling is strictly increasing, so it cannot reorder rows - AUC is bit-identical. At t = 0.5 the confusion matrix is bit-identical too (0.8280 → 0.8280, cells 337/117/491/55).
  • At any other fixed threshold the matrix does move, and the page shows it moving. What is preserved is the whole family: the same matrix reappears at t' = sigmoid(logit(t)/T). Scaling relabels the operating points; it creates and destroys none.

One knob, outside the model

Retraining to fix calibration would be expensive and unnecessary. Guo et al.'s repair needs one parameter: replace p = sigmoid(z) with p = sigmoid(z / T), where z is the model's logit and T is a positive scalar fitted once on validation data. T above 1 softens overconfident outputs toward the middle; T below 1 sharpens underconfident ones outward. The direction of the cure follows the sign of the disease you saw in lesson 4.

How is T found? By making the honest ruler happy: choose the T that minimises mean log loss on rows the model never trained on. Why that target is legitimate rather than arbitrary is exactly the question lesson 6 answers with proper scoring rules - fit first, interrogate the licence second, which is the order that makes the interrogation bite.

What scaling cannot move, proved

Everything here follows from one fact: z → sigmoid(z/T) is strictly increasing for every T > 0. A strictly increasing map cannot swap two rows, so the sorted order of the evaluation set is exactly what it was. Three consequences, in increasing order of how often they are stated wrongly.

One: ranking is untouched, so every ranking statistic is bit-identical. AUC counts concordant pairs, and no pair changed its winner. Average precision, the whole ROC curve and the whole PR curve are sets of points indexed by the sweep, and the sweep visits the same rows in the same order. On this page's held-out half the AUC prints 0.919383 before scaling and 0.919383 after.

Two: the decision at t = 0.5 is untouched. Since sigmoid(0) = 0.5 and T > 0,

sigmoid(z)   >= 0.5   <=>   z >= 0
sigmoid(z/T) >= 0.5   <=>   z/T >= 0   <=>   z >= 0

which is the same condition on the same rows. So at t = 0.5 the confusion matrix is the same table of integers - 337 / 117 / 491 / 55 here, before and after - and accuracy agrees digit for digit. This is the sense in which Guo et al. say temperature scaling does not change a model's accuracy: their decision rule is the argmax, and for a binary sigmoid the argmax is the 0.5 threshold.

Three: at any other fixed threshold the matrix does move - and this is where the usual write-up overreaches. Redo the rearrangement without assuming t = 0.5:

sigmoid(z)   >= t   <=>   z >= logit(t)
sigmoid(z/T) >= t   <=>   z >= T x logit(t)

Those are different cut points in logit space whenever T ≠ 1 and logit(t) ≠ 0, so they select different sets of rows. The page measures it: on the held-out half at t = 0.30 with T* = 0.73, the cells go from 377 / 231 / 377 / 15 to 373 / 188 / 420 / 19. Four decisions flipped. Anyone who tells you accuracy at any fixed threshold is invariant has not tried it away from the middle.

What survives is better than the false version, because it is a statement about the whole operating curve rather than one point on it. Solve T x logit(t') = logit(t) for the threshold that reproduces the old cut:

t' = sigmoid( logit(t) / T )

At t = 0.30 and T* = 0.73 that gives t' = 0.2385, and the scaled scores thresholded there return 377 / 231 / 377 / 15 - the original table, integer for integer, which the build checks live. So the set of confusion matrices the model can produce is exactly the set it could produce before; scaling is a change of coordinates on the threshold axis. Every rate, every trade-off and every fairness gap you computed at t is still available, at t'. If a recalibration ever changed your AUC, or changed a rate you re-read at the corresponding threshold, the pipeline is broken - not the theory.

Two working rules fall out. Quote the threshold with every rate, always, because after a rescaling the number 0.30 no longer names the policy it used to name. And when you report a fairness gap before and after scaling, either hold t = 0.5 or move to t' - comparing a gap at t = 0.30 before against a gap at t = 0.30 after is comparing two different policies and calling the difference an effect of calibration.

Two disciplines around the fit

Hold out the rows, always. Fitting T on the same rows you then report ECE on is leakage: the improvement looks spectacular on those rows and evaporates on fresh ones. The build enforces the split - the fit sees its half, the report reads the other half. If you audit your own Week 4 export in lesson 9, keep that wall up yourself; nobody else will.

Know what Platt scaling adds. Temperature scaling fits one degree of freedom. Platt scaling (Platt, 1999, for SVM outputs) generalises the map to a full affine transform A*z + B through the sigmoid, fitting two parameters where T fits one - worth reaching for when miscalibration varies across the range rather than acting like one uniform stretch. The origin paper concerns support vector machines; the mechanism transfers unchanged to any real-valued score, and both variants belong to the same family: post-hoc maps that leave ranking untouched.

After fitting temperature T = 0.7 on held-out data, which statement is TRUE?
At the sigmoid's own midpoint the rule is z ≥ 0 before and after, so the confusion matrix at t = 0.5 - and accuracy with it - is the same table of integers. (Away from 0.5 the matrix does move; it reappears at t' = sigmoid(logit(t)/T).) The boundary is not rotated, only the confidence attached to each side of it; and Brier falls here because the fit targets a cousin of it.

Fitting and reporting on the same rows is leakage: the gain will halve or vanish on fresh data. Accuracy at t = 0.5 cannot improve under scaling at all, so a reported accuracy gain there is a second red flag; the optimiser details are beside the point.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice P4working

A row has logit 2.0, so sigmoid(2.0) = 0.881. After dividing the logit by T = 2, what is the new probability? Then find the approximate T that maps logit 2.0 to p = 0.70 (one grid step is fine).

Solution

T = 2: z' = 2/2 = 1.0, and sigmoid(1.0) = 0.731. For the target: solve sigmoid(2/T) = 0.70, so 2/T = logit(0.70) = ln(0.7/0.3) = 0.8473, giving T = 2/0.8473 = 2.36.

Notice the direction: T above 1 softens an extreme logit toward the middle - the cure for overconfidence. This page's fitted value came out below 1 because its synthetic distortion ran the other way. The sign of the fix follows the sign of the disease; memorise the mechanism, not a magic number.

Where this came from

Temperature scaling, the held-out fitting protocol and the modern statement of post-hoc calibration all come from Guo et al., arXiv:1706.04599; Platt scaling from J. Platt, "Probabilistic Outputs for Support Vector Machines" (1999). Guo et al. state the invariance for the argmax decision, which for a binary sigmoid is the t = 0.5 threshold; this page states it that way and then measures what happens elsewhere, rather than generalising the claim to every threshold. Every value quoted (T* = 0.73 on a 50% held-out fit; ECE 0.0925 to 0.0671; Brier 0.1215 to 0.1173; log loss 0.3886 to 0.3691; AUC 0.919383 both sides; the cell counts at t = 0.50, t = 0.30 and t' = 0.2385) was recomputed mechanically from this page's own seeded generator and grid-search code under node before publication.
