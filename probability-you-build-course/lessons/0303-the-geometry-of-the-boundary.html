The geometry of the boundary

working 12 min Conditional probability formalism in Statistical Foundations 0020 · Piech, ch. Logistic Regression

The one-minute version

  • The model predicts positive exactly when the score is positive, so the boundary is the line θ·x = 0.
  • θ (without the bias) is that line’s normal vector: it points into positive territory.
  • Scaling θ up moves no line at all - it only steepens how fast probability crosses 0.5. Position and confidence are separate dials.
  • Predict-then-run begins here: two cases, your claims written first, the harness grading after.

1. The boundary is where the score runs out of sign

The decision rule is thresholding the probability at 0.5, and σ(z) crosses 0.5 exactly when z crosses 0. So the model predicts 1 precisely on the set of points whose score is positive:

B = { x : θ₀ + θ₁x₁ + θ₂x₂ = 0 } Read as: the boundary B is the set of points where the score is exactly zero - a straight line in the plane. Points on one side score negative (predicted 0), points on the other side score positive (predicted 1). The sigmoid never bends it, because the thresholding happens before the squash.

2. What each number does to the drawing

Rearranged, the boundary equation gives x₂ = −(θ₀ + θ₁x₁)/θ₂: the two weights set the line’s slope and the bias pins where it sits. The deeper reading: the weight vector (θ₁, θ₂) is the line’s normal - it points toward positive territory, perpendicular to the line itself. Score any point by projecting it onto that arrow.

Now double both weights and leave the bias alone. The line θ·x = 0 does not move: zero times anything is still zero, so the same points sit on the boundary. What changes is everything off the line - their scores double, so probabilities slam toward 0 or 1 faster. Position is a ratio; steepness is a magnitude. Keep them separate in your head and half of this week’s pathology readings become obvious: separation blows up the magnitude without needing the position to move, and the far outlier of lesson 06 twists the direction while accuracy stays put.

  1. A concrete line. θ = (θ₀, θ₁, θ₂) = (0, 1, 1): boundary is x₁ + x₂ = 0, the diagonal through the origin, positives above it.
  2. Slide the bias. θ₀ = −2: now x₁ + x₂ = 2 - the same diagonal shifted right and up. Only the bias moved the line.
  3. Scale the weights. θ = (0, 5, 5): still x₁ + x₂ = 0, identical line. But a point at (1, 1) now scores 10 instead of 2: σ goes from 0.88 to 0.99995. Same border, near-certain confidence just inside it.

Sanity check. Steps 1 and 3 draw the same line but assign different probabilities off it, while steps 1 and 2 draw parallel lines with identical confidence scaling. If a change moved both the line and the steepness, more than one dial turned.

XOR plateaus at 0.54 with weights collapsed to nearly zero. Why the plateau exists - capacity, not optimisation - is lesson 05’s subject.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A point receives a score of z = −3. Where does it sit?
Negative score means negative side, and σ(−3) ≈ 0.047 - so the probability of label 1 is about 5%, i.e. about 95% for label 0. Scores convert cleanly through the sigmoid; only their sign sets the side of the line.
Doubling θ₁ and θ₂ while keeping the bias fixed does what to the drawn boundary?
The line is the level set θ·x = 0, and scaling theta leaves that equation unchanged - same line. What doubles is every off-line point’s distance-from-zero in score terms, so probabilities cross 0.5 faster. Probabilities saturate rather than scale, which is exactly why they cannot “double”.
Your teacher. In the slider figure, try to place a line that achieves better than 80% training accuracy by hand - then compare with where training left it. Afterward, take the XOR case’s verdict to a colleague and ask them to propose a feature you could add so that a straight line suffices; the answer (an interaction like x₁·x₂) is the standard fix, and lesson 05 names it.
