Three definitions of fair, one impossible

15 min the week's peak - arithmetic, not engineering Compute three fairness criteria on your own numbers, watch a perfectly calibrated model collide with them anyway, and derive why no cleverness escapes. This is the lesson that outlives every model you will audit.

The one-minute version

  • Three criteria, three moral questions: demographic parity (equal selection), equalised odds (equal TPR and FPR), predictive parity (equal PPV).
  • On this week's population at t = 0.50 the gaps are: selection 0.134, TPR -0.026, FPR -0.012, PPV 0.171.
  • The base-rate scorer knows NOTHING but each group's prevalence - and is perfectly calibrated within every group. At one shared threshold it gives group 0 FPR = TPR = 0 and group 1 FPR = TPR = 1. Move the knob and the burden jumps wholesale.
  • The reason is arithmetic: within-group calibration forces FPR/TPR to depend on prevalence. Unequal prevalences make equal error rates and equal predictive values mutually exclusive. This is a theorem (Chouldechova; Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan), not a modelling failure.

Three questions, three dials

All three criteria are functions of the per-group confusion matrices you built in lesson 2, read at one declared threshold. Each answers a different question about who the system treats how:

demographic parity:   selection rate equal across groups
                      (are the flags handed out at the same rate?)

equalised odds:       TPR equal AND FPR equal across groups
                      (does the system err at the same rate for everyone?)
   equal opportunity = the TPR half alone          [Hardt, Price, Srebro 2016]

predictive parity:    PPV equal across groups
                      (does a flag mean the same thing about everyone?)

They are not variations on one theme. Demographic parity asks about decisions; equalised odds asks about errors conditioned on truth; predictive parity asks about meanings conditioned on warnings. An organisation can coherently bind any one of them - which is precisely why the audit card makes you name your binding criterion instead of hiding behind "we checked fairness".

The collision, constructed honestly

Select the base-rate scorer above. It assigns group 0 the constant score 0.30 and group 1 the constant score 0.55 - nothing else, no features, no signal. Because each group's constant score equals that group's true prevalence, this scorer is perfectly calibrated within each group: among group-0 rows scored "0.30", almost exactly 30% are positive, and likewise for group 1 (its pooled ECE on these rows is 0.0123). It is the best possible model about groups and the worst possible model about people.

Now place one shared threshold. At t = 0.50: nobody in group 0 is flagged (their score sits below the line) while everyone in group 1 is flagged - FPR and TPR of exactly 0 versus exactly 1. Slide to t = 0.56: suddenly BOTH groups go unflagged entirely. Between those settings there is no middle ground where the groups receive similar treatment, because the scorer offers only two possible behaviours. Equalising error rates would require thresholds that do not exist.

Deriving why it must collide

The demo is not a curiosity of a toy scorer; it is arithmetic that binds every calibrated model. Take any group g with prevalence pi_g, and suppose its scores are calibrated within the group. Condition on being flagged and apply Bayes' theorem inside the group:

PPV_g = P(y=1 | flagged, g)
      = TPR_g x pi_g / (TPR_g x pi_g + FPR_g x (1-pi_g))

Rearrange that equation and something uncomfortable falls out:

FPR_g / TPR_g = [ pi_g / (1-pi_g) ] x [ (1 - PPV_g) / PPV_g ]

Check it against your own numbers. On the real two-group model at t = 0.50: group 0 has FPR/TPR = 0.1823/0.8446 = 0.2158, and the right-hand side with pi = 0.316, PPV = 0.682 computes to 0.2158. Group 1: 0.1704/0.8190 = 0.2080, formula gives 0.2080. Four decimal places, both groups - derived, coded, simulated, the course's signature move closing the loop on the week's central theorem.

Read the identity. If two groups were to achieve BOTH equal error rates (same FPR and TPR, so the left side agrees) AND predictive parity (same PPV, so the second factor agrees), then pi_0/(1-pi_0) must equal pi_1/(1-pi_1) - meaning the prevalences are equal. With unequal prevalences, something in the chain must give: error-rate balance, predictive parity, or within-group calibration. There is no fourth option and no cleverer model around it.

Saying it exactly

This result has two independent proofs, and the conditions are the whole point - quote them precisely or do not quote them:

  • Chouldechova (2017), "Fair prediction with disparate impact" (Big Data 5(2), arXiv:1703.00056): when the groups' prevalences differ, a classifier cannot simultaneously satisfy calibration-free error-rate balance (equal FPR and FNR) and predictive parity (equal PPV), except in degenerate cases - the identity above is her Section 2 argument.
  • Kleinberg, Mullainathan, Raghavan (2017), "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores" (arXiv:1609.05807, ITCS): any risk score that is calibrated within groups AND balances either the positive or the negative class across groups must be perfectly predictive, unless the groups' base rates coincide. Non-perfect, calibrated, unequal base rates: balance is out.

Note what the theorems do NOT say. They do not say fairness is hopeless - Hardt, Price and Srebro (arXiv:1610.02413) show equalised odds is always achievable by post-processing, at a cost the framework prices openly; they simply never promised calibration alongside. And Pleiss et al. (arXiv:1709.02012) close the loop in the other direction: a fully calibrated classifier can still allocate errors unequally, so "our model is calibrated" was never a fairness certificate. Above all, the theorems do not choose your criterion. Which definition binds is a value judgement your organisation makes and your audit states - the mathematics refuses to make it for you, and pretending otherwise is the one move this week forbids.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper - the full derivation

Practice 6hard

Group g has prevalence pi_g, and its scores are calibrated within the group. Let v_g denote its PPV at the shared threshold t. Derive FPR_g/TPR_g = [pi_g/(1-pi_g)] x [(1-v_g)/v_g] from Bayes' theorem, then conclude: if two groups have unequal prevalences, a within-group-calibrated scorer cannot deliver both identical error rates and identical PPV. Present every algebraic step.

Solution

Start from the definition of PPV as a conditional probability and expand by Bayes inside group g: v_g = P(y=1 | flagged) = P(flagged | y=1)P(y=1) / P(flagged). In per-group terms P(flagged | y=1) = TPR_g, P(y=1) = pi_g, and P(flagged) = TPR_g x pi_g + FPR_g x (1-pi_g). So v_g (TPR_g pi_g + FPR_g(1-pi_g)) = TPR_g pi_g. Expand the left side: v_g TPR_g pi_g + v_g FPR_g (1-pi_g) = TPR_g pi_g. Subtract: v_g FPR_g (1-pi_g) = TPR_g pi_g (1 - v_g). Divide: FPR_g/TPR_g = [pi_g/(1-pi_g)] x [(1-v_g)/v_g].

Now suppose both groups share identical error rates (so FPR_0/TPR_0 = FPR_1/TPR_1 whenever the ratios are defined and non-degenerate) and predictive parity (v_0 = v_1 = v). The left sides agree, so the right sides must: [pi_0/(1-pi_0)] x [(1-v)/v] = [pi_1/(1-pi_1)] x [(1-v)/v], and cancelling (for v strictly between 0 and 1) leaves pi_0/(1-pi_0) = pi_1/(1-pi_1), hence pi_0 = pi_1. Contradiction with unequal prevalence. So a within-group-calibrated scorer facing unequal prevalences cannot give both equal error rates and equal PPV; one must break. This is Chouldechova's Section 2 identity; Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan prove the sibling statement for calibrated scores under class balance. Verify it live: the figure above prints 0.2158 vs 0.2158 and 0.2080 vs 0.2080.
