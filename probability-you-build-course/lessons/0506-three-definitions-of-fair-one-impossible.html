Three definitions of fair, one impossible

15 min the week's peak - arithmetic, not engineering Compute three fairness criteria on your own numbers, watch a perfectly calibrated model collide with them anyway, and derive why no cleverness escapes. This is the lesson that outlives every model you will audit.

The one-minute version

  • Three criteria, three moral questions: demographic parity (equal selection), equalised odds (equal TPR and FPR), predictive parity (equal PPV).
  • On this week's population at t = 0.50 the gaps are: selection 0.134, TPR -0.026, FPR -0.012, PPV 0.171.
  • The base-rate scorer knows NOTHING about any individual - only each group's prevalence - and is calibrated within every group by construction. At t = 0.50 it gives group 0 TPR = FPR = 0 and group 1 TPR = FPR = 1. There is no threshold between those that balances anything.
  • The reason is arithmetic. One counting identity ties FPR/TPR to prevalence and PPV for every classifier, calibrated or not; unequal prevalences then make equal error rates and equal predictive values mutually exclusive. This is a theorem (Chouldechova 2017; Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan 2017), not a modelling failure.

Three questions, three dials

All three criteria are functions of the per-group confusion matrices you built in lesson 2, read at one declared threshold. Each answers a different question about who the system treats how:

demographic parity:   selection rate equal across groups
                      (are the flags handed out at the same rate?)

equalised odds:       TPR equal AND FPR equal across groups
                      (does the system err at the same rate for everyone?)
   equal opportunity = the TPR half alone          [Hardt, Price, Srebro 2016]

predictive parity:    PPV equal across groups
                      (does a flag mean the same thing about everyone?)

They are not variations on one theme. Demographic parity asks about decisions; equalised odds asks about errors conditioned on truth; predictive parity asks about meanings conditioned on warnings. An organisation can coherently bind any one of them - which is precisely why the audit card makes you name your binding criterion instead of hiding behind "we checked fairness".

The collision, constructed honestly

Select the base-rate scorer above. It assigns every group-0 row the constant score 0.30 and every group-1 row the constant score 0.55 - nothing else, no features, no signal about any individual. Each group's constant equals the rate its rows were generated at, so the scorer is calibrated within each group by construction: among the rows it scores 0.30, the observed positive frequency is 0.3162, and among those it scores 0.55 it is 0.5470. The residual gaps are sampling error on 1404 and 596 rows, not a bias in the scorer, and they leave a pooled ECE of 0.0123 - better calibrated than the trained model's 0.0843. It is the best possible model about groups and the worst possible model about people.

Now place one shared threshold. At t = 0.50 nobody in group 0 clears the line and everybody in group 1 does: group 0 gets TPR = FPR = 0 and group 1 gets TPR = FPR = 1, the widest error-rate gap the arithmetic permits. Slide to t = 0.56 and both groups go unflagged entirely; slide to t = 0.28 and both are flagged entirely. Between those settings there is no threshold where the groups are treated alike, because a constant-per-group scorer offers each group exactly two behaviours and their switching points differ. Equalising error rates here would need a threshold that does not exist.

Sit with what that costs. The scorer is not sloppy: on the calibration audit of lesson 4 it beats the real model. It is not biased in any sense a debiasing library would recognise - it has no coefficients to debias. It carries no proxy features, no historical labels, no dirty data. Every complaint the fairness-as-data-hygiene story makes is unavailable here, and the error-rate gap is still total. That is the shape of the result: calibration and balance are competing for the same arithmetic, and the more perfectly a model tracks group base rates, the more sharply it separates the groups' error rates.

Deriving why it must collide

The demo is not a curiosity of a toy scorer. Underneath it is one identity that binds every classifier - trained or constant, calibrated or wildly miscalibrated - because it is nothing but the four cell counts rearranged. Fix a group g with prevalence pi_g and read its own confusion matrix at the shared threshold. Of its n_g rows, pi_g n_g are positive and TPR_g of those get flagged; (1-pi_g) n_g are negative and FPR_g of those get flagged. PPV is the first count over the total flagged:

PPV_g = TP_g / (TP_g + FP_g)
      = TPR_g x pi_g / ( TPR_g x pi_g + FPR_g x (1-pi_g) )

No probability model was assumed there and no calibration was used - only the definitions of the three rates. Cross-multiply, expand, cancel TPR_g x pi_g from both sides, and divide:

PPV_g x ( TPR_g pi_g + FPR_g (1-pi_g) ) = TPR_g pi_g
PPV_g x FPR_g x (1-pi_g)                = TPR_g x pi_g x (1 - PPV_g)

FPR_g / TPR_g = [ pi_g / (1-pi_g) ] x [ (1 - PPV_g) / PPV_g ]

Check it against your own numbers. On the real two-group model at t = 0.50, group 0 has prevalence 0.316239 and PPV 0.681818, so the right-hand side is (0.316239/0.683761) x (0.318182/0.681818) = 0.215833; its measured FPR/TPR is 0.182292/0.844595 = 0.215833. Group 1 has prevalence 0.546980 and PPV 0.853035, giving 0.208018 against a measured 0.170370/0.819018 = 0.208018. Six decimal places, both groups. Round the inputs to four decimals first and you reproduce 0.2158 and 0.2081 with a pocket calculator - derived, coded, simulated, the course's signature move closing on the week's central theorem.

Now read the identity as a constraint. Suppose two groups achieved predictive parity, PPV_0 = PPV_1 = v. Then the second bracket is the same number for both, so

FPR_0 / TPR_0     pi_0 / (1-pi_0)
--------------- = -----------------
FPR_1 / TPR_1     pi_1 / (1-pi_1)

If the groups also shared their error rates - equal FPR and equal TPR - the left-hand side would be 1, forcing pi_0/(1-pi_0) = pi_1/(1-pi_1), and hence pi_0 = pi_1. So the moment the prevalences differ, equal PPV and equal error rates cannot both hold. Nothing about calibration entered the argument: it is the same four counts read three ways. And because within-group calibration is a promise about what the scores mean, a calibrated model has no freedom left to buy its way out - which is where the second theorem comes in.

Saying it exactly

Two results are in play. They are different theorems with different hypotheses, and running them together is the commonest way this material is got wrong - including by people who cite both. Quote them precisely or do not quote them.

  • Chouldechova (2017), "Fair prediction with disparate impact" (Big Data 5(2):153-163, arXiv:1703.00056). Her result is about predictive parity, and it needs no calibration assumption at all. Written with FNR = 1 - TPR, the identity above is her relation
    FPR = [pi/(1-pi)] x [(1-PPV)/PPV] x (1-FNR),
    from which: if a classifier satisfies predictive parity but prevalence differs between the groups, it cannot have equal false-positive rates and equal false-negative rates across those groups. That is the whole statement. It says nothing about calibration, and it does not say fairness is impossible - it says these three specific things do not fit together.
  • Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2017), "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores" (arXiv:1609.05807, ITCS 2017). Their result is about calibrated risk scores and names three conditions: calibration within groups (among the people assigned score v in a group, a v fraction are positive); balance for the positive class (the average score of positive members is equal across groups); and balance for the negative class (likewise for negative members). Their theorem: these three can hold simultaneously only in two special cases - perfect prediction, or equal base rates across groups. Outside those two cases, a calibrated score must give up at least one balance condition.

The base-rate scorer is a KMR demonstration you can hold in one hand. It is calibrated within groups; the base rates are unequal (0.3162 against 0.5470); it is about as far from a perfect predictor as a scorer can be. KMR says at least one balance condition must fail, and this one fails both maximally: every group-1 member, positive or negative, receives 0.55 while every group-0 member receives 0.30, so the average score of the positive class differs by 0.25 across groups and so does the average score of the negative class. The theorem's two escape hatches are visible in the same picture - make the base rates equal and both constants coincide; make the prediction perfect and the constants stop being constants.

Note what the theorems do NOT say. They do not say fairness is hopeless - Hardt, Price and Srebro (arXiv:1610.02413) show equalised odds is always achievable by post-processing, at a cost the framework prices openly; they simply never promised calibration alongside. And Pleiss et al. (arXiv:1709.02012) close the loop in the other direction: a fully calibrated classifier can still allocate errors unequally, so "our model is calibrated" was never a fairness certificate. Above all, the theorems do not choose your criterion. Which definition binds is a value judgement your organisation makes and your audit states - the mathematics refuses to make it for you, and pretending otherwise is the one move this week forbids.

Selection rate, error rates and PPV are all ratios built from the same four cells, conditioning on different totals; the identity ties them to prevalence and it was derived from the counts alone. Equal flag rates with unequal PPV means the low-PPV group absorbs most false positives: more of its members experience the adverse action without the underlying condition. Every criterion hides someone's costs; the audit's job is naming whose.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper - the full derivation

Practice P6hard

Group g has prevalence pi_g. Let v_g denote its PPV at the shared threshold t. Derive FPR_g/TPR_g = [pi_g/(1-pi_g)] x [(1-v_g)/v_g] from the four confusion-matrix counts alone, then conclude: if two groups have unequal prevalences, no classifier whatsoever can deliver both identical error rates and identical PPV. Present every algebraic step, and say explicitly at which step you would have needed a calibration assumption - the answer is nowhere, and knowing that is half the problem.

Solution

Count the group's own rows. Of n_g rows, pi_g n_g are positive; TPR_g of those are flagged, giving TP_g = TPR_g pi_g n_g. The other (1-pi_g) n_g are negative; FPR_g of those are flagged, giving FP_g = FPR_g (1-pi_g) n_g. By definition v_g = TP_g/(TP_g + FP_g), and n_g cancels:

v_g ( TPR_g pi_g + FPR_g (1-pi_g) ) = TPR_g pi_g. Expand the left side: v_g TPR_g pi_g + v_g FPR_g (1-pi_g) = TPR_g pi_g. Subtract v_g TPR_g pi_g from both sides: v_g FPR_g (1-pi_g) = TPR_g pi_g (1 - v_g). Divide both sides by v_g TPR_g (1-pi_g), which is legitimate whenever some positive row is flagged and some flag is correct: FPR_g/TPR_g = [pi_g/(1-pi_g)] x [(1-v_g)/v_g].

Where calibration would have been needed: nowhere. Every step used a definition of a rate, not a claim about what the scores mean. That is why the result binds a wildly miscalibrated model exactly as tightly as a calibrated one.

Now suppose both groups share identical error rates (so FPR_0/TPR_0 = FPR_1/TPR_1) and predictive parity (v_0 = v_1 = v). The left sides agree, so the right sides must: [pi_0/(1-pi_0)] x [(1-v)/v] = [pi_1/(1-pi_1)] x [(1-v)/v]. Cancelling the second bracket - legitimate for v strictly between 0 and 1 - leaves pi_0/(1-pi_0) = pi_1/(1-pi_1). The map x -> x/(1-x) is strictly increasing on (0,1), so pi_0 = pi_1, contradicting unequal prevalence. This is Chouldechova's relation; the separate KMR theorem adds that a calibrated score facing unequal base rates must also break balance for the positive or the negative class unless it predicts perfectly. Verify the identity live: the figure above prints 0.215833 against 0.215833 and 0.208018 against 0.208018.
