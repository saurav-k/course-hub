This week you build a tool that decides where your inference money goes

foundation 10 min One quality bar, one dated price list, five spending policies - and by Lesson 9 the cheapest one that clears your bar falls out of arithmetic you derived yourself.

The one-minute version

  • Every AI feature faces the same daily question: which model, how many samples, when to escalate - under a quality bar and a budget.
  • Vendors publish prices. Nobody publishes "the probability this model answers your request correctly". This week makes that invisible quantity explicit.
  • You end the week owning the Spend Planner: a browser tool that computes each candidate policy's chance of a correct answer and its expected cost, names the cheapest policy that clears your bar, and revises itself by Bayes when validation evidence arrives.
  • Nothing is fetched live. The prices are a frozen, dated snapshot committed with these lessons, and the page tells you exactly how old they are.

What the finished planner does

Feed it three things: a quality bar (say, 95 percent of requests answered correctly), your current belief about how often the cheap model is right, and the quality of the check you can run before shipping an answer. The planner then fills a table. One row per candidate policy - a single cheap call, a single frontier call, n cheap samples kept if any lands right, n cheap samples voted on, and a cascade that tries the cheap model and escalates to the frontier one only when a check refuses to ship. Two columns per row: the probability the policy produces a correct answer, and its expected cost per request. Both come from derivations you will have written down yourself by the time the table exists, and a simulation button checks every analytic number against forty thousand simulated requests.

Then it makes the decision for you, out loud: the cheapest policy whose probability clears the bar is highlighted, with its cost. If nothing clears the bar, it prints that honestly rather than quietly recommending a loser. And because a reliability estimate is a belief rather than a fact, the planner carries a Bayes panel: record validation outcomes as they arrive, watch the posterior over hypotheses move, and the plan re-ranks itself when the evidence demands it.

The sibling course on inference owns the systems side of this decision - routing, caching, serving. Its model router lab builds the machinery such a planner would sit inside; this week derives the probabilistic brain that picks the route. When both are read, the router is the plumbing and this table is the judgement.

The frozen catalogue, and where its numbers came from

Every price this week uses lives in one clearly marked constant object committed with the lessons, and nowhere else. It was fetched on 26 August 2026 from the vendors' public price pages. Those numbers were true on that day; the mathematics of the week does not care whether they are still true, which is precisely why the date travels with them. Nothing on any page fetches anything at runtime, so a lesson opened in three years renders exactly as it does today.

cost per request = (900 x price-in + 300 x price-out) / 1,000,000 Read as: a typical request sends 900 input tokens and receives 300 output tokens; multiply each direction's token count by that tier's per-million price and divide by a million to get dollars per request. The nano column: (900 x 0.20 + 300 x 1.20) / 1e6 = 540 / 1e6 = 0.00054 dollars.

The snapshot's anchors, recorded so a future reader can re-derive the spread: the nano row matches the small tier of OpenAI's published family (OpenAI pricing page, gpt-5.6-luna at $0.20 in / $1.20 out), and the standard and frontier rows match Anthropic's published Sonnet and Opus tiers (Claude pricing page, $2/$10 and $5/$25). The load-bearing fact is not any single number but the spread: within one vendor's current family the large tier costs roughly twenty times the small one, and ratios like that survive price drift far better than absolutes do. Latency descriptions are qualitative, from the Artificial Analysis leaderboard snapshot of the same day, which recorded fastest-class time-to-first-token around 0.3 seconds and output speeds ranging from tens of tokens per second up to four figures.

Why frozen beats liveA planner fed yesterday's prices teaches the same decision algebra as one fed today's - the prices enter the expected cost as multiplicative constants, and every comparison in the week is between policies, not between days. Live data would add keys, cost, silent breakage when a model is deprecated, and nothing else. What genuinely cannot be bought is the other input: the probability that a model answers your task correctly. No vendor publishes that. You will estimate it honestly, from evidence, with Bayes.
Your teacher. Skim forward to Lesson 9 and play with the finished planner before reading anything else. Press its Simulate button, drag the quality bar, break it with an impossible target. Ten minutes there will make every derivation in Lessons 1 through 8 feel like finding out why a machine you have already touched does what it does - which is the order this course prefers.

Where this came from

The spend-decision frame follows Chen, Zaharia and Zou, FrugalGPT (2023), which cascades cheap-to-expensive models and reports large cost reductions at matched accuracy, and Snell et al., Scaling test-time compute optimally (2024), which shows the cheapest way to buy accuracy varies with the difficulty of the prompt - the reason this week's independence lesson matters. Sample-and-vote policies trace to Wang et al., Self-consistency (ICLR 2023); the papers course reads all three in depth at its chain-of-thought lesson and its test-time-scaling lesson.
