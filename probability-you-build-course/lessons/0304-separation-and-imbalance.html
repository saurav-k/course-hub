Separation and imbalance

working 13 min Two built-in cases: perfect separation and 19:1 class imbalance · Piech, ch. Logistic Regression

The one-minute version

  • On perfectly separated data the likelihood climbs forever: no finite theta is optimal, weights grow without bound.
  • The honest observable is minimum confidence over training points, not accuracy and not the weight norm.
  • At 19:1, a model that ignores the minority scores 95% accuracy - and recall on the minority of 0%.
  • L2 regularisation adds −λ·||θ||² to the objective, trading a sliver of likelihood for bounded weights.
  • Both failures are invisible to accuracy. Both are obvious in the report table.

1. Perfect separation: the fit that never finishes

The generator draws two tight clouds far apart (40 points each, spread 0.7, centres four units out). Every positive sits beyond every negative, so a whole pencil of lines separates them perfectly - and among those lines, the likelihood prefers the confident ones. Push the line’s steepness up and every point’s probability moves closer to its label; the likelihood keeps rising. There is no best line, only better ones.

What does the harness actually measure on this case? From the canonical run:

  1. Accuracy. Exactly 1.000 - trivially, since the clouds separate.
  2. Minimum confidence. The least-confident training point still sits at confidence ≥ 0.999.
  3. Growth probe. Comparing the weight norm at step 400 against step 1600 gives 1.23: the norm grew about 23% between those checkpoints - still climbing late into training.

Sanity check. Accuracy was guaranteed by construction, so it carries no information; the informative readings are the confidence floor and the continuing climb, both consistent with an unbounded objective.

Why minConf is the assertion In floating-point arithmetic the sigmoid saturates near |z| ≈ 37: probabilities round onto 1, errors round onto zero, and the gradient dies. So a naive check like “is the norm still growing?” eventually flakes - the norm freezes mid-climb. The confidence observable survives: saturated points have minConf past 0.999, which no finite converged fit on overlapping data produces. Assert on what the pathology guarantees, not on how your floats happen to fail.

2. Class imbalance: accuracy’s favourite trick

The generator now draws 190 negatives against 10 positives. Before running anything, do the degenerate arithmetic: a model that predicts negative for everyone gets

  1. Accuracy. 190 correct negatives out of 200 points: 190/200 = 95%.
  2. Recall on the minority. It finds none of the ten positives: 0%.
  3. Recall on the majority. It finds all 190: 100%.

Sanity check. The three numbers share one denominator story: 95% is dominated by the class you did not care about. Any metric averaged over points inherits the majority’s vote.

The trained model is subtler and still caught: on the canonical draw it reaches accuracy 0.97 while catching only 3 of the 10 positives - minority recall 0.30. It learned something real (the positives do cluster away from the bulk), but the loss barely punishes missing them: each missed positive contributes one small term against 190 comfortable ones. This is the trap made runnable. A fraud detector at 1.5% prevalence behaves exactly like this while its dashboard glows green.

3. The lambda slider: what L2 trades

The standard industrial response appends a penalty to the objective:

J(θ) = LL(θ) − λ(θ₁² + θ₂²) Read as: maximise the log-likelihood as before, minus lambda times the squared length of the weight vector (the bias is usually left out). Weights must now buy every extra unit of magnitude with real likelihood, which caps how far separation can inflate them. Large lambda shrinks harder.

Run the suite below, then drag lambda up and re-run. On the canonical data, moving lambda from 0 to 0.002 pulls the separation case’s norm from 1.6 down to 0.92 (confidence floor easing from 0.999 to 0.981), and at 0.01 the norm lands at 0.67 with accuracy still 1.000. On imbalance, the bias relaxes from −3.20 toward −0.82 and the confidence floor rises from 0.005 to 0.14 - at the price of two accuracy points. Nothing broke: a little likelihood was traded for weights a colleague can reason about.

More epochs make separation worse, not better: every extra thousand steps pushes weights further along an unbounded objective. If a fit “improves” with training time only because confidence inflates, nothing about the underlying disagreement between model and data has changed.

On perfectly separable data, training longer makes the weight norm...
Separated data admits infinitely many perfect separators, and the likelihood strictly prefers steeper ones, so the optimum recedes to infinity. Oscillation and shrinkage contradict the direction of the gradient; convergence presumes an optimum that is not there. Only regularisation supplies a finite target.
After adding moderate L2 regularisation to that same separated problem, the weight norm...
The penalty charges rent on weight magnitude, so the climb stops where marginal likelihood gain equals marginal penalty cost - a finite equilibrium. The penalty is subtracted, not multiplied; it pulls toward zero without reaching it while any likelihood remains; and changing the objective certainly changes the argmax.
Data has classes at 19:1. A model predicts the majority class for everyone. Its accuracy and its recall on the minority are...
It aces all 190 majority points (95%) and misses all 10 minority points (0%). The paired 95/95 answer confuses accuracy with per-class skill; 50/0 invents balance the data does not have; and 5/100 flips which class dominates the denominator.
Heavy regularisation drops training accuracy by three points. Disaster?
Regularisation deliberately buys bounded, interpretable weights with a little training fit, and held-out behaviour is where that trade pays or fails. Zeroing lambda undoes the purchase; the “only metric” claim is the imbalance lesson’s casualty; and the last option is simply false - the trade is real, this page measured it.
