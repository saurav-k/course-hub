Three dull axioms secretly buy the most useful shortcut of the week

foundation 9 min Probabilities are non-negative, sum to one, and nothing has probability zero - from that, P(at least one hit) falls out as 1 minus P(all miss).

The one-minute version

  • Three rules pin every probability down: no event scores below 0, the whole sample space scores exactly 1, and disjoint events' probabilities add.
  • On this week's two-outcome space they force P(wrong) = 1 - P(correct) before you compute anything.
  • The general version, P(not E) = 1 - P(E), is the complement rule - and it turns "at least one of my samples is right" into a subtraction instead of a sum over overlapping cases.
  • Five nano samples at p = 0.8 succeed with probability 1 - (0.2)5 = 0.99968. One line. No inclusion-exclusion chains.

The three rules everything else obeys

A probability measure is any way of attaching numbers to events that agrees to three demands. On this week's sample space S = {correct, wrong} they read:

  1. Nothing scores below zero. P(correct) ≥ 0 and P(wrong) ≥ 0. A frequency below zero never appeared in ten thousand simulated requests and never will.
  2. The whole space scores exactly one. P({correct} and {wrong} together) = 1. The request lands somewhere; certainty is the number 1.
  3. Disjoint events add. Because {correct} and {wrong} share no outcome, their probabilities must satisfy P(correct) + P(wrong) = 1 - there is nothing left over.

Two consequences come free. The empty set has probability 0 - it is the event where neither outcome occurs. And rearranging rule 3 gives the week's first derived result, before any multiplication has appeared:

P(not E) = 1 - P(E) Read as: the probability that event E fails equals one minus the probability that E holds. On the two-outcome space with E = {correct}: P(wrong) = 1 - P(correct). If the nano tier is right eighty times in a hundred, it is wrong twenty - and the axioms, not intuition, are what force the twenty.

The foundations course carries the careful version of this argument, including what the axioms force beyond finite spaces, at its axioms lesson; here we keep the two-element case and put it to work immediately.

The payoff: counting misses instead of hits

Now ask the question the Spend Planner actually needs. You send n requests instead of one, and you will accept the batch if at least one answer is correct. Directly, "at least one" is a union of overlapping events - the first might be right, or the second, or both, or all five - and adding their probabilities double-counts every overlap. Lesson 1's quiz already caught somebody selling that arithmetic at 4.

The complement rule flips the question to its easy twin. The opposite of "at least one correct" is "all n wrong". Those are disjoint questions covering everything, so:

P(at least one hit in n) = 1 - P(all n miss) Read as: subtract the single event where every sample lands wrong from certainty. No overlaps survive, because "all miss" is one specific outcome pattern, not a pile of overlapping ones. This move - price the complement because it is easier - is the whole trick.

One ingredient is still needed: the probability that all n miss. For now take it on loan from Lesson 7, which defines independence properly: when samples fail independently, the all-miss probability is the product (1-p)n. Plugging in the nano tier at p = 0.8 with n = 5 samples:

  1. Price the miss. One sample misses with probability 1 - 0.8 = 0.2.
  2. Compound the miss. All five miss with probability 0.25 = 0.00032.
  3. Subtract from certainty. 1 - 0.00032 = 0.99968.

Four decimal places of near-certainty, from one subtraction. The naive addition stopped being legal at two samples; the complement route does not even notice n growing. That asymmetry is why this page exists before anything else in the milestone path.

Milestone M1, checked off

M1 promised a world function whose frequencies behave. The checks now available: the frequency of correct answers sits within about ±0.01 of p at ten thousand trials; every frequency on screen is between 0 and 1, honouring axiom 1; the miss and hit frequencies of the last build sum to 1, honouring axiom 3; and the analytic bars match their simulated twins within simulation noise, roughly ±0.01 at twenty thousand batches. Everything observed obeys everything derived. That is the done-state of M1, and from here every new policy arrives the same way: derive first, then let the simulator vote.

The assumption you just borrowedThe (1-p)n product assumed the five samples fail independently - that one landing wrong tells you nothing about the others. On hard prompt families that assumption is exactly what breaks, and when it breaks, 0.99968 collapses toward 0.90 while the formula keeps printing 0.99968. Lesson 7 builds the machine that shows you the collapse; keep a mental bookmark on this paragraph until then.

Check yourself

Five independent calls, each correct with probability 0.8. What is P(at least one correct)?
The complement of "at least one correct" is "every one wrong", which for independent calls is (0.2)5 = 0.00032, so the answer is 0.99968. Adding 0.8 five times over-counts the overlaps between calls and sails past 1. Raising 0.8 to the fifth answers a different event - all five correct, the strictest reading - and subtracting that from one describes "at least one wrong", which at these odds is nearly certain and useless here.
A colleague doubles the sample count from 5 to 10 nano calls and expects P(any correct) to double as well. What does the complement rule say actually happens at p = 0.8?
All-miss drops from (0.2)5 = 0.00032 to (0.2)10, about a hundred-millionth, so the success probability moves less than 0.001 - the fifth sample was already carrying almost all the certainty anyone can hold. Doubling past 1 is illegal by the axioms; probabilities have a hard ceiling and the complement rule shows exactly how quickly it gets crowded. The third option confuses cost with quality: more samples never lower P(any correct).
Your teacher. The complement trick generalises further than sampling. "At least one of my hundred daily cascades escalates" becomes 1 minus P(none escalate) once Lesson 5 prices a single cascade - and budgeting for the raw expectation alone, without that headroom calculation, is how inference budgets die. Hold the pattern: invert to the event that is easier to price, subtract from one.
