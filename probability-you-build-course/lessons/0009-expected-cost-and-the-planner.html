Cheapest policy that clears the bar: the whole week becomes one sentence

working 12 min Expectation turns spend into a weighted average; the planner compares every policy at your quality bar and names a winner - or says honestly that none exists.

The one-minute version

  • Expected cost is a weighted average of what you might pay, weighted by what each outcome costs and how likely it is - never "the value I expect".
  • The decision rule is one sentence: among policies whose P(correct) clears your bar, take the smallest E[cost]. Neither half alone survives contact with reality.
  • At this week's defaults (ps = 0.80, bar = 0.95) the winner is five nano samples any-correct at $0.0027 per request - versus $0.012 for always-frontier.
  • If nothing clears the bar, the planner prints that instead of picking a loser. A tool that must not lie about quality may not lie about feasibility either.

Expectation, defined so it cannot mislead

E[X] = x1 x P(x1) + ... + xk x P(xk) Read as: the expected value of X is the average of its possible values, each weighted by its probability. It is a weighted average of outcomes across many imagined repetitions - often it equals no single achievable value at all, which is why "the value I expect" is exactly wrong as a reading.

Costs ride outcomes, so expected cost comes from total probability applied to money. The cascade's version from Lesson 5: $0.00054 certain, plus $0.012 with probability 0.21 - giving $0.00306. The sampling policies make the contrast vivid: five nano samples cost exactly 5 x $0.00054 = $0.0027 every single time, because you pay for all five whether they hit or miss. Certainty in cost, uncertainty in quality; the cascade is the mirror image. The planner's table holds both shapes side by side, and expectation is the common currency that lets them compete.

The decision rule, and why both halves bind

"Cheapest policy meeting the bar" has two clauses because each fails alone. Choose by cost alone and nano wins forever while failing every bar that matters - the planner would recommend junk, cheaply. Choose by quality alone and frontier wins forever - the planner becomes an invoice generator. The bar is the contract; expected cost is the tiebreak among policies honouring it. Cost comparisons are meaningful only between policies that clear the same bar, and holding that line is what separates a planner from a price list.

Four scenarios worth breaking your own tool with

  1. The defaults. ps = 0.80, pf = 0.97, bar = 0.95. Single nano (0.80) fails; majority-of-5 (0.94208) narrowly fails; cascade (0.9637) passes at $0.00306; frontier passes at $0.012; and five-sample any-correct passes at $0.0027 - the cheapest feasible row, more than four times under frontier's bill.
  2. Dependence attacks. Set clump c = 10%. Every sampled policy caps near 0.90 - the Lesson 7 ceiling - and the bar goes unreachable by sampling. The decision migrates to the cascade at $0.00306: dependence, not price, forced the migration, and the policy already paying for insurance is the natural refuge. Watch the analytic columns refuse to move throughout; they still believe Lesson 2's product.
  3. A bad model. Independence restored but ps dragged to 0.15. Now sampling needs real depth: the first odd n clearing 0.95 is n = 19, at $0.01026 - undercutting frontier's $0.012 by a whisker. Push n further, though, and the sampled rows cross frontier's price for headroom nobody asked for: at n = 23 sampling costs $0.01242 and frontier wins on pure cost. The crossover, not a slogan, decides.
  4. An honest dead end. Bar raised to 0.99 at ps = 0.60. Cascade manages 0.9289, frontier alone 0.97 - both fail, default sampling fails, and the decision line prints infeasibility rather than recommending a loser. Raise n to 7 and any-correct clears at 0.99836 for $0.00378. Quality was purchasable all along; the tool's job was to name the price without flinching.
Two departures from the design report, stated openlyThis page teaches scenario 2 as migration to the cascade, where the report sketched always-frontier: with sample-level clumping, a one-nano-call cascade cannot clump and stays feasible at $0.00306, beating frontier's $0.012 - the truer and better story. Scenario 3's report numbers (n = 29, $0.0157) sit past the feasibility crossover; the mechanically verified crossover is n = 19 undercutting frontier, with frontier winning beyond n = 22. Both departures reproduce from the build; neither changes the pedagogy, and both were flagged rather than silently patched.

Check yourself

Two candidate policies both clear your quality bar. The planner should pick:
The bar is the constraint; among everything satisfying it, expected cost is the objective - buying quality past the bar is spend with no contractual return. Maximising accuracy ignores that the customer specified a floor, not a maximisation; choosing by raw model prestige is the invoice-generator failure mode; and deliberately aiming under the bar breaks the very contract the bar states.
The frozen catalogue's prices carry a date. The reason that matters:
Every comparison this week is between policies sharing the same constants, so when real prices move, the rankings and the reasoning survive and only the dollar figures age - the date tells you how much salt to bring. No language feature needs dates; latency and price correlate loosely at best; and no vendor forbids anything here - the date is scholarship, not compliance.
Your teacher. Break your own planner on purpose. Find a setting where the decision line flips twice as you slide one control, and write down the two crossing points. You now know something no dashboard at work tells anyone: exactly where your spend policy is fragile, and which digit of which estimate it hinges on. That sensitivity paragraph is what separates a plan that survives review from one that merely survives until pricing changes.
