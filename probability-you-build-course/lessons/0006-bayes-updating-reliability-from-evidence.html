A reliability number nobody measured deserves to move when evidence arrives

working 10 min Bayes' rule on a discrete grid of hypotheses turns recorded validation trials into a posterior - and three wrongs in ten genuinely dent the planner's confidence.

The one-minute version

  • Every ps in this week's planner is a guess until validation measures it. A guess held fixed while evidence accumulates is not an estimate, it is an opinion.
  • Lay a grid of candidate reliabilities H = {0.60, 0.65, ..., 0.95}, start them equal (uniform prior), and score each by the likelihood of what you actually observed.
  • Posterior ∝ prior x likelihood, renormalised. That is Bayes' rule; the awkward denominator is just a normalising sum.
  • After three wrongs in ten trials, the expected ps falls from the prior mean 0.775 to about 0.72 - noticeable, but nothing like the collapse intuition shouts.

Why a grid, rather than a formula

The clean way to say "I do not know ps" is to stop pretending it is one number. Instead carry a list of candidates - here every multiple of five between 0.60 and 0.95 - with a weight on each expressing how much you believe it. Before any evidence, honesty demands the weights be equal: eight hypotheses, each carrying one-eighth of your belief, whose expected value is 0.775. This discrete-grid construction is Bayes at its most concrete: no integrals, no conjugate priors, arithmetic a spreadsheet could do - which is exactly why it belongs in a tool a learner wrote themselves.

Evidence arrives as trials: a request went out, the answer was later judged correct or wrong. Under hypothesis h, a correct trial had probability h and a wrong one 1 - h. So each outcome multiplies its hypothesis's weight by h or by 1 - h. After m trials with k hits, hypothesis h carries weight proportional to:

weight(h) ∝ hk x (1 - h)(m - k) Read as: the full expression would carry the binomial coefficient C(m,k) in front, counting which trials were the hits - but that factor is the same constant for every hypothesis, so when everything is renormalised it cancels. Only how well h predicts the observed hit-rate survives.
posterior(h) = prior(h) x likelihood / ∑h' prior(h') x likelihood(h') Read as: Bayes' rule. Each candidate keeps its prior share, scaled by how well it explains the data, then everything is divided by the total weight so the bars sum to one again. That denominator is the law of total probability from Lesson 5, wearing its other hat - here it is called the evidence.

The foundations course derives the continuous version carefully at its Bayes-rule lesson; the grid version here computes exactly the same object with eight multiplications instead of calculus, and has the virtue of being visible: every bar is one hypothesis you can argue with.

The update, worked in full

The pattern matters more than any single row: the posterior peaks at 0.70 - the exact observed hit rate - mass concentrates around it, and the extremes starve. Carrying the rows through and taking the weighted average gives E[ps | data] ≈ 0.723, down from the prior mean 0.775. Milestone M3's done-state is exactly that drop appearing in the planner, with the policy table re-ranked underneath it if the fall crosses a decision threshold.

Notice what did not happen. Intuition, fed three failures out of ten, wants to slash the estimate toward 0.7 outright - as if ten requests settled anything. Bayes moves belief exactly as far as the evidence carries it: ten trials are weak evidence against an eight-point grid, and the posterior stays spread. The width of those bars is your honest remaining uncertainty, and Lesson 9's planner will keep spending according to it.

Flipping the conditional, for real this time

Lesson 3 planted the prosecutor's fallacy and left it dangling: P(ship given correct) = 0.95, yet P(correct given ship) ≈ 0.96 - similar-looking numbers facing opposite directions. Bayes is the machine that produces the second from the first, and the direction of the conditioning is the whole content of the rule. Given that a check shipped an answer, the chance that answer was secretly right is the joint P(right and shipped) divided by the evidence P(ship): 0.76 / 0.79 ≈ 0.962. Reading the same two numbers in the swapped order - treating 0.95 as if it were the flipped quantity - would put an innocent model in prison, or a guilty one on your payroll.

Check yourself

Validation shows 3 wrong in 10 trials. Which hypothesis does Bayes punish least?
Likelihood peaks where a hypothesis matches the observed frequency - on this grid, h = 0.70 reproduces seven-in-ten exactly and gains the most posterior mass of all eight rows. Of the options offered, only 0.75 sits anywhere near that peak, so it loses the least weight; both corners survive badly, since nine successes in ten were expected under h = 0.95 and five under h = 0.60, against the seven observed. Evidence separates hypotheses sharply enough to rank them; ten trials simply cannot crush any of them alone.

Within the universe of shipped answers - mass 0.79 - the right ones occupy 0.76, so the rescaled probability is 0.76 / 0.79 ≈ 0.962. Quoting the joint answers "how often is an answer right-and-shipped", a different question about a different universe; flipping the division produces something over 1, which no probability may be, and the axioms from Lesson 2 are what make that visible instantly.
Your teacher. Feed the panel twenty trials at a true-ish 80 percent - press correct sixteen times, wrong four, shuffled. The posterior crowds onto 0.80 and the bars elsewhere starve. Then ask yourself how many validation runs your last production feature got before launch. If the honest answer is fewer than twenty, your team's reliability beliefs are priors wearing measurement costumes.

Where this came from

Bayes' theorem in its discrete form predates its name - Thomas Bayes, posthumously, 1763 - and the foundations course works the continuous statement at lesson 0023 with independence at 0024. Grid approximations of posteriors are the standard first construction in Bayesian texts such as McElreath, Statistical Rethinking, chapter 3; the prosecutor's-fallacy framing follows the literature on the error, e.g. the foundations course's own treatment above.
