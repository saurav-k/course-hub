Split the world into branches, price each branch, add - that is the whole law

working 10 min The law of total probability turns the cascade's decision tree into its two headline numbers: P(final answer correct) = 0.9637 and E[cost] = $0.00306 - then simulation checks both.

The one-minute version

  • Ship versus refuse partitions the request space: every request does exactly one, and together they cover everything.
  • The law of total probability says: for any event A, P(A) = P(ship) x P(A | ship) + P(refuse) x P(A | refuse).
  • Aiming A at "final answer correct": 0.79 x 0.9620 + 0.21 x 0.97 = 0.9637. The cascade buys near-frontier quality.
  • The same move on costs: $0.00306 per request, about four times cheaper than always-frontier at $0.012. Milestone M2 lands here.

The law, stated once and plainly

P(A) = P(B1) x P(A | B1) + ... + P(Bk) x P(A | Bk) Read as: if B1 through Bk are disjoint events that together cover everything (a partition), then the probability of any event A is the weighted blend of its probability inside each branch, weighted by how often each branch occurs. No branch is special; the law just refuses to let you average branch behaviour without weighting by branch frequency.

This is the mathematical centre of the week. Everything before it built ingredients - conditionals in Lesson 3, path products in Lesson 4 - and everything after it consumes outputs: Bayes' denominator in Lesson 6, expected cost in Lesson 9. The cascade is where the law earns its keep because the cascade's branches carry real money.

Quality first: P(final answer correct)

Aim the law at the event "the answer the customer finally sees is correct". Inside the ship branch, correctness means the nano answer was right: conditional probability 0.76 / 0.79 = 0.9620. Inside the refuse branch, correctness means the frontier call lands: 0.97 unconditionally, since the frontier knows nothing about the nano attempt it replaces:

  1. Price the ship branch. P(ship) = 0.79 from Lesson 3; P(correct | ship) = 0.76 / 0.79 = 0.9620; contribution 0.79 x 0.9620 = 0.76 - which is just the joint from Lesson 4, as it must be.
  2. Price the refuse branch. P(refuse) = 1 - 0.79 = 0.21; P(correct | refuse) = 0.97; contribution 0.21 x 0.97 = 0.2037.
  3. Add the disjoint pieces. 0.76 + 0.2037 = 0.9637.
The classic error, and its fingerprintThe tempting shortcut writes P(final correct) as "ship-and-right plus wrong-and-frontier-right" - conditioning escalation on the nano answer being wrong. That forgets refused correct answers also pay for and receive a frontier call. The build below catches it automatically: under this error the analytic bar sits visibly below the simulated one, because simulation pays the frontier on every refusal while the broken formula only pays on some. Analytic too low means a missed escalation branch. The design report records its own prototype making exactly this mistake - 0.79 analytic against 0.96 simulated - before the fix.

Money second: E[cost] over the same partition

Costs ride the same branches. Every request pays the nano call; only refusals pay the frontier on top:

E[cost] = c-nano + P(refuse) x c-frontier Read as: expected cost per request is the certain cheap-model charge, plus the escalation charge weighted by how often escalation happens. At frozen prices: 0.00054 + 0.21 x 0.012 = 0.00054 + 0.00252 = 0.00306 dollars. Expected cost is a weighted average - a realisation of Lesson 9's definition arriving early.

Set the two headline numbers side by side with the single-call policies and the trade-off names itself: the cascade reaches 0.9637 quality against frontier-alone's 0.97 - a gap of six thousandths - while costing $0.00306 against $0.012, nearly four times less. Whether that trade is worth taking depends on your bar, your check's false-alarm rate, and nothing else in the world. Problem 7 solves the exact false-alarm rate at which parity breaks; Lesson 9 makes the planner compare these rows mechanically.

Check yourself

P(final answer correct) for the cascade sums exactly two disjoint pieces. Which pair?
A correct final answer arrives either because the check shipped a genuinely right nano answer (joint 0.76), or because the request was refused - whatever the nano answer deserved - and the frontier landed (0.21 x 0.97). The first option double-counts neither branch but misses refusals of good work entirely; the second pairs a success with a failure mode; the fourth multiplies quantities that never co-vary like that. Omitting the refused-good-work slice from escalation is the classic error whose fingerprint the build exposes.
The check refuses a nano answer that was secretly right. Does the request's bill include the frontier call?
The payment mechanism reads only the check's verdict, never the hidden truth - that is exactly why Lesson 4 grouped both refused corners into one 0.21 mass before this page priced them. Believing correctness waives the cost produces the too-low analytic column the simulation exposes. The base rate and the false-alarm dial shape how often refusal happens, but given a refusal the frontier charge is unconditional.
Raising the quality bar from 95% to 99% typically does what to expected cost?
A higher bar disqualifies cheaper policies and forces more sampling or better models, so cost climbs - and at some threshold the winning row switches family outright, as Lesson 9's capstone scenarios show concretely. Quality absolutely can be bought, which is why the bar sits on the cost side of the ledger; and tight bars make plans harder, not impossible - enough samples eventually clears almost anything, at a price.
Your teacher. Re-run the build three times with seeds 42, 43, 44 and watch the simulated bars jitter around their analytic twins. The jitter is Monte Carlo noise doing you a favour: a derivation that survives being nudged by noise is standing on arithmetic, and one that drifts away from its simulator was never arithmetic at all.

Where this came from

The law of total probability gets its formal statement and proof at foundations lesson 0022; the cascade pattern follows FrugalGPT's verifier-gated chains (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, 2023), which report matching strong-model accuracy at markedly lower cost by escalating selectively.
