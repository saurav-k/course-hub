The neuron you already met

10 min week 4, wearing a new name One neuron is logistic regression with a fresh label. Train one live and watch the straight boundary appear - then see the plan for how this page grows into a glass-box network and a sampling bench.

The one-minute version

  • A neuron takes a weighted vote of its inputs, adds a bias, and squashes the total through a sigmoid. That is exactly the model you built and tested in week 4. Nothing more.
  • This week's artifact, the Glass Network, trains multi-layer versions of that same object in your browser, and lets you pause mid-training and read what every neuron currently believes about any point you hover.
  • Today's slice: a single neuron trained on two Gaussian blobs. You will write your prediction of the boundary shape before you press run.
  • The loss drawn on the right is week 4's binary cross-entropy, unchanged. When the curve crosses the dashed line at ln 2 ≈ 0.693, the model has just beaten an uninformed guess.

1. What you will own by the end of the week

Large models are invisible by default, so people either treat them as oracles or memorise architecture diagrams that connect to nothing. This week builds something small enough to be made of glass: every weight, every activation, every gradient can be watched while it works. By lesson 5 you will have coded your own backward pass and proved it correct to six decimal places. By lessons 6 and 7 you will have driven the exact softmax-with-temperature knob that every large language model API exposes. And lesson 8 will state, honestly, what a two-feature toy does and does not share with GPT-class models.

The week ships in five milestones, each independently runnable in the page:

  1. M1, lessons 1–3. One neuron draws a straight boundary on blobs; the 2–8–8–1 network bends around two moons. Run, pause, reset all work.
  2. M2, lesson 4. The hood opens: hover any point and read every layer's pre-activations and activations, plus the predicted probability.
  3. M3, lesson 5. You replace the supplied trainer with your own backward pass, and a finite-difference checker prints how nearly exactly your gradients match the truth.
  4. M4, lessons 6–7. The Sampling Bench: six draggable logits, a temperature slider, bars that always total 100%, and sampled tokens animating a real completion.
  5. M5, lesson 8. Overfitting islands you can screenshot, an attention-pattern explorer, and the mapping table from toy to transformer.

All datasets are synthetic and seeded: a linear congruential generator with seed 42 produces identical points on every machine, so your screenshots will match the prose and everyone else's. No external data is loaded, ever; the only frozen numbers in the whole week are the seeds and the illustrative attention scores named in lesson 8.

2. The neuron is week 4 renamed

Write down the week-4 classifier once more, in this week's notation. Inputs are x, weights are w, the bias is b. The pre-activation is the weighted vote plus bias,

z = w · x + b One number: how strongly the evidence votes for class 1. In week 4 you called this the logit; nothing about it changes because we now draw it as a circle.

and the activated value squashes that score into a probability,

a = g(z) = 1 / (1 + e-z) g is called the activation function. With g the sigmoid, a is p̂, spoken "p hat": the model's probability that the label is 1. Week 4's entire model is these two lines.

That is the whole object. A "neural network" lesson that starts anywhere else is hiding something: the output neuron of every network you will train this week is the week-4 classifier, computing exactly these two lines. What is genuinely new later in the week is only what sits between x and that final vote. The formal treatment of the loss below lives in cross-entropy; gradient descent mechanics live in gradient descent is a first-order model. We use both here without re-deriving them.

The loss is also unchanged: binary cross-entropy, averaged over the rows,

L(p̂, y) = -( y log p̂ + (1 - y) log(1 - p̂) ) How surprised the model's probability was by the truth. If y = 1 and p̂ = 0.999, the loss is -log(0.999) ≈ 0.001, almost none. If y = 1 and p̂ = 0.001, it is -log(0.001) ≈ 6.9, enormous. Same number you optimised in week 4.

3. Predict, then run

The trainer below is pinned to the blobs dataset: 200 points, two Gaussians centred near (-0.5, -0.5) and (+0.5, +0.5), labels 0 and 1. The map shows input space from (-2, -2) bottom-left to (2, 2) top-right. Blue regions are where the model says p(class 1) above one half; rust regions below one half; the pale band between them is the boundary itself.

Predict firstBefore touching anything: what shape will the boundary be? One neuron computes a weighted vote of x and y plus a bias, then squashes. Set of points where z = 0 is the set where w1x + w2y + b = 0. What kind of curve is that? Write your answer down - actual paper, or a note file - then run. This honour-system rule holds all week: the builds grade your predictions, not the model.

Three things worth noticing while it runs. First, the line rarely lands exactly halfway between the blob centres early in training; gradient descent on a smooth bowl moves fastest where the slope is steepest, so early epochs look dramatic and late epochs crawl. Second, the learning-rate field is live: raise it to 6 and watch the line overshoot and oscillate; drop to 0.3 and watch it crawl. Third, reset re-initialises the three weights from the same seed every time, so your first hundred steps are byte-identical to everyone else's - the screenshots in this course are not artist's impressions.

4. Why start so small

Because the jump to networks later this week is easy to mythologise, and it helps to have seen that the destination cell is made of parts you already trust. When lesson 3 stacks eight neurons into a hidden layer and the boundary visibly bends around two crescents, nothing new will have entered the mathematics except composition: many week-4 votes feeding one final week-4 vote. The failure that forces that stacking is geometric, and it arrives in the next lesson, where the trainer finally gets its dataset switch and immediately meets a dataset that no straight line can separate.

5. Check yourself

A single neuron with a sigmoid activation is exactly which week-4 object?
The mechanics are identical: z = w·x + b, then a = sigmoid(z) is a probability. Calling the circle a "neuron" changes the drawing, not the mathematics. Option four is the subtle trap - sigmoid is not decoration applied to a regression's output; the weighted vote and the squash are computed together, and the loss is cross-entropy on the resulting probability, not squared error.
The model outputs p̂ = 0.999, the true label is y = 1. Binary cross-entropy for this row is about:
With y = 1 the loss reduces to -log(p̂), so high confidence in the correct class costs almost nothing. The 6.9 answer is the right number pointed the wrong way: it is what the model pays if y were 0. Maximum confusion is -log(0.5) ≈ 0.693, the dashed ln 2 line on the loss plot. And the loss is a function of the probability, never equal to it.
Your teacher. Before lesson 2, open the trainer above, press run, and while it trains drag the learning rate from 2 up to 6 and back down to 0.5. Say out loud, in one sentence each, what high and low rates did to the line's journey. If you cannot, re-read section 3 - lesson 4 will assume you own that sentence.
