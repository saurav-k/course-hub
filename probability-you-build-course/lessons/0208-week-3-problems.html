Nine problems that make the likelihood reflex permanent

The one-minute version

  • Problems 1-3 drill the week's core act: score data under competing hypotheses and compare the scores honestly.
  • Problems 4-7 run the derive-confirm-audit loop: algebra finds a peak, you verify it is a peak, then you audit what the estimator aims at.
  • Problems 8 and 9 are code: your own grid search over the pyramid surface and your own gradient ascent on the tracker, each checked against numbers printed in these lessons.
  • Every solution hides until you ask for it; every one ends in a sanity check you can run without a computer.

The concept in one paragraph

A maximum-likelihood analysis is three moves in a fixed order. Write the probability of everything observed under a hypothesis - multiplying independent pieces, summing their logs. Rank hypotheses or optimise the score, by algebra when the peak has a formula (set the derivative to zero) and by your own ten-line climber when it does not. Then interrogate the winner: how much better was it than the runner-up, what does the estimator aim at across fresh datasets, and does the peak even live where the mathematics assumed? The problems below are those moves with the scaffolding removed.

The problems

Problem 1warm-up

Likelihood versus probability, one coin. A coin shows 7 heads in 10 tosses. Compute the likelihood L(p = 0.5) and L(p = 0.7), then derive the maximum-likelihood estimate of p from the log-likelihood. Which hypothesis explains the data better, and by what factor?

Show the worked solution
  1. Likelihoods. With C(10,7) = 120 orderings: L(0.5) = 120 × 0.510 = 0.1172. L(0.7) = 120 × 0.77 × 0.33 = 120 × 0.08235 × 0.027 = 0.2668.
  2. Derive p-hat. LL(p) = 7 ln p + 3 ln(1-p) plus the dropped constant. dLL/dp = 7/p - 3/(1-p); setting to zero gives 7(1-p) = 3p, so 10p = 7 and p-hat = 0.7.
  3. Compare. L(0.7)/L(0.5) = 0.2668/0.1172 = 2.28: the fair-coin hypothesis makes this exact record about 2.3 times less probable than the coin's observed proportion does.

Sanity check. L(0.7) > L(0.5), consistent with the peak sitting at the observed proportion - and both likelihoods are tiny, as they must be for any specific 10-toss sequence, which is exactly why nobody reads them as probabilities of hypotheses.

Problem 2warm-up

Poisson single-bin score. One angular bin expects m = 25 muons under the no-void model but observes k = 31. Using the per-bin score k·ln(m) - m (constants dropped, derived in lesson 0203), score the no-void hypothesis; then rescore with the hypothesis m = 31. Which wins and by how many log units?

Show the worked solution
  1. No void. 31 ln(25) - 25 = 31 × 3.2189 - 25 = 99.79 - 25 = 74.79.
  2. M = 31. 31 ln(31) - 31 = 31 × 3.4340 - 31 = 106.45 - 31 = 75.45.
  3. Gap. 75.45 - 74.79 = 0.67 log units, a likelihood ratio of e0.67 ≈ 1.95 in favour of m = 31.

Sanity check. The winning hypothesis predicts exactly what arrived, and no prediction can beat that at the Poisson term's own peak - the curve in lesson 0203 tops out precisely at m = k. Any other claimed winner would be arithmetic gone wrong.

Problem 3steady

Two hypotheses, one ratio. A bin observes k = 18 muons. The solid-rock model expects m0 = 24; a void model expects m1 = 19. Score both, name the winner, give the gap in log units and convert it to a factor. Would you call that decisive?

Show the worked solution
  1. Rock. LL0 = 18 ln(24) - 24 = 18 × 3.1781 - 24 = 57.21 - 24 = 33.20.
  2. Void. LL1 = 18 ln(19) - 19 = 18 × 2.9444 - 19 = 53.00 - 19 = 34.00.
  3. Verdict. The void model wins by 34.00 - 33.20 = 0.79 log units; as a factor, e0.79 ≈ 2.2.
  4. Judgement. Not decisive. Both models sit close to the observation, so the gap must be small - and sub-unit gaps are rumour-grade evidence, as lesson 0206's w-versus-x asymmetry showed.

Sanity check. Both expected counts bracket the observation (18 lies between 19 and 24), so neither hypothesis can dominate: the small gap was foreseeable before any logarithm was taken.

Problem 4steady

Derive the Poisson MLE. Counts k1, ..., kn are independent draws from Pois(lambda). Run the full recipe from lesson 0204: write LL(lambda), differentiate, solve, and confirm the stationary point is a maximum. State the result in words.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write. LL = Σi[ ki ln(lambda) - lambda - ln(ki!) ] = ( Σki ) ln lambda - n·lambda - const.
  2. Differentiate. dLL/dlambda = ( Σki )/lambda - n.
  3. Solve. Setting to zero: lambda-hat = ( Σki )/n - the sample mean.
  4. Confirm maximum. d²LL/dlambda² = -( Σki )/lambda², strictly negative for any positive counts, so the stationary point is a summit everywhere - no boundary surprises in this family.

Sanity check. If every count equals c, lambda-hat = c: the hypothesis that expected precisely what arrived, matching problem 2's single-bin logic scaled up n-fold.

Problem 5steady

Trilateration by hand. Towers stand at (0, 0) and (100, 0); distance estimates from two signal readings come out as 70 m and 80 m. Find the candidate phone positions and explain what breaks with only these two towers.

Show the worked solution
  1. Collapse the circles to one line. Subtracting squared distances kills the y-dependence: x = (d1² - d2² + D²)/(2D) with D = 100: x = (4900 - 6400 + 10000)/200 = 8500/200 = 42.5 m.
  2. Solve for y. From tower 1: y² = 70² - 42.5² = 4900 - 1806.25 = 3093.75, so y = ±55.6 m.
  3. Name the ambiguity. Two mirror-image candidates, one above and one below the towers' line: two circles meet in two points. A third tower off the line resolves the sign - which is why the tracker needs towers spread in two dimensions, and why its bad-geometry preset stretches the blob along the near-collinear direction.

Sanity check. 42.5² + 55.6² ≈ 1806 + 3094 = 4900 = 70²: both candidates sit exactly on tower 1's circle, and symmetrically on tower 2's.

Problem 6stretch

Gradient ascent by hand. One tower, one position axis. Model rssi(d) = -30 - 34 log10(d); sigma = 5 dB; observed rssi = -90 dBm; current guess d = 64 m. Take two ascent steps with learning rate eta = 100 on LL(d) = -(rssiobs - rssi(d))²/50, estimating slopes numerically with one-metre probes. Where should the steps be heading?

Show the worked solution
  1. Tabulate the score around the answer first. pred(d) = -30 - 34 log10(d); residual e = -90 - pred; LL = -e²/50:
    dpredeLL
    56-89.44-0.56-0.0063
    57-89.70-0.30-0.0018
    58-89.96-0.04-0.00003
    59-90.21+0.21-0.0009
    60-90.46+0.46-0.0042
    The score peaks where the residual dies: solving -30 - 34 log10(d) = -90 gives log10 d = 60/34, so d* = 101.7647 = 58.17 m.
  2. Slope at d = 64. LL(65): pred = -91.64, e = 1.64, LL = -0.0537. LL(63): pred = -91.18, e = 1.18, LL = -0.0277. Slope = (-0.0537 - (-0.0277))/2 = -0.0130 per metre: negative, so ascending moves d downward, toward the tower.
  3. Two steps with eta = 100. Step 1: d = 64 + 100 × (-0.0130) = 62.70 m. Recomputing the slope there gives -0.0104: step 2 lands at d = 62.70 - 1.04 = 61.66 m.
  4. Read the pattern. Steps shrink as the residual flattens - the signature of a healthy climb. The walk is homing in on 58.17 m, the unique distance whose predicted reading matches the observed one.

Sanity check. Each step moved toward 58.17 and shrank; had eta been larger the second step could overshoot past 58.17 and oscillate, which is lesson 0205's pathology rehearsed in miniature.

Problem 7stretch

Bias of the variance MLE. Samples {1, 2, 6} come from some Normal process. Compute sigma-hat² with divisor n (the maximum-likelihood choice) and with divisor n-1. Which is bigger, and why does the MLE version systematically undershoot?

Show the worked solution
  1. Centre. Mean = (1+2+6)/3 = 3.
  2. Deviations and squares. -2, -1, +3; squares 4, 1, 9; total 14.
  3. Both divisors. Divisor n: 14/3 = 4.67. Divisor n-1: 14/2 = 7.
  4. Why MLE undershoots. Deviations were measured from the fitted mean - the value chosen precisely to minimise their squares. Absorbing that degree of freedom shrinks residuals on average, so divisor n aims low, most visibly at small samples like this trio.

Sanity check. 4.67 ≤ 7 always holds, with equality only when deviations are measured from a known, unfitted centre: shrinking is structural, not luck.

Problem 8code

Grid-search the void yourself. This page loads the pyramid build's core as window.PYBMuon (open your browser console here): PYBMuon.generateObserved(seed), PYBMuon.expectedCounts(x, w) and PYBMuon.poissonLogLik(obs, x, w). Write the double loop scanning x in [-120, 120] step 5 and w in [0, 80] step 5 that returns the argmax, run it on the seeded dataset (seed 42), and report x-hat, w-hat and the achieved LL. State your tie-breaking rule.

Show the worked solution
  1. The loop.
    your grid search · paste into this page's console
    const obs = PYBMuon.generateObserved(42);
let best = { x: null, w: null, ll: -Infinity };
for (let w = 0; w <= 80; w += 5) {
  for (let x = -120; x <= 120; x += 5) {
    const ll = PYBMuon.poissonLogLik(obs, x, w);
    if (ll > best.ll) best = { x, w, ll };
  }
}
best;
  2. Tie-breaking. Strict > keeps the first maximum encountered - lowest w, then lowest x. Ties are exact only in degenerate data, but stating the rule is part of shipping an optimiser someone else can reproduce.
  3. Result on seed 42. x-hat = 40, w-hat = 25, best LL = 11,759.9 - matching the printout in lesson 0203's heatmap figure line for line, because both run the same core on the same seed.

Sanity check. x-hat equals nature's true centre exactly at this grid resolution, while w-hat landing on the true 25 is partly fortune - refit on seeds 43 onward and watch the widths scatter (lesson 0206 measured them from 10 to 35).

Problem 9code

Hand-coded ascent on the tracker. This page loads the tracker core as window.PYBTracker. Implement ascend(meas, q0, eta, steps) using the mean gradient - PYBTracker.gradLL(meas, q) already divides by the number of active measurements - starting at the map centre (180, 180) with 60 steps. Verify three behaviours on the seeded measurement set (PYBTracker.measure(PYBTracker.WORLD.DEFAULT_TOWERS, PYBTracker.WORLD.PHONE, 42)): a sane eta lands within about 30 m of truth; eta = 0.5 barely crawls; eta = 1800 diverges.

Show the worked solution
  1. Your climb.
    your ascent · paste into this page's console
    const meas = PYBTracker.measure(
  PYBTracker.WORLD.DEFAULT_TOWERS,
  PYBTracker.WORLD.PHONE, 42);
function ascend(q0, eta, steps) {
  let q = q0;
  for (let s = 0; s < steps; s++) {
    const g = PYBTracker.gradLL(meas, q);
    q = { x: q.x + eta * g.x, y: q.y + eta * g.y };
  }
  return q;
}
const truth = PYBTracker.WORLD.PHONE;
const dist = q => Math.hypot(q.x - truth.x, q.y - truth.y);
[40, 0.5, 1800].map(eta =>
  ({ eta, err: dist(ascend({ x: 180, y: 180 }, eta, 60)) }));
  2. Measured outcomes. eta = 40 ends 15.9 m from truth on seed 42 (across eight fresh seeded sets: 11.1 to 29.8 m). eta = 0.5 remains roughly 97 m out after sixty steps and needs hundreds more. eta = 1800 zigzags off the map, finishing about 310 m out - diverged.
  3. Why the mean matters. gradLL divides the summed slope by the number of measurements, so one eta transfers between four towers and two. Remove that division and every effective step size quadruples - the raw-sum version oscillated during this course's own prototyping, exactly as lesson 0205 warned.

Sanity check. Halving a good eta should roughly lengthen the climb near the regime edge - steps shrink in proportion, so steps-to-converge grows in inverse proportion. If halving eta changed nothing, you are measuring float saturation or a bug, not convergence.
