Beyond PAI: one public artifact, assembled by you

10 min the brief Six weeks built six artifacts. The capstone asks for a seventh that is yours alone: a small probabilistic system, trained, audited, and published where a stranger can run it.

The one-minute version

  • The capstone is scaffolding, not an assignment. Nobody hands you a dataset or a spec. You bring a question, the course supplies the standard.
  • A finished project must demonstrate five things: probability doing work, a trained component with honest self-knowledge, a critique of that component, a runnable artifact, and prose a non-statistician can follow.
  • Deliberately not required: novel research, scale, originality for its own sake, or a domain you do not care about.
  • A modest question answered with complete honesty outranks a grand one answered with hand-waving. The rubric enforces exactly this ranking.

1. What kind of thing this is

Weeks 1 through 6 each handed you a milestone path: a build specified in advance, broken into runnable slices, with the theory arriving when the build needed it. The Spend Planner priced a cascade of AI calls, the Distribution Garden made random variables drawable, the pyramid chamber and phone tracker of Week 3 turned maximum likelihood into two instruments you wrote yourself. In every case the artifact existed before you started, and your job was to assemble it.

The capstone inverts that. There is no build here, no canvas to fill in, no milestone list with checkboxes. What this section of the course supplies instead is the standard a finished project must meet: eight worked proposals if you want a starting point, a rubric on four axes, publishing guidance, and a blunt catalogue of how projects like this fail. The project itself - the question, the data, the code, the writing - is yours.

Why end this way? Because the course is modelled on Stanford's Probability for AI programme, whose promise is a portfolio, not a transcript. A portfolio piece has a different shape from homework: it must survive being opened by someone who does not know you, run without your help, and state plainly where it could be wrong. Those three survival conditions are the entire capstone, and every page that follows serves them.

2. The five things a finished project must demonstrate

Every item below traces back to a specific week. When you draft your project, use this list as the contract: a reader (or the rubric) should be able to point at each one in your repository.

1. Probability doing work weeks 1–3

The project's core claim is probabilistic: something is modelled as uncertain, with named distributions or explicit likelihoods - not vibes plus a neural network. A reader can point at the place in your system where probability enters, and name the object doing the work: a binomial count, a Poisson arrival process, a likelihood over a rate, a posterior updated by evidence. If the honest answer to "what is random here?" is "nothing, really", the project does not qualify yet. This is the habit the planner, the garden, and the likelihood instruments were all drilling.

2. A trained component with honest self-knowledge weeks 4–5

At least one fitted model - a logistic regression like Week 4's, a small multi-layer network like the Glass Network of week 5, or an equivalent - trained by code you can explain line by line. It reports probabilities rather than bare labels, because a label carries no information about how sure the model is, and the audit in requirement 3 eats confidence for breakfast. Borrowed training code is acceptable; unexplainable training code is not.

3. A critique of it week 6

The project audits itself. That means, concretely: confusion-matrix rates at a threshold you declare, a calibration statement, intervals on headline numbers, group-wise analysis wherever groups exist in the data, and a stated fairness position - which criterion you would bind and why, in the language of the Audit Bench. A project that cannot be wrong in public has not been done. This is the part most portfolios skip, which is exactly why it signals the most.

4. An artifact a stranger can run the demo is the argument

Browser-openable. No install step, no account, no API keys, no "email me for the dataset". The demo carries the argument: someone who reads none of your prose should still come away knowing what you built and roughly how well it works. Every hub lesson you have read models the constraints - open off disk, degrade gracefully offline, state provenance on screen, stay legible in dark mode and in print. Page 3 turns these into a checklist.

5. Prose that survives a non-statistician the README

The README explains what was built, what the uncertainty means practically, and what observation would falsify the approach - in language a manager reads without a glossary. Not dumbed down: translated. "We flag fewer innocent people at the same recall" survives a meeting; "FPR improved at constant TPR" does not. Page 3 gives the skeleton.

3. What is deliberately not required

Four things, each excluded on purpose:

  • Novel research. Reproducing an existing analysis with your own tools and your own audit is a complete capstone. Proposal 5 below re-audits a public dataset thousands of people have touched; it remains one of the strongest projects on the menu because the judgement, not the novelty, is the signal.
  • Scale. Six points and a clean question beat six million rows and a vague one. Week 4's suite proved on a canvas of eighty pixels that sample size is a design decision, not a virtue.
  • Originality for its own sake. A well-audited spam filter is worth more than a badly-audited novel idea, and employers know the difference.
  • A domain you do not care about. You will live with this project for weeks and defend it in interviews. Pick something you want to be asked about.

If your idea is modest and you can execute it completely honestly, it clears the bar. If your idea is grand and needs hand-waving anywhere the rubric looks, shrink it until it does not. Shrinking early is the cheapest move in this whole course.

4. Where to go next

Three paths through the rest of the capstone, depending on where you are:

  • "I have no idea what to build." Read the eight proposals. Seven are fully specified with data sources and probabilistic cores; the eighth is a four-question gate that qualifies any idea of your own.
  • "I have an idea." Take it straight through the same gate - it is the first checkpoint of the proposals page - then check it against the failure catalogue before you write any code.
  • "I have a project." Score it against the rubric honestly, then let the publishing guide shape the repository, the README, and the demo before anyone else sees it.

Check yourself

A friend proposes: "I'll fine-tune a large language model on my notes and show the accuracy on held-out questions." Which required element is missing first?
Accuracy on held-out questions is a single number about a black box, not a probabilistic core. Nothing is modelled as uncertain with a named distribution or explicit likelihood, so requirement 1 fails first - and requirements 2 and 3 are also shaky, since a fine-tuning job is hard to explain line by line and reports labels rather than auditable confidence. The fix is not more model; it is a question where uncertainty has a name.
Two projects cross your desk. Project A answers a narrow question with bootstrap intervals on every number and a stated falsifier. Project B answers a flashy question with one big accuracy figure and no baseline. Which does the capstone rank higher, and why?
The brief says it outright: a modest question answered with complete honesty outranks a grand one answered with hand-waving. Project B's single unaudited number is the exact disease week 6 exists to cure, and the failure catalogue names it "accuracy theatre". The rubric makes the ranking mechanical: B scores near zero on correctness and honesty regardless of how impressive the question sounds.
Your teacher. Before opening the proposals, write one sentence of the form "I want to know whether ___ behaves like ___ claims." If your sentence names something countable, repeatable, and uncertain, you already have a probabilistic core and the menu is optional reading. If it does not, the menu will teach you what one looks like.

Where this came from

The capstone's shape follows Stanford's Probability for AI programme's public framing - a portfolio certificate earned by building, not exams - with the portfolio standard made concrete here as the five requirements above. The auditing vocabulary traces to the sources week 6 already trusts: ProPublica's Machine Bias investigation and compas-analysis repository for audits of a real deployed model, Guo et al. (2017) for calibration, and fairmlbook.org for the fairness criteria and their collisions.
