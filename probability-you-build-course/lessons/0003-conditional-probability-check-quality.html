Conditional probability is how a quality check gets measured honestly

foundation 9 min A check that ships 95% of good answers and 15% of bad ones is describing two different universes - and confusing them is the single most common wrong column in a cascade table.

The one-minute version

  • The cascade's engine is a check: before shipping the cheap model's answer, something inspects it - a grader, a verifier, the unit tests.
  • The check has two error rates defined in two different universes: sensitivity a = P(ship given the answer was correct) and false-alarm rate b = P(ship given the answer was wrong).
  • Conditional probability, P(A given B), rescales the world to the universe where B happened. Both check rates are conditionals; they live in different conditions and must never be averaged bare.
  • With ps = 0.8, the overall ship rate works out to 0.79 - by weighting each conditional by how often its universe occurs. That weighting is Lesson 5's machinery, previewed here.

The check, and its two universes

A cascade tries the cheap model first, but only ships the result if a check approves it. What the check is varies with the task: an LLM grader scoring an answer against a rubric, a test suite executing generated code, a schema validator rejecting malformed output. Whatever its mechanism, from the outside it has exactly two dials, and they are defined in two different worlds:

a = P(check ships | answer correct)    b = P(check ships | answer wrong) Read as: a is the probability that the check ships, computed within the universe of requests whose answer was actually correct - engineers call this the sensitivity or true-positive rate. b is the same shipping probability computed within the universe of wrong answers - the false-alarm rate. The vertical bar means "given", and everything after the bar names the universe the number describes.

Why two numbers rather than one "accuracy"? Because a single approval percentage silently mixes the two universes in proportion to how often each occurs, and the mix changes when your base rate changes even if the check never does. A check that ships 79% of everything might be a superb check on easy traffic and a disastrous one on hard traffic. The conditional pair separates the tool's quality from your traffic's composition - which is the separation you need before you can spend money intelligently.

The definition, in one line and in full

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B) Read as: to get the probability of A within the universe where B happened, take the probability of landing in both A and B, and divide by the probability of that universe itself. The division is the rescaling: requests where B failed drop out of the denominator entirely, so what remains measures behaviour inside B only.

Rearranged, the definition also says P(A and B) = P(B) x P(A | B) - which is Lesson 4's multiplication rule arriving early. Hold onto that rearrangement; the cascade's whole price sheet comes from it.

One warning before the numbers, because it is the trap this lesson plants deliberately. P(ship given correct) and P(correct given ship) are different quantities. The first ranges over all correct answers and asks how often the check passes them. The second ranges over shipped answers and asks how many were secretly right. Confusing those two directions is the prosecutor's fallacy - the reasoning error that puts innocent people in prison - and the foundations course treats it carefully at its conditional-probability lesson. Here it suffices to see numerically that they differ: with ps = 0.8, a = 0.95, b = 0.15, the first is 0.95 while the second works out to about 0.96 - close enough to feel interchangeable, different enough to corrupt a table. Lesson 6 derives the second properly with Bayes.

The four corners, drawn to scale

Every request ends in one of four joint outcomes: right-or-wrong crossed with shipped-or-refused. Their probabilities multiply along the corner - P(right and shipped) = ps x a, and so on - and at the frozen defaults (ps = 0.8, a = 0.95, b = 0.15) they fill this grid:

The column total is doing something new: it adds the two shipped cells because they cannot co-occur - a request is either right-shipped or wrong-shipped, never both. Weighting each universe's conditional by how often that universe occurs,

P(ship) = P(correct) x a + P(wrong) x b = 0.8 x 0.95 + 0.2 x 0.15 = 0.79 Read as: the overall ship rate blends the check's two behaviours, weighted by the base rate of each universe. Eighty percent of requests behave like the first row, twenty like the second. This weighted blend is the law of total probability in embryo - Lesson 5 makes it the centrepiece.

The trade-off the two dials create

Sensitivity and false-alarm rate fight each other. A strict check - one that demands strong evidence before shipping - pushes a toward 1 only by letting b creep up alongside it, since the same harshness that refuses bad answers also refuses good ones. Loosen the check and more good work ships, but more bad work rides through too. There is no free setting; there is only a dial position chosen to make some downstream quantity - cost, quality, or their combination - come out best. Lesson 5 prices exactly that trade-off, and problem 7 of the week solves it backwards: finding the false-alarm rate at which the cascade stops being able to match a frontier model at all.

Check yourself

The check keeps 95% of correct answers (a = 0.95) and passes 15% of wrong ones (b = 0.15). With ps = 0.8, what is P(ship)?
Each universe contributes its own ship rate weighted by how often it occurs: correct answers ship at 0.95 across the 0.8 of requests, wrong ones at 0.15 across the remaining 0.2, totalling 0.76 + 0.03 = 0.79. Averaging the two rates assumes the universes occur equally often - they do not. Reading 0.95 as the overall ship rate confuses a conditional with an unconditional quantity, exactly the slide this page warned about. And swapping the rates attaches each behaviour to the wrong universe, producing a number that would be right only for a check that rewards wrongness.
You tighten the check so that a rises from 0.95 to 0.99 while b stays 0.15. Which statement is true?
Only the correct universe changed behaviour: 0.8 x 0.99 = 0.792 now ships from there, while the wrong universe still leaks 0.2 x 0.15 = 0.03. Overall P(ship) rises to 0.822. The first option has the sign right for refusals but misses that the shipped-and-correct cell grew; the second contradicts the arithmetic outright; and the fourth defers to a lesson that plays no role here - Bayes revises beliefs about ps, not the geometry of this table.
Your teacher. Name your own project's check. If you ship AI features, something already decides "good enough to show" - a lint pass, a human review queue, a confidence threshold. Write down its a and b as honest guesses, and notice how little you actually know about either. That discomfort is the correct feeling, and Lesson 6 builds the machinery that shrinks it with evidence instead of vibes.
