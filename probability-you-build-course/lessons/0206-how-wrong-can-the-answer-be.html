The estimate aims at the truth; only the data decide how tightly

14 min two builds inside A number from a fitter is a claim. This lesson teaches the two honest questions about any claim: does it even aim at the truth, and how wide is its scatter?

The one-minute version

  • Bias is aiming: an unbiased estimator hits the truth on average over fresh datasets. The MLE of variance with divisor n aims low - measurably, reproducibly.
  • Consistency is tightening: a consistent estimator's scatter shrinks as data grow. Removing tracker towers un-shrinks it, tower by tower, visibly.
  • Weak identification is neither: some parameters stay fuzzy no matter what. Thirty refits of the pyramid pin x within about 15 px every time while w swings from 10 to 35 px.
  • A point estimate without its blob is a sentence with the verb deleted.

1. Bias: aim, checked over fresh worlds

Any single fit lands somewhere specific because of that dataset's particular noise. Ask instead what happens across many parallel universes of data drawn the same way: if the average landing spot sits on the truth, the estimator is unbiased; if it systematically hangs off to one side, the offset is its bias. Bias is not sloppiness - it is a property of the procedure, and it can be measured exactly.

The classic example is small enough to do by hand. Take three measurements, {1, 2, 6}, from some Normal process, and estimate its variance the maximum-likelihood way - maximising the likelihood yields divisor n:

  1. Fit the centre first. The mean of {1, 2, 6} is 9/3 = 3.
  2. Square the deviations. (1-3)² = 4, (2-3)² = 1, (6-3)² = 9; total 14.
  3. Divide by n. sigma-hat² = 14/3 = 4.67. Dividing instead by n-1 gives 14/2 = 7.
  4. See why MLE undershoots. Deviations were measured from the fitted mean, not the true one - the fit already chose the centre that minimises these very squares. Absorbing one degree of freedom shrinks residuals on average, so divisor n aims low, most visibly at small n.

The n-1 version fixes the aim for variance estimation specifically. The general lesson transfers: an optimised score is a slightly flattering summary of the data that scored it. Every in-sample accuracy you have ever quoted felt that same tug.

3. Weak identification: the fuzz that will not shrink

Bias is fixable, scatter shrinks with data - and then there is the third fate. Some parameters are barely visible to the instrument at any sample size. Check yourself

The grid search scores w = 30 ahead of w = 25 by 1.8 log units. What is the honest conclusion?
Log units are the currency of evidence, and under two of them is small change: the likelihood barely distinguishes these widths, which is exactly the weak identification the resample experiment displays. Decisiveness would look like the x-direction, where twenty pixels of error costs eight units.
Four towers put the estimate 30 m out; after removing one, the error is 60 m. Is the method broken?
Fewer readings mean wider contours around the same target: the estimator still aims at the truth, the data just constrain it less. Convergence quality and information content are unrelated axes, and calling it luck denies the systematic widening you can reproduce tower by tower.
sigma-hat² with divisor n is called a biased estimator of variance. Biased how?
Fitting the mean first absorbs one degree of freedom, shrinking squared deviations on average - so divisor n aims below the true variance, and {1, 2, 6} showed it concretely: 4.67 against 7. The estimator still works fine for large n (it is consistent); the aim correction is what dividing by n-1 performs, for variance alone.
Your teacher. Take any metric your team quotes to two decimal places and ask the three questions in order: what aims this procedure, how wide is its cloud, and which parameter of my model is merely weakly identified? If the third question has no answer yet, you have found next quarter's measurement project.
