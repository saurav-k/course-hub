Every angular bin is a Poisson count, and the counts already disagree with solid rock

12 min build inside Trace the rays, count the arrivals, overlay two predictions, and see the excess with your own eyes before any statistics enters.

The one-minute version

  • The detector counts muon arrivals in 24 angular bins. Each bin's count is a Poisson random variable: whole-number arrivals, arriving independently, at a steady average rate set by how much stone that direction crosses.
  • This lesson's build generates one exposure of such counts (seeded, so you see the same numbers as the prose), traces every bin's ray through simulated stone, and draws the observed bars.
  • Overlaying the solid-rock prediction shows bins through the pyramid's middle falling short. Overlaying the void prediction closes the gap in exactly those bins.
  • That gap is the evidence. The rest of the week only turns "looks like a fit" into a number you can defend.

1. From geometry to an expected count

Build 1 keeps its promise that no physics beyond lesson 0200's curve is needed. One angular bin means one range of arrival directions; a muon counted in that bin travelled along some ray within it, from the sky, through however much stone lies on that ray, to the detector. So each bin has three ingredients:

  1. An open-sky rate. Muons arrive near-vertically, with the fraction pointing at angle t falling off roughly as cos²(t) - the standard sea-level approximation, cited here as folklore rather than derived fact. Steep bins start with fewer arrivals than vertical ones.
  2. A survival probability. From lesson 0200: exp(-L/LAMBDA), where L is the length of stone the ray crosses and LAMBDA is this simulation's absorption-length teaching constant.
  3. A counting model. The expected count for the bin is the open-sky rate times the survival probability. What actually arrives is a Poisson draw around that expectation - independent bin to bin, whole numbers, scatter growing like the square root of the mean.

Read the two overlays against each other carefully. Neither matches everywhere - the Poisson noise guarantees that. The question a fit will answer is not "which hypothesis matches perfectly" but "which hypothesis leaves the observations looking ordinary". With the rock overlay the central bins look systematically short; with the void overlay the shortfall moves to where the hypothesis cannot reach. Why Poisson hereCounts of independent arrivals in a fixed window are the textbook habitat of the Poisson distribution - the same reason it will model the payment-failure counts of Build 3's cousin in Week 4 and the request arrivals of Week 1. Its signature property does half the work later: one parameter (the expected count m) fixes both the centre and the spread (√m) of the observations, so a single number per bin is a complete prediction.

Check yourself

In the muon model, what does exp(-L/LAMBDA) represent?
It is the exponential survival law from lesson 0200: cross more stone, arrive less often, in the stated proportion. Deflection plays no role in this model, detector efficiency is not modelled at all, and volume never enters - only path lengths do.
Why is each angular bin's count modelled as Poisson rather than Normal?
Independent arrivals counted over a fixed window is precisely the situation the Poisson distribution describes, whole numbers and all. A Normal would be a smoothing convenience with the wrong shape at small counts and no boundary at zero. Speed and correlation have nothing to do with the choice.
Your teacher. Tick and untick the two overlays until you can say, without looking, which bins carry the signal and why. If a colleague walked past and asked "how do you know there is anything there?", point at bins 11 to 15 and answer in one sentence before any mathematics appears.

Where this came from
