To price where a cascade lands, multiply along the path it took

foundation 8 min The multiplication rule turns the cascade's decision tree into four numbers - and shows exactly where those numbers may be added back together.

The one-minute version

  • The multiplication rule reads P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B | A): a joint probability is the probability of the first step times the conditional probability of the second.
  • Drawn as a tree, each root-to-leaf path is one joint outcome, and its probability is the product of the edge labels.
  • At the frozen defaults the four corners are 0.76, 0.04, 0.03 and 0.17 - and they sum to 1 because they partition everything that can happen.
  • Adding across complete, disjoint paths is legal; adding partial overlapping pieces is not. That distinction is tomorrow's whole lesson.

The rule, and why trees make it obvious

P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B | A) Read as: the probability of reaching the world where both A and B hold equals the probability of reaching A first, multiplied by the probability of B within that world. Each factor is an edge label: the first step is priced unconditionally, every later step is priced within the universe its parent created.

This is Lesson 3's definition rearranged - nothing new enters except notation, and the notation earns its keep the moment outcomes stop being single events and start being journeys. A request through the cascade takes exactly one path: it lands correct or wrong, then the check ships or refuses. Four journeys exist. Writing each as a product:

Sanity checks the axioms demand, and which the table passes: all four joints are non-negative, and they sum to exactly 1 - 0.76 + 0.04 + 0.03 + 0.17 = 1.00 - because every request takes precisely one path. A tree whose leaves do not sum to 1 has a missing branch or a double-counted one, and the sum-to-one check catches both cheaper than any reviewer.

Where multiplying ends and adding may begin

The tree invites one more observation that tomorrow's page turns into the week's centrepiece. Look at the two refused branches: 0.04 of requests were right but refused, 0.17 were wrong and refused. Both continue into escalation - the frontier gets paid whether or not the nano answer deserved its fate. Grouping them is legitimate because they are disjoint complete paths: no request occupies both, and together they cover exactly the escalation universe. Their combined mass is 0.21, which you may recognise as 1 - P(ship).

The discipline the tree enforcesMultiplication is for steps along one path, where each step lives inside the previous step's universe. Addition is for separate complete paths, which share no request. The classic error - and the design report documents a prototype hitting it - is writing P(final correct) as "ship-and-right plus wrong-and-refused-and-frontier-right", conditioning escalation on being wrong. There is no such condition: refusal happens regardless of hidden correctness, so escalation's cost and quality attach to the merged 0.21, not to a hand-picked slice of it. Tomorrow's page makes that mistake impossible to re-commit unnoticed, because simulation votes against it.

Check yourself

At the frozen defaults (ps = 0.80, a = 0.95, b = 0.15), what is P(answer wrong AND check ships)?
The joint event walks one path - into the wrong-answer universe, probability 0.2, then inside it ships with conditional probability b = 0.15 - so the multiplication rule gives 0.03. Adding the marginals prices two different universes as if they were sequential steps; dividing computes a conditional quantity, P(ship given wrong) itself, which was already known; and 0.04 is the neighbouring corner where good work is refused, a different failure entirely.
Why does a joint probability come out as a product along its path rather than a sum?
Every edge after the first is a conditional probability, defined only inside the universe its parent edge created - compounding those fractions is what "reaching both worlds" means, which is the multiplication rule stated as a journey. Disjointness governs adding separate paths, not steps on one; shrinking is a common consequence of multiplying small numbers but not the reason; and the drawing direction is pure decoration.
Your teacher. Take any two-stage process you own - deploy passes CI then survives staging, email passes the spam filter then gets opened - and draw its four corners like today's table. If the corners sum to anything but 1, either a branch is missing or two corners overlap, and you have learned more about your own pipeline in five minutes than a dashboard usually says in a quarter.

Where this came from

The multiplication rule and its path-tree reading are standard; the foundations course derives it directly at its multiplication-rule lesson. Tree diagrams as the working notation trace to introductory texts such as Ross, A First Course in Probability, chapter 3, and remain the fastest error-check available for cascades exactly as for card games.
