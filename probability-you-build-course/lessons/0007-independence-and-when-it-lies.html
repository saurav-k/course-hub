Every sample-counting promise this week made rests on one word: independent

working 9 min Flip one toggle and failures clump: the formulas keep printing their old promises while the simulated bars sink toward a ceiling no amount of sampling can lift.

The one-minute version

  • Independence: P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B). For samples, it means one landing wrong tells you nothing about the next.
  • The complement trick and every counting formula to come silently assume it. If errors clump on hard prompts, the assumptions break.
  • With clump probability c = 0.10, five nano samples at p = 0.8 deliver 0.8997 - not the promised 0.99968. The missing ten points are pure dependence.
  • Worse: P(any correct) is now capped at 1 - c = 0.90 forever. Adding samples cannot buy back what dependence took.

The definition, and where this week leaned on it

independence: P(A and B) = P(A) x P(B) Read as: two events are independent exactly when their joint probability factors into the marginals. Knowing A happened changes nothing about your odds for B. For a run of samples it generalises to "each draw is its own private coin", which is what licensed writing P(all n miss) as the product (1-p)n back in Lesson 2.

Look back and the loan is visible everywhere. The any-correct derivation multiplied misses. The Bayes likelihood hk(1-h)(m-k) factored a trial record into per-trial terms. The majority arithmetic arriving next lesson counts sequences whose per-sequence probability is another product. None of those steps are valid unless consecutive samples genuinely fail separately. The foundations course states the formal property and its contrapositive at its independence lesson; this page's job is the part textbooks skip - showing what happens to a spending decision when the property fails quietly.

Why would it fail? Because requests are not coins in a hat. Prompts come in families: some tasks sit inside the model's competence and nearly every phrasing succeeds, others sit outside it and nearly every phrasing fails. Snell et al. (2024) document exactly this difficulty-dependence for test-time sampling: extra samples help enormously on medium problems and barely at all where the model is out of its depth. In the language of this page: on hard prompt families, samples arrive already clumped.

One number prices the damage

Model clumping with a single parameter c, the probability that a batch fails together regardless of n - the hard-prompt case. Otherwise the batch behaves independently as before. Then:

  1. Price the bad world. All five miss together with probability c = 0.10.
  2. Price the good world. With the remaining 0.90, all miss independently: 0.9 x (0.2)5 = 0.9 x 0.00032 = 0.000288.
  3. Add the disjoint cases and subtract. P(any correct) = 1 - (0.10 + 0.000288) = 0.899712, against the independent promise of 0.99968.

Ten points of success rate vanished into correlated failure - and notice the structure of what remains: P(all miss) can never fall below c itself, so P(any correct) can never rise above 1 - c. The clump is a ceiling that sample count cannot touch. A team that trusts the independent formula scales from five samples to fifty chasing 0.9997, spends ten times more, and watches the meter read 0.900 still. Dependence, not price, is what forced that migration.

The trade-off you actually own

Independence is not a mathematical courtesy you may assume; it is an empirical claim about how your prompt distribution sits relative to the model's competence, and only evidence can settle it - validation runs that record batches, so clumped failures show up as suspiciously all-wrong groups rather than averaging into the hit rate. The falsification test is concrete: if more than a few percent of your validation batches fail entirely together, the sampling policies' guarantees are void and the plan should migrate - to the cascade, which pays a frontier call precisely when the cheap answer looks bad, or to frontier-always if even that check cannot be trusted. Lesson 9's capstone walks that migration with the frozen catalogue's numbers.

Check yourself

Nine samples vote; on hard prompts all nine fail together 10% of the time. Compared with the independent formula's prediction, the true P(majority wrong) is:
Clumped failure concentrates mass on exactly the all-wrong outcome that sinks a majority, so wrongness exceeds the formula's forecast - the formula was derived in the independent world and simply does not apply here. Correlation absolutely enters the quantity being computed, even though it never enters the formula's inputs, which is the gap between a model and the world. Nothing needs proving to zero before a direction of error is known; and clumping helps nothing, it only aligns disasters.
A teammate proposes fixing clumping by taking many more samples. Why does that not work?
In the clumped branch of the model, sample count does not appear at all: the batch dies as one, so P(any correct) tops out at 1 - c however large n becomes. Sampling harder against a hard prompt family is spend without ceiling and without return - Snell et al.'s difficulty-dependence wearing work clothes. Sample count never lowers p; there is no magic hardness threshold; and quality-per-dollar degrades far worse than quadratically once the ceiling binds.
Your teacher. Pull twenty of your own recent AI-feature failures and read them as batches rather than singles. Grouped by day, task type or customer, do failures arrive alone or in company? You have just estimated c for your own system - roughly, crudely, but honestly - and most teams discover it is nowhere near zero. That number belongs next to your quality bar in every spend discussion you will ever have.
