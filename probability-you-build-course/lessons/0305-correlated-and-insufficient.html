Week 4 · Logistic regression · Lesson 06

Correlated features and missing capacity

frontier 13 min Cross-refit statistics: the suite’s subtlest instruments · Piech, ch. Logistic Regression

The one-minute version

  • When two features are nearly duplicates, only their weighted combination is pinned by data - not either coefficient alone.
  • The instrument is a cross-refit statistic: fit twenty times on fresh draws, then correlate w₁ against w₂. Canonical value here: −0.80.
  • Strongly anti-correlated coefficients mean predictions are stable while the story you tell about each feature is not.
  • XOR fails differently: the linear model’s best answer is the zero model, and gradient ascent finds it flawlessly.
  • Capacity limits are fixed by changing the features, never by training longer.

1. Duplicated features: the sum is real, the split is noise

The generator builds each point from a base value b: label y comes from thresholding b plus Gaussian noise, feature x₁ is b itself, and feature x₂ is b plus a whisper of extra noise (0.25 standard deviations). So x₂ is nearly a copy of x₁, and the score θ₁x₁ + θ₂x₂ barely changes when we write it as (θ₁+θ₂)·b. The data constrains the sum of the weights. How the credit gets split between the twins is decided by whatever noise happened to land in your sample.

One fit shows you one arbitrary split. Twenty fits on twenty fresh draws show the pattern. On the canonical draws the harness reports w₁ and w₂ correlating at −0.80 across refits - when one lands high the other lands low, their sum staying put - while accuracy stays at a healthy 0.80. Predictions rock solid; per-feature story all over the place.

Why correlation, not a range Early versions of this suite judged instability with the range of each coefficient over eight refits, and the case flipped verdict between page reloads - too few draws, too brittle a statistic. Correlation over twenty refits measures the compensation pattern directly and barely moves between runs. When you invent your own observables later, prefer statistics that describe a relationship over statistics that ride on one extreme draw.

2. Missing capacity: converging perfectly to zero

XOR’s four clouds put same-labelled pairs on opposite diagonals. Any straight line gets at most half the structure right, and by symmetry every line does equally (mis)well. Now recall lesson 03: the update is (y − σ(z)) · x, summed over points. Symmetric wrongness cancels in that sum - errors pull the weights in opposing directions - and the fit settles where the gradient vanishes: weights of essentially zero.

The canonical run reports accuracy 0.54, chance level, with weights collapsed to (−0.02, −0.05). Read that carefully: the optimiser worked perfectly. It found the exact optimum of its objective - the zero model, the honest best a line can do here. Lesson 04’s warning was convergence despite pathology; this is convergence to pathology:

Blaming the optimiser wastes hours that belong to the features. When accuracy plateaus at chance and the weights collapse together, no learning rate, epoch count or lambda will help: the boundary family itself is wrong, and the fix is a new feature - for XOR, an interaction term like x₁·x₂ turns the problem back into something a straight line can carve.

3. Run the pair

Write your two predictions first - honour system as always. For correlated features, claim what happens to predictions and to individual coefficients separately. For XOR, commit to a number: what accuracy will the fit plateau at?

What to stop claiming once you have seen this: “feature A matters twice as much as feature B because its weight is bigger” - unsupported whenever features correlate, which in real data is most of the time. What to start claiming: the combination the data actually identifies, stated with the refit spread beside it. Lesson 07 turns that into the full honest-reading protocol.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two features are nearly identical. Across twenty refits on fresh draws, what behaviour do the fitted coefficients and the predictions show?
Only the combination the data can see is pinned; the split between the twins rides on sampling noise, so the two weights move opposite ways around a fixed sum. Nothing forces them equal, and predictions inherit the stability of the sum rather than the swing of the parts.
A colleague reports coefCorr(w₁, w₂) = −0.85 across twenty refits, with accuracy steady at 0.81. What is the correct reading?
Anti-correlation is the signature of compensated redundancy: when one lands high the other lands low, keeping the usable sum fixed - which is exactly why accuracy can stay strong. Causation was never measured, numerical failure produces NaN rather than a clean −0.85, and accuracy survives because it depends only on the stable combination.
Your teacher. Add a third column to your own mental report: run the correlated case with the lambda slider at 0.01 and watch what the penalty does to the swing. Then take the deeper question to paper: if x₂ were x₁ plus genuinely informative independent signal instead of noise, should coefCorr move toward zero? Reason it out before testing anything.
