How projects fail

9 min read this one twice Seven ways capstones die, each common enough to have a name. This page is deliberately blunt and will not soften itself with encouragement. Every entry here is a project that looked finished from inside.

The one-minute version

  • None of these failures is a coding error. All seven are honesty errors wearing technical costumes.
  • Each has a cheap early test - a question you can ask in the first week of work that kills the failure before it grows.
  • The catalogue applies to your project at ship time with full force. Read yours against it the day before you publish, not the day you choose a topic.

1. Accuracy theatre

One big number takes the stage: 94%, no interval, no baseline, no calibration statement. This is the exact disease week 6 exists to cure, and relapsing in the capstone fails the course's central promise. A single accuracy figure cannot be reasoned about - you cannot tell whether it beats "always predict the majority class", whether it holds on the rare class, or whether the model believes its own outputs.

The early test: can your headline number be compared against a dumb baseline on the same page, next to an interval? If assembling that comparison feels like work, the comparison has been avoided, not performed.

2. Model worship

A neural network appears where three lines of counting would do, because neural sounded serious on a CV. The probabilistic core vanishes - nothing is modelled as uncertain, no distribution is named - and rubric axis one dies along with the reader's trust. Week 3 proved the opposite reflex pays: two likelihood instruments found a hidden chamber in a pyramid, and neither contained a trainable parameter.

The early test: finish this sentence before writing any code: "the simplest method that could plausibly answer my question is ___, and I am choosing something more complex because ___." Because-I-want-the-practice is honest and sometimes sufficient; because-it-sounds-serious never is.

3. Data laundering

Scraped or redistributed data with unknown licence pasted into the repository, provenance unstated. Two failures in one move: legal exposure you personally carry, and a provenance hole in the audit - if nobody knows where the rows came from, nobody can say what population they describe, which makes every downstream number decorative.

The early test: for every file in data/, name in one sentence where it came from, under what terms you may use and redistribute it, and when you fetched it. Any file that resists the sentence does not ship.

4. The unauditable demo

It works only on the author's machine. It needs keys, or a server, or a specific browser version - or it hides its seed, so no number in it can ever be reproduced, including by future-you. Nobody, including the reviewer who wanted to believe you, can check anything.

The early test: hand the link to one person outside the course and watch silently. Everything they hesitate on is broken. The publishing guide's rules - opens from disk, no keys, seeded randomness, provenance on screen - exist because this failure mode is universal otherwise.

5. Fairness cosplay

Group metrics computed once, unthresholded, undiscussed, to check a box. A table of per-group rates sits in an appendix with no thresholds declared, no base rates stated, and no position taken - worse than omitting the analysis entirely, because it claims the virtue without doing the work. Week 6 taught the collision: with different base rates, equalised odds and calibration cannot coexist (Kleinberg et al. 2016; Chouldechova 2017). An appendix table that ignores the collision announces it was never read.

The early test: your group analysis names the threshold, states the base rates, and ends with the sentence "therefore we bind criterion X." No sentence, no section - delete it rather than cosplay.

6. Scope collapse into a tutorial

Re-implementing a week's lesson verbatim with new variable names. The spam filter that is the week-4 suite with different data; the garden with different distributions. Nothing illegal about it, and it still fails: the capstone must answer a question the course did not ask, or it demonstrates only that you can follow instructions - which the six weekly builds already demonstrated.

The early test: write down the question your project answers that no lesson page asked. One sentence. If the sentence describes a page that already exists, you have a tutorial, not a project - take it through the four-question gate again and grow it or change it.

7. Silence about failure

No falsifier anywhere. No limits paragraph, no "this breaks when…", no claim about where the approach stops working. An evaluation that cannot go against you is not an evaluation; it is marketing copy with charts. Reviewers read the absence of a limits section as the loudest signal in the whole document - louder than any number you print.

The early test: search your draft for the sentence beginning "the conclusion changes if". Its absence means you are not done, whatever the page count says.

What to do with this page

Twice. Once now, while the project is still soft clay - most of these failures cost hours to avoid at design time and weeks to excavate later. And once the night before you publish, scoring your finished work against all seven with the same honesty you want from your own bootstrap intervals. A project that survives this page and clears the rubric bar is genuinely done, and rare enough to be remembered.

Check yourself

A project ships a confusion-matrix appendix breaking results down by demographic group, with no thresholds declared and no discussion. Diagnose it.
The requirement is a critique: thresholds declared, base rates stated, a criterion bound and defended. Undiscussed tables actively damage credibility because they prove the collision result was never engaged with. Deleting the appendix and writing three sentences about one threshold would score higher.
Which sentence, appearing honestly in a README, indicates the project is NOT failing by silence-about-failure?
Only option 4 names a concrete condition, quantifies it, and ties it to a specific claim the reader should therefore not act on - and the fix it implies, a wetter season, is checkable. Option 1 is the disease itself; options 2 and 3 gesture at humility without committing to a single checkable fact - gestures are how marketing dresses.
Your teacher. Pick the failure you find personally likeliest - most people know theirs immediately - and write its early test into your calendar as a recurring weekly question for the life of the project. Failure modes are cheapest to catch the week they are born.

Where this came from
