The glow layer: density is brightness, and rejection is the price of shape

12 min working Uniform proposals, one comparison each, and a nebula appears whose brightness you derived before you saw it.

The one-minute version

  • A continuous density f gives areas as probabilities; its height at a point is not a probability - any single exact point has probability zero.
  • Rejection sampling manufactures that shape from uniforms: propose uniformly, keep the proposal when u < f(proposal).
  • The acceptance rate is not noise to tolerate. It equals the mean of f over the proposal region, which you can derive in four lines and verify on screen.
  • Raising the tightness k makes the glow smaller AND the sampler waste more - two views of one parameter.

1. Heights are not probabilities; areas are

The starfield's dots were discrete events at exact positions, but the glow's underlying variable R - a radius drawn from a shaped cloud - can land anywhere in an interval. For such a continuous random variable the density function f plays the role the PMF played on the last page, with one discipline-changing difference:

P(a ≤ R ≤ b) = the area under f between a and b,   and P(R = x) = 0 for any single x Read as: probability lives in intervals, measured as area under the density. A single exact point has width zero, so it holds zero probability - even though the density there may be large.

Say the uncomfortable part aloud: a point can carry enormous density and exactly no probability. Density is a per-unit-length concentration, like population per square kilometre - a census tract can be densely populated without containing any particular person. That reading is what the build's brightness encodes: brighter means denser, never "likelier at that pixel".

2. Rejection sampling: shape from uniforms

How do you make points obey exp(-k r) when your generator only produces flat uniforms? You do not transform anything yet - you interview candidates. Propose a point uniformly across the region, compute the desired density there, and keep the proposal with probability equal to that density. Where f is high, most proposals survive; where f is low, nearly all die. The survivors pile up exactly where the density says.

Note what the counters are counting. Every proposal is a Bernoulli(p) indicator with p equal to the overall acceptance chance, and the kept total among m attempts is Binomial(m, p). The acceptance percentage flashing in the readout is therefore an estimate of a mathematical object: p-hat, the sample mean of the indicators.

3. What the acceptance rate measures

Here is the derive step of the week's signature triple. Because u is uniform on [0,1] and independent of the proposal, P(u < f) = f exactly, provided f never exceeds 1 - which holds for our f(r) = exp(-k r), whose maximum at r = 0 is 1. So the overall acceptance probability is the average of f over the proposal region. With the unit disc proposed inside a 2-by-2 square:

  1. Write the average as an integral. Rate = (integral of f over the disc) divided by (area of the square, which is 4).
  2. Go polar. The disc integral becomes 2π times the radial integral: ∫01 e-k r r dr.
  3. Integrate by parts once. ∫01 r e-k r dr = (1 - (1+k)e-k) / k². Check it by differentiating the right-hand side back, or trust the boundary test below.
  4. Assemble. Rate = (π/2) · (1 - (1+k)e-k) / k².

Two free sanity checks come with the formula. At k = 0, f is identically 1, every in-disc proposal survives, and the rate must equal the disc's share of the square: π/4 ≈ 0.785 - and the formula delivers exactly that. At k = 3 it gives (π/2)(1 - 4e-3)/9 ≈ 0.1398. The build will show its simulated acceptance beside these numbers; agreement within about one percent at thousands of keeps is the simulate step closing the loop.

4. The cost of shape

This is why the technique is loved for prototypes and distrusted for production samplers. Its virtues are real: three lines of code, correct for any density bounded above by 1, no calculus needed at implementation time. Its vice is the curve above: in high dimensions or under tight parameters, acceptance collapses toward zero and the sampler spends nearly all its effort producing nothing.
