The axioms at work

TermIn one sentencePage
Union and intersectionA ∪ B is the event that at least one of A and B happens, read "A or B"; A ∩ B is the event that both happen, read "A and B".L3 Part 1
Empty eventThe set containing no outcomes, written ∅; it is disjoint from itself, which forces P(∅) = 0.L3 Part 1
Complement ruleThe probability that A does not happen is one minus the probability that A happens, because A and its complement split the world disjointly.L3 Part 1
ContainmentIf every outcome of A is an outcome of B, then P(A) ≤ P(B); a bigger event is never less likely.L3 Part 1
Addition ruleP(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B): add the two events, then subtract the overlap once, because adding counted it twice.L3 Part 1
Inclusion-exclusionThe alternating-sum formula for a union of many events: singles, minus pairs, plus triples, until every region of the Venn diagram is counted exactly once.L3 Part 2
Union boundP(∪ Ai) ≤ ∑ P(Ai): a one-line ceiling on a union, paid for by ignoring the overlaps.L3 Part 3
Bonferroni inequalitiesBrackets around a union's probability made by truncating the inclusion-exclusion sum; odd depths give ceilings, even depths give floors.L3 Part 3

Conditioning and belief

TermIn one sentencePage
Conditional probabilityP(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B): the probability of A once B is known, measured inside the shrunken world that B leaves behind.L3 Part 4
Multiplication ruleP(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A): the conditional definition read backwards, which on a tree is the product along a path.L3 Part 5
Chain ruleP(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B | A) × P(C | A ∩ B), each factor conditioned on everything before it.L3 Part 5
PartitionEvents that never overlap and together cover the sample space, so every outcome lands in exactly one slice.L3 Part 6
Law of total probabilityP(A) = ∑ P(Bi) P(A | Bi) over a partition: price the event in each slice and add; the tree read as a sum.L3 Part 6
PriorP(Bi): belief about which hypothesis is true before any data arrives.L3 Part 7
LikelihoodP(A | Bi): how well hypothesis Bi explains the data A that was observed.L3 Part 7
EvidenceP(A), the total probability of the data across all slices, which is Bayes rule's normalising denominator.L3 Part 7
PosteriorP(Bi | A): belief after the data, the prior reweighted by how well each hypothesis explains what happened.L3 Part 7
Bayes rulePosterior = prior × likelihood, divided by evidence: the total-probability figure read backwards, from the data to the hypothesis.L3 Part 7
Disjoint eventsEvents that cannot both happen because they share no outcomes, so P(A ∩ B) = 0; with positive probabilities that makes them maximally dependent, the opposite of independent.L3 Part 8
Independent eventsP(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B): a numerical fact no picture shows, equivalently P(A | B) = P(A), so learning B changes nothing about A.L3 Part 8
Pairwise independenceEvery pair passes the product test, which still does not force the triple intersection to factor.L3 Part 8
Mutual independenceEvery intersection factors, including the triple; what an unqualified "independent events" means.L3 Part 8

Summarising data

TermIn one sentencePage
Arithmetic meanThe total divided by the count; it uses the size of every value, so one extreme value moves it a long way.Part 2
MedianThe middle value once the data is sorted; it uses only the ordering, so a value's size never reaches it.Part 2
OutlierAn observation far away from the rest, which is real data worth keeping and often the wrong basis for a typical-case summary.Part 2
RobustA summary is robust when moving a small fraction of the data, however far, barely moves the answer.Part 3
CentringSubtracting a column's own average from every entry, so the result says how far above or below the crowd each entry sits.Part 7
BaselineThe single number a system adopts as "normal" for one subject, from which everything else is measured as a departure.Part 4

Randomness and models

TermIn one sentencePage
Random variableA quantity whose value is decided by a random process, so it has a distribution rather than a single fixed value.Part 5
ExpectationThe long-run average of a random variable, computed as a weighted average of its outcomes with the probabilities as weights.Part 3
VarianceThe average squared distance of a random variable from its own expectation, which is how spread out it is.Part 5
BernoulliThe simplest random variable there is: a single yes-or-no event, worth 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise.Part 5
Binomial distributionHow many successes occur in a fixed number of independent yes-or-no trials that all share the same probability.Part 3
Binomial coefficientC(n, k), read "n choose k": how many different subsets of size k a set of size n contains.Part 3
IndependenceTwo events are independent when knowing one happened tells you nothing at all about whether the other did.L3 Part 8
Probability densityA curve over a continuous quantity whose areas, never its heights, are the probabilities.Part 4
Exponential distributionA one-parameter model for a positive quantity with a long right tail, with density λe−λx.Part 4
Rate parameter λThe single dial that fixes an exponential's whole shape; larger λ means the curve falls away faster and big values are rarer.Part 4
Tail probabilityThe chance a quantity lands beyond some level, which is the area under the density to the right of that level.Part 4
Normal distributionThe bell-shaped curve, fixed by a centre and a spread, that averages of many independent pieces tend towards.Part 5
Parameter estimationUsing observed data to set the dial or dials of an assumed model, as when the median fixes λ.Part 4

Estimation and uncertainty

TermIn one sentencePage
EstimatorA recipe for turning data into a guess at something unknown, which is itself random because the data was.Part 5
The hat notationp̂ is a value computed from data, while bare p is the true value in the world that we never observe directly.Part 5
UnbiasedAn estimator is unbiased when it is right on average across repeated samples, even though it is wrong almost every time.Part 5
Sampling distributionThe distribution of answers an estimator would produce if the same experiment were re-run many times.Part 5
Standard errorThe spread of an estimator's sampling distribution: how far a typical repeat of the experiment would land from this one.Part 5
StandardisingSubtracting the centre and dividing by the spread, so any quantity is measured in "how many spreads from the middle".Part 5
Central Limit TheoremFor large samples, the average of many independent pieces is nearly bell-shaped whatever shape the individual pieces had.Part 5
Confidence intervalA range built around an estimate, wide enough that a stated fraction of repeat experiments would contain the true value.Part 5
The overlap checkComparing two intervals by eye: no overlap does establish a difference, but overlap does not establish its absence.Part 5
Sample size nHow many observations an estimate rests on; standard error falls with its square root, so half the error costs four times the data.Part 6
A/B testAn experiment that shows two variants to two randomly assigned groups and compares an outcome rate between them.Part 5

Relationships between variables

TermIn one sentencePage
Correlation coefficientPearson's r, a number between −1 and +1 measuring how closely two columns move together in a straight line.Part 7
Leading indicatorAn observable behaviour that moves ahead of an outcome you care about, and so is worth watching as an early warning.Part 7
Correlation is not causeTwo things moving together says nothing about which drives which, or whether a third thing drives both.Part 7
