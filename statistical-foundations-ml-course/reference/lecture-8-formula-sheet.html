  • Everything here is one machine: a weighted sum of values against probabilities. Change what sits in the value slot and you change which quantity you get.
  • The source notes state no numbers. Every closed form below is the lecture's; every decimal anywhere in this lecture is this course's own arithmetic.
  • Three of these results are exercises in the source and are worked in the pages under this course's name: the geometric mean, the Poisson mean, and the Gaussian variance.

The two definitions

E[X] = Σk ∈ EX k · pX(k)   (discrete)    E[X] = ∫−∞∞ x fX(x) dx   (continuous) X is the random variable. EX is its support, the set of values it can actually take. pX(k) is the probability that X equals k. fX(x) is the density, which is not a probability; only its area is. E[X] is the expectation, a weighted average of the values X takes, weighted by their own probabilities.
Where the definition comes from. Run the experiment N times and let Nk count the times X came out k. The observed average is the sum of k times Nk/N. As N grows, Nk/N settles at pX(k), and the observed average becomes the sum above. The frequency argument is the motivation; the sum is the definition.

Moments and variance

k-th moment = E[Xk]    Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])2]    Var(X) = E[X2] − E2(X) k is a positive whole number. The k-th moment is the expectation of X raised to that power, so the first moment is the mean. Var(X) is the expected squared distance from the mean, which is why it is never negative. E2(X) means the mean squared, not the mean of the square, and the whole content of the third identity is that those two differ by exactly the variance. The square root of the variance is the standard deviation, which is back in the units of X.

Computing without deriving a new distribution

E[g(X)] = Σx g(x) pX(x)    E[g(X, Y)] = Σx Σy g(x, y) pXY(x, y) g is any function of the random variable, such as squaring it or taking an indicator of an event. pXY is the joint probability mass function, the chance that X and Y take a given pair of values together. The rule says the average of g(X) is computed against the distribution X already has: the distribution of g(X) is never needed. The continuous versions replace each sum with an integral against the density.
E[aX] = aE[X]    E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] a is any constant. The second identity is linearity, and it holds whether or not X and Y are independent, whether or not they are correlated, and whatever their joint distribution is. It is the single most used result in the lecture.
S = Σi Xi with Xi = 1 when the i-th thing happens and 0 otherwise, so E[S] = Σi P(the i-th thing happens) Xi is an indicator: a random variable that is 1 exactly when its event happens. Its expectation is the probability of that event, since the value 0 contributes nothing to the weighted sum. S counts how many of the events happened. The identity turns a counting question into a sum of probabilities, and it needs no independence at all.
The worked instance. Drop m balls into n bins, each ball into a bin chosen at random. Bin i is empty when every ball missed it, which has probability ((n − 1)/n)m. Summing over the n bins, the expected number of empty bins is n((n − 1)/n)m. The n indicators are not independent, and the answer does not care.

The direction that failsIndependent implies uncorrelated. Uncorrelated does not imply independent. The counterexample in Part 8 takes X uniform on the four values −2, −1, 1, 2 and Y uniform on −1 and 1, independent of each other, and sets Z = XY. Then Cov(Z, X) = 0 while X and Z are plainly dependent. The converse holds in exactly two cases: jointly Gaussian variables, which the source defers, and binary variables, which Part 9 proves cell by cell.

What this sheet does not carry

Two things are named in the source and not developed there, so they are not on this sheet: the measure-theoretic definition of expectation, which the notes raise as a margin question and leave open, and the variance of a sum, which needs the covariance term this lecture only just defined. Both belong to later sessions.
