Reading the parameters. a and b are the ends of the uniform's interval. λ is the exponential's rate: larger λ means a steeper decay and smaller values. μ is where the Gaussian is centred and σ is its effective width, so a small σ gives a narrow peak and a large one a broad spread. This lecture goes no further than that. It says, and then defers, that μ is the expected value, σ2 the variance, and the exponential's mean 1/λ.

The definition, and the four things that follow from it

Everything in the second half of the lecture is a consequence of one clause.

X is continuous if there is a function fX : R → [0, ∞) with FX(x) = P(X ≤ x) = ∫−∞x fX(z) dz for every real x
X is the random variable. fX is its probability density function, which is non-negative everywhere but is not itself a probability. FX is its cumulative distribution function, the probability that X lands at or below the point you evaluate it at. z is the variable of integration and carries no meaning of its own.

The one that is easiest to misrememberA density is not a probability and is not capped at 1. Only its integral over an interval is a probability. The uniform on [0, 0.5] has density 2 everywhere on that interval, which is this course's own example rather than the lecture's, and its area is still exactly 1.

The tail, and memorylessness

P(X > x) = 1 − FX(x) = e−λx for an exponential, x ≥ 0
P(X > x) is the probability of exceeding x, which is what a risk question almost always asks for. FX(x) is the CDF at x, so the tail is one minus it. λ is the rate parameter and e is the base of natural logarithms.
P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s) for every s, t ≥ 0
s is the extra amount you are asking about and t is how much has already elapsed. The vertical bar reads "given that". The identity says the elapsed t makes no difference at all, which is why the exponential is called memoryless.
  1. The intersection collapses, because X > s + t already implies X > t: P(X > s+t and X > t) = P(X > s+t).
  2. Apply the definition of conditional probability: P(X > s+t) / P(X > t).
  3. Substitute the tail formula twice: e−λ(s+t) / e−λt.
  4. The exponents subtract: e−λs, which is P(X > s).
Proved in one direction onlyThis proves the exponential is memoryless. It does not prove the exponential is the only continuous distribution that is, which Lecture 6 asserted and left as an exercise. That claim is still unproved anywhere in this course.

The two worked examples, in one place

The polling model: n people sampled independently, each supporting candidate A with probability p = 0.6, and the poll predicts whichever candidate has the majority of the sample.

P(the poll calls it wrong) = P(X ≤ ⌊n/2⌋) = FX(⌊n/2⌋) = ∑k=0⌊n/2⌋ C(n, k) pk(1 − p)n−k
X is how many of the n sampled people support A. ⌊n/2⌋ is n halved and rounded down, which is the largest count that still leaves A in the minority. The sum runs over every way the poll could come out that badly or worse.

Your teacher. If you print one page from this lecture, print the three-shapes table at the top. Every modelling question in this session starts by locating a quantity in those three rows, and the column doing the most work is the support: an interval, a non-negative half-line, or the whole real number line. That single reading is what ruled the exponential out for profit and loss in the session's closing example, before anybody looked at the shape of a curve.
