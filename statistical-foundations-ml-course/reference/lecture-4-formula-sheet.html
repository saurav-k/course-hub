The four boxes: sampling n items from a basket of m

Two yes-or-no questions select the box. Does swapping two draws give a different outcome, and can the same item come out twice?

With replacementWithout replacement
Ordered
(Blue, Red) ≠ (Red, Blue)		 mn
case 1. Every draw sees the whole basket. No condition on n.		 m! / (m−n)!
case 2. The basket shrinks by one each draw. Needs m ≥ n.
Unordered
{Blue, Red} = {Red, Blue}		 C(n + m − 1, m − 1)
case 4. Stars and bars. Count the vector, not the sequence. No condition on n.		 C(m, n) = m! / (n! (m−n)!)
case 3. Case 2 divided by the n! orderings. Needs m ≥ n.
Reading the table. m is how many distinguishable items are in the basket and n is how many draws are made. Case 4 is the only box that is not the multiplication rule with a division after it, because a collection holding a repeat has fewer distinct orderings than one without, so no single divisor works. All four are worked with the lecture's own examples on Part 10 and Part 11, and compared on one basket on Part 12.

The worked example: a screening test

FormulaRead it aloud asWhere
P(D), P(+ | D), P(− | Dc) Prevalence, sensitivity and specificity. The first is measured over everybody; the second only inside the ill; the third only inside the well. Every one of them conditions on something the patient does not know. Part 1
P(+ | Dc) = 1 − P(− | Dc) The false-positive rate. The complement rule applied inside the conditional world of the well, which is legal because conditioning on a fixed event gives a probability space of its own. Part 1
P(+) = P(D) P(+ | D) + P(Dc) P(+ | Dc) The law of total probability, partitioning by health. Two disjoint roads lead to an alarm; price each and add. Equivalently P(+ ∩ D) + P(+ ∩ Dc). Part 2
P(D | +) = P(+ | D) P(D) ÷ P(+) Bayes rule. Prior times likelihood over evidence. Read as a share: of all the alarms, what fraction came from the ill road. Part 3
P(D) = P(+) P(D | +) + P(−) P(D | −) The same law with the partition running the other way, by test result. All the disease sits either inside the alarms or inside the all-clears. The lecture writes this and stops; it evaluates no number for P(D | −). Part 4

Independence

FormulaRead it aloud asWhere
A ⊥ B  ⇔  P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B) Independence. The overlap equals the product of the two singles. Symmetric in A and B, and legal even when one of them has probability zero. Part 5
P(A) = P(A | B), for P(B) > 0 The equivalent conditional form. The world shrinking to B does not change A's share of it, so learning B is worth nothing as evidence about A. Part 5
for every I0 ⊆ {1..n}: P(∩i∈I0 Ai) = ∏i∈I0 P(Ai) Independence of n events. One equation per subset, not one equation in total. The lecture states explicitly that the full n-way product is only one of the required equations. Part 6
P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C) P(B | C) Conditional independence given C. The ordinary product test, run inside the world where C is known. It does not imply independence: mixing two independent tables with different rates gives a dependent one. Part 7

Counting

FormulaRead it aloud asWhere
P({i}) = 1 / |Ω| In a finite space where every outcome is equally likely, each single outcome carries one over the size of the space. The vertical bars are a count, not an absolute value. Part 8
P(A) = |A| / |Ω| The finite uniform model: favourable outcomes over total outcomes. Valid only under equal likelihood, and choosing a sample space where that fails is the commonest counting error there is. Part 8
m1 × m2 × ... × mn The basic counting rule. A configuration built from n stages, where stage i offers mi options whatever came before. When the count at a stage really does vary, split into cases, multiply inside each, and add the cases. Part 9
{n1, ..., nm}, ni ≥ 0, ∑ ni = n The outcome of case 4: one entry per basket item saying how many times it was drawn. The vector has length m, not n, and has nowhere to record an order. Counted by stars and bars. Part 11

Two places the lecture stops shortFirst, the reverse decomposition P(D) = P(+) P(D | +) + P(−) P(D | −) is written on the board and never evaluated, so the value 1/9,406 in the table above is this course's derivation and carries no key-number mark. Second, conditional independence is defined and never illustrated, so the two-replica figures are this course's construction. Both are flagged on their own pages, and the reasoning behind finishing the first rather than hiding it is recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md.
Your teacher. Print this sheet and fold it so only the four-box table shows. Then, for each box, say the lecture's own example aloud before looking: the playlist, the two posts, the four candidates, the popcorn. Four examples is a smaller thing to remember than four formulas, and each one reconstructs its formula in about ten seconds. The two lines to keep on the other side of the fold are Bayes rule and the independence product, which between them account for most of the wrong probability anybody will ever hand you.
