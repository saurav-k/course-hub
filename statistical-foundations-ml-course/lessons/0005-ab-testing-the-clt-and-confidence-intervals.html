A four point lift that might be nothing at all

15 min the densest page in the lecture Two buttons, two thousand users, and a difference that looks convincing until you ask how much of it randomness could have produced on its own.

The one-minute version

  • 1,000 users each see a grey button or a gold one. Grey gets 300 clicks, so p̂A = 0.30. Gold gets 340, so p̂B = 0.34.
  • Each user is a coin flip, so the observed rate is itself a random quantity. It has a centre, which is the true rate p, and a spread of p(1−p)/n.
  • The Central Limit Theorem says that for large n that random quantity is shaped like a bell, which is what lets us put a ruler on the noise.
  • The 95 percent intervals come out as [27.1%, 32.9%] and [31.0%, 37.0%]. They overlap, so this experiment has not established the lift.

1. The experiment, and the question underneath it

Marketing wants a higher conversion rate and believes a gold button will beat the grey one. So they run the obvious test: split traffic into two groups of 1,000 users each, show group A the grey button, show group B the gold button, and count clicks. Group A converts 300 times. Group B converts 340 times. The gold button is ahead by four percentage points.

The question slide 11 actually poses is sharper than "which is bigger". It is: are the observed differences real, or due to random sampling noise? Flip a fair coin a thousand times twice and you will not get 500 heads both times. Some difference always appears. The job is to work out how big a difference randomness alone can manufacture, and then see whether four points clears that bar.

2. Modelling one user, then a thousand

Slide 12 builds the model from the smallest possible piece. For each user i, define a variable that records only whether they clicked:

Xi = 1 if user i clicks, and 0 otherwise  ⇒  Xi ~ Bernoulli(p) A Bernoulli variable is the simplest random object there is: a single yes-or-no event with probability p of yes. The tilde ~ is read "is distributed as". Note the letter: p without a hat is the true, unknown click rate of the whole population.

The quantity we actually observe is the fraction of the group that clicked. Because each Xi is 0 or 1, adding them up counts the clicks, and dividing by n turns that count into a rate:

p̂ = (1/n) ∑i=1n Xi Read p̂ aloud as "p hat". The hat is the standard mark for an estimate computed from data, as opposed to the bare p, which is the true value we never see. Getting this pair straight is most of the battle on this page.

Here is the move that makes everything else work. p̂ is not a number. It is a random variable, because it is built out of a thousand random pieces. If you re-ran the same experiment tomorrow with a different thousand users, you would get a different p̂. So p̂ has a distribution of its own, and that distribution has a centre and a spread.

  1. The centre. Each user contributes 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise, so on average each contributes p. Averaging n of them still averages p: E[p̂] = p. The estimator is unbiased, meaning it is right on average even though it is wrong almost every time.
  2. The spread of one user. Since X is only ever 0 or 1, squaring it changes nothing, so E[X²] = E[X] = p. Then Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² = p − p² = p(1−p).
  3. The spread of the sum. The users are independent, and variances of independent things add, so the sum of n of them has variance n × p(1−p).
  4. The spread of the average. Dividing a random quantity by n divides its variance by n². So Var(p̂) = n × p(1−p) / n² = p(1−p) / n.

That final line is the engine of this whole case study, and of the next page as well. The uncertainty in an estimated rate shrinks as the sample grows, and the n sits in the denominator. Nothing else on the next three slides is new; it is all consequences of that one expression.

3. Putting a ruler on the noise

Slide 13 turns the bell shape into a usable number in two steps. First it standardises: subtract the centre and divide by the spread, so that whatever the original units were, the result is measured in "how many spreads away from the middle am I".

Z = ( p̂ − p ) / √( p(1−p)/n )  ≈  N(0, 1) N(0, 1) is the standard normal: the bell curve centred at 0 with a spread of 1. Standardising is the reason one table of numbers serves every normal distribution ever used.

Second, it reads one fact off that standard bell curve. About 95 percent of a standard normal sits between −1.96 and +1.96:

Pr( |Z| ≤ 1.96 ) ≈ 0.95 |Z| means the size of Z ignoring its sign, so this says "Z lands within 1.96 of zero, in either direction". The number 1.96 is not magic; it is simply where 95 percent of the area lives, and a different confidence level would give a different multiplier.

Undoing the standardisation gives the sentence slide 13 ends on: p̂ typically lands within ±1.96 √(p(1−p)/n) of the true p. That quantity, the spread of the estimate, is what everyone calls the standard error.

4. The two intervals, worked in full

One substitution has to happen before any of this is usable, and slide 14 makes it quietly. The formula wants p, the true rate, which is exactly the thing we do not know. So we put the estimate in its place. That gives the practical standard error:

SE = √( p̂(1 − p̂) / n ) Note the hats on the right-hand side: this is the estimate standing in for the truth. For n in the hundreds or thousands the substitution costs very little accuracy, which is why nobody makes a fuss about it.
  1. Standard error for the grey button. 0.30 × 0.70 = 0.21. Divide by 1,000 to get 0.00021. The square root of that is 0.01449, which the deck reports as 0.0145.
  2. Standard error for the gold button. 0.34 × 0.66 = 0.2244. Divide by 1,000 to get 0.0002244. The square root is 0.01498, reported as 0.0150.
  3. Choose the multiplier. Slide 14 takes Z ≈ 2 for the arithmetic, so each interval reaches out two standard errors either side of its estimate.
  4. Interval for the grey button. 2 × 0.0145 = 0.029, so CIA = 0.30 ± 0.029 = [27.1%, 32.9%].
  5. Interval for the gold button. 2 × 0.0150 = 0.030, so CIB = 0.34 ± 0.030 = [31.0%, 37.0%].
  6. Compare them. A reaches up to 32.9 percent and B reaches down to 31.0 percent. The two ranges share the strip from 31.0 to 32.9, so they overlap.
Overlap is a rough test, not a verdictComparing two confidence intervals by eye is a blunter instrument than the deck's phrasing suggests, and the asymmetry matters. If two intervals do not overlap, the difference is significant at that level. But if they do overlap, that does not establish the absence of a difference: overlapping intervals can still correspond to a genuinely significant result, because the correct comparison uses the standard error of the difference rather than the two separate standard errors. The proper tool is a two-proportion test, which this course reaches once hypothesis testing has been built up. The honest reading of slide 14 is therefore "this experiment has not established the lift", not "the gold button is no better". See Greenland et al. (2016), which catalogues this exact misreading among others.
One small inconsistency in the deckSlide 13 states the theory with the exact multiplier 1.96, and slide 14 then does the arithmetic with Z ≈ 2. That is normal practice and the difference is about two percent of the interval width, but it is worth noticing rather than being confused by. 1.96 is the exact 95 percent multiplier; 2 is the version you can do in your head. The intervals quoted on this page use 2, exactly as the deck does.

5. Check yourself

Group B's standard error, 0.0150, is slightly larger than group A's 0.0145, even though both groups had exactly 1,000 users. Why?
The spread depends on p̂(1−p̂), and that product is largest at one half and falls away towards either end. Since 0.34 is nearer to 0.50 than 0.30 is, group B carries slightly more inherent variability. Both groups had 1,000 users, so the second option contradicts the setup. The colour of a button changes the rate being measured, not the difficulty of measuring it. And nothing on slide 11 says the groups ran for different lengths of time; the sample sizes are stated as equal.
The two 95 percent intervals share the strip from 31.0 to 32.9 percent. What is the honest conclusion to report to marketing?
An overlap means the data is consistent with several true stories, including one where the two buttons are identical and one where gold is genuinely better, so nothing has been settled either way. The first option claims proof of no effect, which an inconclusive result never gives you. The third claims proof of an effect, which is the mistake the whole slide exists to prevent. The fourth blames the design, but the design is sound: it simply did not gather enough users, which is precisely what Part 6 fixes.
Your teacher. The habit worth building here is to stop reporting point estimates without their intervals. "Conversion went from 30 percent to 34 percent" and "conversion went from 27 to 33 percent up to 31 to 37 percent" are the same measurement, but only the second one lets the reader see how much of it might be nothing. Try rewriting one metric in your own reporting that way and watch how the conversation changes.
