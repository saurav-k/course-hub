Lecture 3: From the axioms to Bayes

8 pages foundation 5 min Aug 22, 2026. One lecture, split into eight sittings, because a whole lecture in one page is a lecture you close.

The one-minute version

  • Lecture 2 handed you a probability space and three axioms. This lecture puts those axioms to work: every rule on these eight pages is forced by them, not asserted.
  • First the rules the axioms imply on their own: complements, containment, and what to do when two events overlap.
  • Then the tools for many overlapping events, and for when even that is not computable: inclusion-exclusion, the union bound, and the Bonferroni brackets.
  • Finally the idea the rest of the course stands on: conditioning. Evidence shrinks the world, and Bayes rule is how a belief moves when it does.

How this lecture is arranged

Lecture 2 built the container: a sample space of outcomes, events as sets of outcomes, and a probability P that gives each event a number between 0 and 1, with three axioms fixing how those numbers behave. The container alone computes nothing yet. This lecture is the step from container to calculator, and each page is one move in that step.

The lecture is split here into eight content pages. Each one is a single idea, sized to one sitting, and each one ends by telling you why you are about to turn the page. Read them in order the first time. After that, the formula sheet is the faster way back in.

The eight parts

Each part names the faculty notes it covers, so you can read alongside them if you have them open. The notes for this session are seven handwritten pages dated Aug 22, 2026.

  1. What the axioms force (notes page 1). Four rules fall straight out of the three axioms: the empty event, the complement rule, containment, and the addition rule for overlapping events. Each one derived, not memorised.
  2. Inclusion-exclusion (notes page 1). Three events drawn as a three-circle Venn with every region labelled, then the general alternating sum for n events.
  3. The union bound and Bonferroni (notes pages 1 and 2). When the exact answer is hopeless, cut the alternating sum short and bracket the truth instead. Worked on 100 balls dropped into 20 bins.
  4. Conditional probability (notes pages 3, 4 and 7). Learning that B happened redraws the boundary of the world. The formula is that picture, written down.
  5. The multiplication rule (notes pages 5 and 6). The conditional definition read backwards, which chains: a probability tree for a coin tossed three times.
  6. The law of total probability (notes pages 5 and 6). Cut the world into slices that never overlap and together cover everything, price the event in each slice, and add.
  7. Bayes rule (notes pages 6 and 7). Prior belief, then data, then updated belief. The total-probability picture with the arrow reversed.
  8. Independence (ahead of the notes). One number equals the product of two others, and no Venn diagram can show it. The tutorial needs it, so it is taught here and the page says so.

If a formula ever arrives before you can picture what it is doing, that is a defect in the page and not in you. The glossary holds one-sentence definitions for every term, and the formula sheet holds every formula in the lecture on a single printable page.
Your teacher. Hold one question in your head as you read this lecture: when I add two probabilities, what exactly went wrong, and what fixes it? The answer is always the same, and it is always the overlap. Pages 1 to 3 fix it exactly, page 3 shows what to do when fixing it exactly is impossible, and everything after that builds on the picture of a world that shrank.
