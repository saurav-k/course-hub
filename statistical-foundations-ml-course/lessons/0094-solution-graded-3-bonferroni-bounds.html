Homework 1 · Graded problem 3 · the worked solution

Four routes to the same 91/216, and the third bound lands on it exactly

working 18 min The question is on the problem page and is not restated. This page carries the answers, four derivations of them, and the comparison part (c) asks for.

The one-minute version

  • Part (a). P(E1), exactly one six in three rolls, is 75/216 = 25/72, about 0.3472.
  • Part (b). P(E2), at least one six, is 91/216, about 0.4213.
  • Part (c). First order says P(E2) is at most 1/2. Second order says it is at least 5/12, about 0.4167. Third order says it is at most 91/216 - which is the exact value itself.
  • Three events have no fourth level, so the third truncation is the whole of inclusion-exclusion. The bracket does not merely tighten; it closes.

1. What every route needs first

All four derivations share three facts. Fair gives every face probability 1/6 on one roll. Independent lets a statement about all three rolls factor into single-roll ones. And one naming choice does the structural work: let Ai be the event that roll i shows a six, for i = 1, 2, 3.

P(Ai) = 1/6    P(Ai ∩ Aj) = 1/36    P(A1 ∩ A2 ∩ A3) = 1/216 One named roll shows a six once in six; two named rolls both do once in thirty-six; all three do once in two hundred and sixteen. The symbol ∩ ("intersection") means "and". Each is the one before it times another 1/6, which is what independence licenses.

E2 is then exactly A1 ∪ A2 ∪ A3, the union every Bonferroni inequality is written about - which is why part (c) attaches to part (b) and not to part (a).

2. Route one: the axioms first

Part (a). Split E1 by which roll carries the six, into patterns B1, B2, B3. They are disjoint: a pattern with its six on roll 1 has roll 2 showing something else, so it is not the roll-2 pattern.

  1. Price one piece by the multiplication rule. Independence factors a three-roll statement into single-roll ones: P(B1) = (1/6)(5/6)(5/6) = 25/216.
  2. The other two match. The same three factors in a different order, so B2 and B3 are each 25/216.
  3. Add by finite additivity. The third axiom sums disjoint events: P(E1) = 3 × 25/216 = 75/216 = 25/72 ≈ 0.3472.

Part (b). E2 and its complement are a disjoint cover of Ω, and P(Ω) = 1, so the two sum to 1. That is the complement rule, and "no six on any roll" is one product.

  1. Price the complement. Each roll misses six with probability 5/6, so P(no six) = (5/6)3 = 125/216.
  2. Subtract from one. P(E2) = 1 − 125/216 = 91/216 ≈ 0.4213.

Part (c). Monotonicity and finite additivity give subadditivity, which is the first-order bound; the second and third orders are inclusion-exclusion stopped after two and three levels.

  1. The singles sum. S1 = 3 × 1/6 = 1/2 = 108/216. First order: P(E2) ≤ 1/2.
  2. The pairs sum. Three pairs at 1/36, so S2 = 1/12 = 18/216. Second order: P(E2) ≥ 108/216 − 18/216 = 90/216 = 5/12 ≈ 0.4167.
  3. The triple. S3 = 1/216. Third order: P(E2) ≤ 90/216 + 1/216 = 91/216.

The third bound reproduces part (b) exactly, and that is structural: three events give the alternating sum no fourth level, so keeping three keeps all of them.

3. Route two: counting in a uniform space

Fairness and independence together say more than either alone: all 6 × 6 × 6 = 216 ordered triples of faces are equally likely. A probability is then a count over 216, and the problem becomes integer arithmetic a marker can check by eye.

  1. Count E1. Three positions for the six, one face there, five non-six faces at each of the other two: 3 × 1 × 5 × 5 = 75 triples, so P(E1) = 75/216.
  2. Count E2 by its complement. Triples with no six: 53 = 125. So E2 holds 216 − 125 = 91, and P(E2) = 91/216.
  3. Count the Bonferroni terms. Each Ai holds 36 triples, so the singles sum counts 108; each pair intersection holds 6, so the pairs sum counts 18; the triple holds 1.
  4. Read the bounds off. 108/216 = 1/2; (108 − 18)/216 = 5/12; (108 − 18 + 1)/216 = 91/216.

Every quantity is a whole number over one denominator, which is what makes part (c) hard to get wrong here.

4. Route three: the set picture

Draw the three events as overlapping regions and part (c) becomes visible rather than algebraic. Seven zones exist, and the counting route has priced every one.

Follow the multipliers and the alternation stops being a rule to memorise. The singles sum overcounts each lens by one and the centre by two. Subtracting the pairs sum takes one copy off each lens and, since the centre lies in all three pairs, three off the centre - one too many, leaving it at zero. Adding the triple back puts it at one, and every zone now stands at exactly one, which is what a union needs.

5. Route four: conditioning on the first roll

The routes above look at all three rolls at once. This one is the law of total probability, applied to the first roll alone.

Part (b). Write un for at least one six in n rolls. Either the first roll is a six, which settles it, or it is not and n − 1 rolls remain with the same question. Disjoint, and they cover everything, so they add.

un = 1/6 + (5/6) × un−1, with u0 = 0 Read as: the chance of at least one six in n rolls is the chance the first roll is a six, plus 5/6 times the chance the remaining n minus 1 rolls produce one. With no rolls there is no six, so u subscript zero is 0.
  1. One roll. u1 = 1/6 + (5/6) × 0 = 1/6 = 36/216.
  2. Two rolls. u2 = 1/6 + (5/6) × (1/6) = 6/36 + 5/36 = 11/36 = 66/216.
  3. Three rolls. u3 = 1/6 + (5/6) × (11/36) = 36/216 + 55/216 = 91/216.

Part (a). The same split prices exactly one six. Write en for that and qn = (5/6)n for no six at all.

  1. State the step. A first six needs none after it, a first non-six needs one: en = (1/6) × qn−1 + (5/6) × en−1, with e0 = 0.
  2. Two rolls. e2 = (1/6)(5/6) + (5/6)(1/6) = 10/36.
  3. Three rolls. e3 = (1/6)(25/36) + (5/6)(10/36) = 25/216 + 50/216 = 75/216.
This route does not deliver part (c)The recursion never forms an intersection sum, so it has nothing to truncate: it reaches the exact union directly and skips the object the inequalities are built from. Calling it a fourth derivation of the bounds would mean computing those sums another way and relabelling the result. Parts (a) and (b) therefore carry four routes each, and part (c) carries three - the axioms, the counting and the set picture.

8. Check yourself

Why does the third-order Bonferroni bound land exactly on P(E2) here rather than merely above it?
Inclusion-exclusion over n events has exactly n levels, so keeping three of three keeps all of them. Independence only made each term easy to price, and a dependent trio would still close at the third order; fairness does the same job and no more. A larger triple intersection would change the number, not the exactness.
The singles sum counts 108 outcomes when only 91 exist. Where do the 17 extra counts come from?
Three lenses of 5 counted twice leave 15 spare, and the centre of 1 counted three times leaves 2: that is the 17. A lens once and the centre twice leaves one spare, which is the second-order position. Nothing outside the union enters a singles sum, and the no-six triples are in no A(i) at all.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

The same fair die is now rolled four independent times, and Ai is again the event that roll i shows a six.

Find the exact P(at least one six), then the first-, second- and third-order Bonferroni bounds on it, and say which of the three brackets the truth and which no longer does.

Hint

There are now four singles, six pairs and four triples. Work in 1296ths: every term is a whole number over 64.

Show the worked solution
  1. The exact value. 1 − (5/6)4 = 1 − 625/1296 = 671/1296 ≈ 0.5177.
  2. The singles sum. 4 × 216/1296 = 864/1296. First order: at most 2/3 ≈ 0.6667.
  3. The pairs sum. Six pairs, each 36/1296, so 216/1296. Second order: at least (864 − 216)/1296 = 648/1296 = 0.5000.
  4. The triples sum. Four triples, each 6/1296, so 24/1296. Third order: at most (864 − 216 + 24)/1296 = 672/1296 ≈ 0.5185.
  5. Read the bracket. 0.5000 ≤ 0.5177 ≤ 0.5185, so the second and third orders still bracket the truth. The third order is now a genuine bound rather than the exact value, because a fourth level exists and has been dropped: the missing term is the single quadruple intersection, 1/1296, and 672/1296 − 1/1296 is exactly 671/1296.

Sanity check. Four rolls must beat three, and 0.5177 is above 0.4213, as it must be. The third-order bound overshoots by exactly 1/1296, which is the term you dropped - if your gap is not one single outcome, a term is wrong.

Your teacher. Notice what a fourth roll did to the third-order bound: it stopped being exact the moment a fourth event appeared. A truncation is exact only when nothing is left to truncate, which is why the union bound outlives this page. With three replicas you can compute the exact chance that one breaches its target; with three hundred you cannot, and the first-order bound is what you ship. Here is where you found out what it costs while you could still check: 18.7 per cent.
