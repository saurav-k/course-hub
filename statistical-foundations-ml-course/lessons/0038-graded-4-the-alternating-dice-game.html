The game either ends now, or forgets that it happened

hardest thinking per mark 10 min The last two marks on the sheet, and the only question on it with no parts: two players, two targets, alternating throws, and a structure that repeats.

The one-minute version

  • A and B throw a pair of dice alternately. A wins the game if his throw totals 9; B wins if his throw totals 4. A throws first. Find the probability that B wins. [2 marks]
  • Look at one exchange: A throws, and then - only if A missed - B throws. The exchange ends in one of three ways, and one of those ways leaves the game exactly where it started.
  • That is the whole problem: either the game ends inside the first exchange, or it restarts from scratch. The self-similarity is the handle, and the tool that prices it is the law of total probability.
  • Before any equation, you need each player's per-throw probability: the chance that a throw of two dice totals 9, and the chance it totals 4. This page names all of that and sets up nothing.

The problem, restated in full

A and B throw a pair of dice alternately. A wins the game if he gets a total of 9, and B wins the game if he gets a total of 4. If A starts the game, find the probability that B wins. [2 marks]

Every symbol, named: a pair of dice is two ordinary six-sided dice thrown together on one turn, and the total is the sum of the two faces showing. Alternately means the turns strictly alternate: A throws, then B throws, then A again, and so on for as long as nobody has won. A starts means the very first throw of the game is A's, which quietly decides who can win on the very first exchange. A wins the game and B wins the game are the two ways the game can end; the question asks for the probability of the second of them.

What it is really testing

Whether you can see that an endless-looking game has a repeating shape, and price that shape with the tool built for slicing a world into cases: the law of total probability. The conditioning instinct comes from Lecture 3, Conditional probability - B only ever throws given that A missed - and the chaining of the two throws inside one exchange is the multiplication rule. Nothing here needs machinery beyond Lecture 3. What it needs is the observation, and the observation is worth staring at before any algebra happens.

The structure: one exchange, three endings, one of them the game again

Watch one exchange of the game - A's throw, and then, only if A missed, B's throw. Exactly three things can happen:

  1. A's throw totals 9. The game is over, and B never throws at all.
  2. A misses, and B's throw totals 4. The game is over, and B has won it.
  3. Both miss. Nobody has scored, and now look at the position: it is once again A's turn to throw, nobody has scored yet, and the targets are unchanged. The game stands precisely where it stood before the first throw.

That third ending is the entire problem. Either the game ends inside the first exchange, or it hands itself back to itself, unchanged, to run again. Whatever is true of the game from its starting position is true again after every fruitless exchange, because the position is the same position. This self-similarity is the handle: it is what turns "an unbounded number of rounds" from a frightening infinity into one exchange plus itself. The tool that converts "one exchange, three endings, one of which is the whole game again" into a number is exactly the slice-and-weight scheme of the law of total probability - but the equation that does it is yours to write, and this page stops short of it on purpose.

The two numbers you need before anything else

Both exits of the exchange are priced by a single throw of the pair, so before the structure can become an equation you need two per-throw probabilities, defined and in hand:

  • the probability that one throw of the pair totals 9 - A's per-throw probability; and
  • the probability that one throw of the pair totals 4 - B's per-throw probability.

Each is a share of the natural sample space for one throw: the grid of ordered pairs, one axis per die, every cell equally likely. A target total collects the cells whose faces sum to it. Which cells belong to each target, and how many there are, is the first computation of the problem - and the two targets are different totals, so expect the two shares to be different numbers. Nothing about the two players is interchangeable: A throws first and aims at 9, B throws second and aims at 4, and any solution that leans on symmetry between them is solving a different game.

The traps

  • A starts, and that is not decoration. B's first throw only happens if A misses. The exchange is a sequence - A's throw, then conditionally B's - and treating it as two independent shots at nothing misprices both exits.
  • The two targets are different totals, so the two per-throw probabilities are different numbers. Nothing is symmetric between the players: not the targets, not the throw order. A solution that argues "by symmetry" is answering a different game.
  • "A pair of dice" means two dice, and the total is the sum. The sample space of a throw is the grid of ordered pairs - which die shows what - not the eleven possible sums. Counting on the sums as if they were equally likely is the classic way this problem goes wrong at step one.
  • The question asks for the probability that B wins. Not A. With a game this easy to misread, reread the final sentence before you write your answer down.
  • The game can in principle run forever. Nobody is owed a win on any finite number of exchanges. That is not a flaw in the problem, and the method does not need the game to end - the returning arrow in the map above is what absorbs the infinity.

How to start

Define the two per-throw probabilities first: A's, the share of the ordered-pair grid whose cells total 9, and B's, the share whose cells total 4. Count each honestly from the grid. Then slice the first exchange by its three endings - A hits; A misses and B hits; both miss - and notice which of the three endings is the game again, unchanged. Then stop. The equation that turns "one exchange, three endings, one of which is the whole game" into the probability that B wins is the homework. It is one line long, and you have to be the one to write it.

Check yourself

The first exchange ends with both players missing. What is true of the game at that moment?
Both players missed, so nobody scored, the targets are unchanged, and the turn passes back to A: the position is the starting position, which is the self-similarity the whole solution hangs on. B does not get a turn - B only ever throws straight after an A miss - the game is nowhere near over, and there is no sudden-death rule anywhere in the wording.
Which tool from the course turns "the first exchange has three endings, one of which is the game again" into a probability?
The endings slice the first exchange into disjoint cases, and the event "B wins" happens inside one of them or recurs through the third - pricing each case and adding the weights is exactly the slice-and-weight scheme of total probability. Bayes rule runs evidence backwards to causes, which is not the shape of this question. The union bound produces ceilings, not the exact answer a two-mark question wants. And the throws inside an exchange are sequential rather than overlapping, so there is nothing for inclusion-exclusion to correct.
Before any equation can be written, which quantities does the solution need in hand?
Both exits of the exchange are priced by per-throw probabilities - A's share of cells totalling 9 and B's share totalling 4 - so those two numbers come first, exactly as the how-to-start section says. The game has no fixed length to predict, the no-winner event is never asked about, and the grid of ordered pairs already treats the dice as distinguishable, which is what makes the cells equally likely in the first place.
