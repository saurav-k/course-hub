"At least one" questions are union questions, and the overlaps carry the answer

working 11 min Three practice problems from the unsubmitted half of Practice Set 1, each carried to a number and a limit.

After this page

  • You can run inclusion-exclusion over three events, pricing each overlap by a least common multiple rather than a product.
  • You can turn "nobody" into the complement of a union, price it with the n-event formula, and read off its limit.

Before you start: inclusion-exclusion, for the alternating sign pattern, and what the axioms force, for the complement and addition rules.

The one-minute version

  • All three ask a "one or more" question, so the event is a union of overlapping pieces and inclusion-exclusion prices it without double-counting.
  • Problem 10's divisors share factors, so its overlaps are least common multiples, not products. The answer is 388,889 out of a million.
  • Problems 20 and 23 are one question asked twice: "nobody keeps his own hat" and "at least one does" are complements.
  • The alternating sum is the front of the series for e-1, so Pn settles at about 0.3679 and the union at about 0.6321.

What decides each of the three

Problem 10 - divisible by at least one of 4, 6 and 9

The sheet names the tool inside the question, so the route is fixed: three singles added, three pairwise overlaps subtracted, the triple overlap added back. The one place a rushed attempt dies is the overlaps. Divisible by both 4 and 6 means divisible by 12, not by 24, because 4 and 6 share a factor of 2; the same shrinkage hits 6 with 9, and only the coprime pair 4 with 9 has an overlap equal to its product.

Problem 20 - the absent-minded hat-checker

"No man gets his own hat" is a property of the whole rearrangement, not of any one man, so it is the complement of the union of the events Ei that man i is served correctly. Those events are neither disjoint nor independent, which is exactly why the full n-event formula is needed and why every shortcut built on either assumption collapses.

Honest gapThe course's counting pages have counted uniform finite spaces but have not yet worked arrangements of n distinct objects. The step below that sizes the space at n! and a fixed-k family at (n - k)! is that missing move, so the solution checks it against a full hand count at n = 3.

Problem 23 - the hat-themed party game

Different furniture, identical randomness: guests reordered at random against a fixed row of hats is the same uniform rearrangement as the restaurant's. The sheet even hands over the notation. So problem 23's target is one subtraction away from problem 20's, and the only fresh work is recognising that there is none.

In problem 10, the overlap "divisible by both 4 and 6" is priced through a least common multiple rather than a product, because:
That equivalence is the definition doing the work: anything divisible by both 4 and 6 is divisible by their smallest common multiple, which sits strictly below the product because the two share a factor. Scale is irrelevant - nothing about one million drives it, and the same reasoning holds on any range. Multiplication preserves divisibility information perfectly well, so nothing degrades as numbers grow. And the formula accepts whichever quantity equals the overlap: for a pair sharing no factor, that quantity is exactly the product.

Practice about 30 minutes, with paper

Sheet problem 10 - divisible by 4, 6 or 9working

A number is chosen uniformly from 1 to 1,000,000. Using the inclusion-exclusion principle, determine the probability that the chosen number is divisible by one or more of 4, 6 and 9.

Hint

Multiples of d up to N number floor(N / d). Every overlap is the multiples of a least common multiple - work each one out before counting.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the events. D4, D6, D9 are the events that the draw is a multiple of 4, 6, 9. The target is P(D4 ∪ D6 ∪ D9). With N = 1,000,000 equally likely draws, every probability is a count over N, and the multiples of d in the range number floor(N / d).
  2. The three singles. floor(N / 4) = 250,000; floor(N / 6) = 166,666; floor(N / 9) = 111,111. Sum: 527,777.
  3. The three overlaps, by least common multiple. lcm(4, 6) = 12 because 4 = 22 and 6 = 2 × 3 give 22 × 3; lcm(4, 9) = 36, the two being coprime; lcm(6, 9) = 18. Counts: floor(N / 12) = 83,333; floor(N / 36) = 27,777; floor(N / 18) = 55,555. Sum: 166,665.
  4. The triple overlap. lcm(4, 6, 9) = 22 × 32 = 36, so floor(N / 36) = 27,777.
  5. Assemble with alternating signs. 527,777 - 166,665 + 27,777 = 388,889 favourable numbers.
  6. Divide. P = 388,889 / 1,000,000 = 0.388889.

Sanity check. Ignore the rounding in the floors and work in exact fractions with denominator 36: 9 + 6 + 4 - 3 - 1 - 2 + 1 = 14, so P ≈ 14/36 = 0.3889, agreeing to four decimals. Two structural checks: the 4-and-9 overlap and the triple overlap are both the multiples of 36, so those terms cancel and the answer is really 250,000 + 166,666 + 111,111 - 83,333 - 55,555. And 0.3889 must sit between 1/4 = 0.25, the largest single, and 1/4 + 1/6 + 1/9 = 0.5278, the sum with no correction - it does.

Sheet problem 20 - the absent-minded hat-checkerfrontier

A group of n men enter a restaurant and check their hats. The hat-checker is absent-minded and, on their way out, redistributes the hats at random. What is the probability Pn that no man gets his correct hat, and how does Pn behave as n approaches infinity?

Hint

Let Ei be "man i gets his own hat" and price the union. Fixing k named men still leaves the other n - k hats free.

Show the worked solution
  1. Size the space. An outcome is one complete assignment of n hats to n men, so there are n! equally likely outcomes.
  2. Price a k-fold overlap. For a fixed set of k men, the outcomes in which all k hold their own hats leave the remaining n - k hats to be scattered freely: (n - k)! of them. So the probability of that intersection is (n - k)! / n!, the same for every choice of k men, and there are C(n, k) such choices.
  3. Run inclusion-exclusion. P(E1 ∪ ... ∪ En) = Σk=1..n (-1)k+1 × C(n, k) × (n - k)! / n!.
  4. Simplify one term. C(n, k) × (n - k)! / n! = [n! / (k! (n - k)!)] × (n - k)! / n! = 1 / k!. Every binomial coefficient and factorial cancels, leaving P(union) = Σk=1..n (-1)k+1 / k! = 1 - 1/2! + 1/3! - ...
  5. Take the complement. Pn = 1 - P(union) = Σk=0..n (-1)k / k! = 1 - 1 + 1/2! - 1/3! + ... + (-1)n / n!.
  6. Let n grow. That is the first n + 1 terms of the series for ex at x = -1, so Pn → e-1 ≈ 0.3679. It does not fall towards zero: adding more men barely moves it.

Sanity check. Test n = 3 by hand. The formula gives 1 - 1 + 1/2 - 1/6 = 1/3. Listing all 3! = 6 assignments of hats to men 1, 2, 3, exactly two - (2, 3, 1) and (3, 1, 2) - leave nobody with his own hat, and 2/6 = 1/3. The series alternates with shrinking terms, so P4 = 0.375 and P5 ≈ 0.3667 already bracket the limit 0.3679 tightly - convergence is fast enough that P7 is right to four decimals.

Sheet problem 23 - the party hat gamefoundation

Guests at a party place their hats on the ground in front of them, are reordered randomly with all orderings equally likely, and each grabs the hat now in front of them. Let Ai be the event that guest i picks up his own hat. Find P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ ... ∪ An), and say what it converges to as n grows infinitely large.

Hint

The random part is identical to the restaurant's. Ask which of the two events this one is.

Show the worked solution
  1. Match the experiment. The hats stay in a fixed row and the guests are permuted uniformly, so the guest-to-hat correspondence is a uniformly random rearrangement of n items - the same space of n! equally likely outcomes as problem 20, with Ai playing the role of Ei.
  2. Identify the event. The union of the Ai is "at least one guest ends up with his own hat", which is exactly the complement of problem 20's "no man gets his correct hat".
  3. Subtract. P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An) = 1 - Pn = Σk=1..n (-1)k+1 / k! = 1 - 1/2! + 1/3! - ... + (-1)n+1 / n!.
  4. Let n grow. Since Pn → e-1, the union tends to 1 - e-1 ≈ 0.6321. Roughly five parties in eight see at least one guest reunited with his own hat, however large the party.

Sanity check. At n = 3 the formula gives 1 - 1/2 + 1/6 = 2/3, and problem 20's hand count found 2 derangements out of 6, so 4 of the 6 assignments leave somebody holding his own hat: 4/6 = 2/3. The two answers add to one, as complements must. And the limit is not 1 - if it were, a big enough party would guarantee a match, which the shrinking alternating terms plainly do not deliver.

Carry this away

  • An overlap of two divisibility events is the multiples of a least common multiple. Reaching for the product is the one error problem 10 is built to catch.
  • "Nobody" is the complement of "at least one", and "at least one" is a union - so a nobody question is an inclusion-exclusion question in disguise.
  • In the hat problem every binomial coefficient cancels, leaving the alternating series for e-1: about 0.3679 nobody, about 0.6321 at least one, for any large n.
