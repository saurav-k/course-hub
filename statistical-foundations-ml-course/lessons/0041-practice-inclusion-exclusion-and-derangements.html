"At least one" questions are union questions, and the overlaps carry the answer

working 11 min Three practice problems from the unsubmitted half of Practice Set 1. Each entry says what the question really asks and where to start, then stops: every count, every probability and every limit is yours to produce.

The one-minute version

  • Practice Set 1 carries thirty practice problems that are not submitted. This page guides three of them - sheet problems 10, 20 and 23 - and solves none of them.
  • All three ask a "one or more" question. The event is a union of overlapping pieces, and inclusion-exclusion is the tool that prices such a union without double-counting.
  • Problem 10's divisors share factors, so its pairwise overlaps are priced by least common multiples rather than products.
  • Problems 20 and 23 are one question asked two ways: "nobody gets his own hat" and "at least one guest keeps his own hat" are complements. Together they form the classical derangement problem. The limit, its formula and the constant behind it stay out of these notes on purpose.

The second half is thirty practice problems, not submitted, in the sheet's own words "for practice, solutions not to be submitted". Everything on this page belongs to the second half. Keep the two apart when you plan the week: only the first four cost marks.

The three problems, one at a time

Sheet problem 10 - divisible by at least one of 4, 6 and 9

Restated in full. A number is chosen uniformly from the range running from 1 up to 1,000,000. Using the inclusion-exclusion principle - the sheet names the tool inside the question itself - determine the probability that the chosen number is divisible by one or more of 4, 6 and 9.

The symbols, named. "Uniformly" means every number in the range is equally likely, so probabilities come from the uniform measure of the probability measure page: an event's probability is its share of the range. Define three events: D4 is the event that the drawn number is a multiple of 4, D6 that it is a multiple of 6, and D9 that it is a multiple of 9. "Divisible by one or more of them" is the union D4 ∪ D6 ∪ D9, an event in the sense of the events-are-subsets page.

What it is really testing. The three-event form of inclusion-exclusion: single probabilities added, pairwise overlaps subtracted, the triple overlap added back once, because every point must land in the tally exactly once. The two-event seed of the same idea is the addition rule of the axioms page. The divisibility wrapping is new; the sign pattern is not.

The traps. The decisive one: these three divisors are not pairwise coprime. Divisible by both 4 and 6 does not mean divisible by their product - it means divisible by their least common multiple, which sits strictly below the product precisely because 4 and 6 share a factor. The same holds for 6 with 9. Only the pair 4 and 9, which share no factor, has a least common multiple equal to its product. So of the three lens-shaped overlaps, two shrink and one does not, and each must be priced through its own least common multiple - whatever those numbers are; none is evaluated on this page. Second trap: "one or more" includes numbers divisible by all three at once, which is exactly why the formula carries a triple-overlap term. Third trap: the sheet dictates the method. Even if another route tempts you, the exercise is running the named principle, term by term.

How to start. Write the union. Underneath it, write what inclusion-exclusion needs for three events: three single probabilities, three pairwise overlap probabilities, one triple overlap probability, with alternating signs. Replace each overlap by the words "multiples of the relevant least common multiple". Each of the eight terms is then a share of the uniform range - favourable numbers over all numbers - and each share is a count you have not done yet. Do those counts on paper, assemble the eight terms with their signs, and evaluate: none of that arithmetic appears on this page, because doing it is the exercise.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Moderate: the sign pattern is bookkeeping from the inclusion-exclusion page, and the lcm point above is the one place a rushed attempt goes wrong.

Sheet problem 20 - the absent-minded hat-checker

Restated in full. A group of n men enter a restaurant and check their hats. The hat-checker is absent-minded, and upon leaving she redistributes the hats back to the men at random. The question has two halves: what is the probability Pn that no man gets his correct hat, and how does Pn behave as n approaches infinity?

The symbols, named. n is the number of men, and there are n hats, one belonging to each man. "Redistributes at random" means every way of handing the n hats back to the n men is equally likely - a uniform space in the sense of the probability measure page. Pn is the sheet's own name for the target. For each man i define the event Ei: man i receives his own hat back. Each Ei lives in the sample space of complete rearrangements, per the sample-space page: one outcome settles who receives whose hat, for everybody at once.

What it is really testing. Turning a "nobody" statement into a complement of a union. "No man gets his correct hat" is the same run as "it is not the case that at least one man gets his correct hat", which is the complement of E1 ∪ E2 ∪ ... ∪ En - the complement rule of the axioms page. That union is priced by the general n-event form of inclusion-exclusion, and each alternating term is a share of rearrangements in which a specific set of men all receive their own hats, countable under the uniform discipline of the geometry-and-counting page. This pair of questions is known in the literature as the derangement problem; the name is worth having, and the working still belongs to you.

The traps. First, the events Ei are neither disjoint nor independent - several men can hold their own hats in the very same rearrangement - so any shortcut built on either assumption collapses. Second, whatever can be said about one man's chances alone is not yet a statement about nobody; the distance between those two is the whole problem, and crossing it is where inclusion-exclusion earns its keep. Third, the second half asks a limiting question about how Pn behaves as the party grows. Whether it settles anywhere, and if so where, is precisely what the sheet wants from you - nothing on this page previews the destination, the expression behind it, or the constant that would appear there.

Honest gapThe course's counting pages have counted uniform finite spaces, but they have not yet worked arrangements of n distinct objects - the move that sizes the whole rearrangement space before any event inside it is counted. The principle behind it is nothing new: it is the sample-space discipline of the sample-space page plus the uniform counting of the geometry-and-counting page. If sizing the space stalls you, the gap is in the course's coverage so far, not in your reading.

How to start. Define the events Ei. Express "no man gets his correct hat" as a complement of their union. Write down what the n-event inclusion-exclusion formula asks you to price: unions of increasing families of the Ei, taken with alternating signs, each a share of rearrangements in which every man of a chosen set simultaneously holds his own hat. Count those shares with paper and patience. Then, and only then, look at what your expression seems to do as n grows, and answer the sheet's second half in your own words. Every step past "define Ei" is yours.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Among the harder practice items: the complement flip demands care, and the growing-n half demands a sentence about behaviour, not merely a computed value at some fixed size.

Sheet problem 23 - the hat-themed party game

Restated in full. Guests at a party line up and place their hats on the ground in front of them. The guests are then reordered randomly, all orderings equally likely, and afterwards each guest grabs the hat now in front of them. The sheet asks for the probability that at least one guest is wearing his own hat at the end, and for what that probability converges to as the number of guests grows infinitely large. It even supplies the notation: let Ai be the event that the i-th person picks up his own hat; the quantity of interest is written as the probability of A1 ∪ A2 ∪ ... ∪ An, the union over all guests.

The symbols, named. Ai is the sheet's own letter for "guest i picks his own hat", playing exactly the role Ei played in problem 20. The reordering is again a uniformly random rearrangement of people against hats, so the underlying experiment is the same object as before: one outcome settles, for every guest at once, which hat ends up on whose head. The target is a union, in the sense of the sets page: it happens whenever at least one of the Ai happens.

Two sheets, one questionProblem 20 and problem 23 are the same question asked two ways. Both stage one uniformly random redistribution of n hats among n people. Problem 20 asks for nobody holding his own hat, Pn; problem 23 asks for at least one guest keeping his own hat, the union of the Ai. Those two events are complements: on every single run of the game, exactly one of the two occurs, so the two probabilities sum to one, and pricing either hands you the other for one subtraction. The pair together is the classical derangement problem named above. What the limit is, what expression it converges to, and which constant it involves - none of that appears on this page, and both sheets will accept their answers only from your hand, related by that one complement step.

The traps. Reading the party story as a different problem because the costumes differ - line up, grab the hat in front - when the random part is identical to the restaurant's. Ignoring the notation the sheet hands you free of charge: the Ai and the union are written there, so the setup is done before you begin. And answering the convergence half by feel: whatever you believe about crowds growing large, the sheet wants the behaviour derived from the expression you assembled, and this page leaves that derivation untouched.

How to start. Convince yourself first that the game generates the same space of equally likely rearrangements as problem 20's restaurant - same people-versus-hats correspondence, different furniture. Write the union of the Ai. Relate it to Pn by the complement rule of the axioms page, and confirm the relation reads the same in both directions. Then carry over, word for word, the starting recipe from problem 20: define the events, write the union, list what inclusion-exclusion prices, and stop where the counting starts. The stopping line is the same line.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Short once the twinship with problem 20 is seen; the only fresh work is recognising that there is no fresh work.
