Every independence verdict is one product test, once the events are straight

working 12 min Four practice problems from the unsubmitted half of Practice Set 1. Each entry says what the question really asks and where to start, then stops: the verdicts are yours to produce.

The one-minute version

  • Practice Set 1 carries thirty practice problems that are not submitted. This page guides four of them - sheet problems 3, 4, 13 and 27 - and solves none of them.
  • All four are independence and conditioning questions. Each is decided by the same move: compare the overlap's probability with the product of the singles.
  • Problem 13 is the hardest of the four. Its reward events are two-toss windows, and whether two windows share a toss is the whole question.
  • Problem 27 runs one inequality in two directions: given P(A | B) > P(A), produce two further strict inequalities.

The four problems, one at a time

Sheet problem 3 - three events from three tosses

Restated in full. You flip a fair coin three times and record the outcome as an ordered triple of H and T, one position per toss in order. Three events are defined. A is the event that all tosses have the same result, meaning all H or all T. B is the event that you see at least one H and at least one T. C is the event that you see at most one H. The question asks, for each of the three pairs - A with B, B with C, and A with C - whether the pair is independent.

The symbols, named. The ordered triple records toss 1, then toss 2, then toss 3. "Fair" means every triple is equally likely, so probabilities come from the uniform measure of the probability measure page: an event's probability is its share of the sample space. A, B and C are events in the sense of the events-are-subsets page - each is the set of triples on which its description holds.

What it is really testing. The product test from the independence page: a pair is independent exactly when the probability of the pair's overlap equals the product of the two single probabilities. The problem dresses that one test in three different event descriptions and asks you to run it three times, on a space you count yourself. It is the practice-section twin of tutorial problem 12, minus the mutual-independence twist of the pairwise-versus-mutual page.

The traps. First, "at most one H" includes the case of no H at all - read "at most" literally, it is the classic misread here. Second, B's wording is long, but it names one event, not two: seeing at least one of each letter is the same triple set as "not all three the same". Third, before computing anything, check which pairs can co-occur at all, by meaning alone - the independence page devotes a section to why events that cannot co-occur are the furthest thing from independent, and one of these three pairs is of that shape.

How to start. List the sample space as ordered triples of H and T. Mark, for each triple, whether it sits in A, in B, and in C. Then fill the table above one row at a time: the overlap's probability from your marks, each single probability from your marks, the product, the comparison. The counting is the work, and the work is yours.

Sheet marks and difficulty. 1 mark per part, 3 in all. Routine product tests; the only real difficulty is reading C's wording carefully and not rushing the counts.

Sheet problem 4 - complements of an independent pair

Restated in full. Show that if two events A and B are independent, then Ac and Bc are also independent. Here Ac is the complement of A - everything in the sample space outside A - and Bc is defined the same way for B.

What it is really testing. Whether you can take the definition of independence - one equation between probabilities - and push it through a set operation. This exact fact is proved on the independence page, section 5, and the TA page on pairwise independence proves it for all three complemented pairings at once. The sheet still wants the proof in your own hand, so treat those pages as answer keys to check against afterwards, not as substitutes for the attempt.

The traps. The sheet asks only about the pairing where both events are complemented, not about the two mixed pairings - though proving a mixed pairing first is a legitimate stepping stone if you want one. And there is no rule that lets you "complement both sides" of an independence statement in a single move; the honest route runs through the space's partition and De Morgan's law, named on the sets page.

How to start. Write down what you may assume: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). Write down what you must produce: the same product rule with both events complemented. The four tiles in the figure are your bridge - express what you must produce through the tile structure, let the addition rule turn unions into sums, and use the assumption where the first tile appears. De Morgan's law is the cleaner route to the fourth tile if you prefer it.

Sheet marks and difficulty. 2 marks. Routine once the partition is seen - it is a bookkeeping proof, not a clever one. The course has proved it twice; producing it unaided is the point of the exercise.

Sheet problem 13 - reward windows over consecutive tosses

Restated in full. A coin is tossed independently n times, and the probability of heads at each single toss is p, the same at every toss. At each time k, for k running from 2 to n, you are handed a reward if the k-th toss was a head and the toss just before it was a tail. Ak is the event that a reward is obtained at time k. Part (a): are the events Ak and Ak+1 independent? Part (b): are the events Ak and Ak+2 independent? The sheet adds two words that carry marks-shaped weight: justify all answers.

The symbols, named. n is the number of tosses. p is the probability of heads on any one toss. k is a time index, and it starts at 2 because a reward needs a previous toss and toss 1 has none. Ak is a compound condition on two named tosses: toss k-1 must show T and toss k must show H, in that order. Ak+1 and Ak+2 are the same shape, one and two steps later.

What it is really testing. The product test again, but the events now live on windows over a sequence, and this is where independence stops being a definition to recite and becomes a question about what each event actually looks at. Everything needed is on the independence page; the multiplication rule page is the tool for pricing a compound condition on several tosses.

The traps. Order matters: tail first, then head. A tail followed by another tail earns nothing, and neither does a head followed by a head. Second, "the tosses are independent" is given, but the question asks whether the events are independent - those are different statements, and answering one with the other is the trap the problem is built around. Third, look at where the windows sit before computing anything: adjacent windows share a toss, and windows two apart share nothing. What a shared toss does to the product test is the entire question.

The sharing is the questionThe sheet put both parts in one problem so that you feel the difference between two events built on separate tosses and two events whose windows overlap. Resist deciding either part by slogan - "shared a toss, so dependent" is exactly the kind of verdict-shaped sentence that skips the justification the sheet demands. Write the demands, price the joint event, run the product test, and let each part land where it lands.

How to start. Fix k. Write Ak as two conditions on two named tosses. Write Ak+1 the same way, then Ak+2. For part (a), work out what the two events jointly demand of every toss position they touch, price the probability of the joint event, and set it against the product of the two single probabilities, each priced from p with the multiplication rule. For part (b), do the same with the two separated windows, and notice what the tosses' independence does and does not hand you for free. Stop at the point where each verdict falls out - the falling is your work.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks are printed for this problem, consistent with the practice section's not-submitted status. It is the hardest problem on this page: not because any step is technically deep, but because the justification has to be airtight in both parts, and the two parts genuinely differ.

Sheet problem 27 - one inequality, two conclusions

Restated in full. Let A and B be events such that P(A | B) > P(A). Show that P(B | A) > P(B), and that P(A | Bc) < P(A). Here P(A | B) is the conditional probability of A given B - A's share of the shrunken world B, in the picture of the conditional probability page. Bc is the complement of B: everything in the sample space outside B.

What it is really testing. Whether the conditional definition is, for you, an algebraic object and not just a ratio to plug numbers into. One hypothesis is given, and two different strict inequalities must be produced from it. The independence page is the conceptual backdrop: this hypothesis says the product test fails in one specific direction.

The traps. Writing P(A | B) at all requires P(B) > 0, and producing P(B | A) requires P(A) > 0 - say explicitly where each assumption enters, because "justify" is where this problem is graded. Every inequality in the claim is strict, so every step must preserve strictness; an argument that yields "greater than or equal" has not proved the claim. And the second conclusion lives in the world outside B - it will not fall out of the first by swapping letters, because the hypothesis says nothing directly about Bc.

How to start. Turn the hypothesis into an unconditioned statement with the conditional definition: it becomes a comparison between P(A ∩ B) and a product. Read that same comparison with the event names exchanged, and the first conclusion is in reach. For the second, split A into the part inside B and the part inside Bc - the two pieces are disjoint, so the addition rule prices A from the pieces - and ask what the hypothesis, already in hand, constrains the first piece to be. Then stop and write the two proofs up properly; that writing is the exercise.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Moderate: two claims from one hypothesis, and the rigour points above are exactly where a grader would look.
