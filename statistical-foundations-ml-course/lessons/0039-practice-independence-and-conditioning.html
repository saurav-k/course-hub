Every independence verdict is one product test, once the events are straight

working 12 min Four practice problems from the unsubmitted half of Practice Set 1, each carried to a verdict.

After this page

  • You can settle independence for a pair of events by pricing the overlap and comparing it with the product of the singles.
  • You can push the definition of independence through complements, and turn one conditional inequality into two others.

Before you start: independence, for the product test, and conditional probability, for the shrunken world.

The one-minute version

  • All four problems are decided by one move: compare the overlap's probability with the product of the singles.
  • Problem 3 runs that test three times on eight coin triples. Two pairs pass, one fails, and the failing pair fails because it cannot co-occur at all.
  • Problem 13's reward windows are two tosses wide. Adjacent windows demand H and T of the same toss, so their overlap is empty; windows two apart share no toss and pass.
  • Problem 27 is pure algebra on P(A ∩ B) > P(A) × P(B), read once with the letters swapped and once across the split of A by B.

The second is thirty practice problems, not submitted. Everything here belongs to the second half, so none of it costs marks; all of it is exam material.

What decides each of the four

Problem 3 - three events from three tosses

Three tosses give eight equally likely triples, so every probability is a count over eight. The one trap is C's wording: "at most one H" includes no H at all, which puts TTT in C. Run the test three times and two pairs pass, which is the surprise - "obviously related" is not a verdict, and neither is "obviously unrelated".

Problem 4 - complements of an independent pair

A and B cut the sample space into four tiles, and Ac ∩ Bc is the fourth one. De Morgan's law names that tile as the complement of A ∪ B, which is the whole proof: the addition rule prices the union, the hypothesis prices its overlap term, and the remainder factorises on sight.

Problem 13 - reward windows over consecutive tosses

A reward at time k needs toss k-1 to be a tail and toss k a head, so each Ak is a two-toss window and k starts at 2. Adjacent windows overlap in one toss and disagree about it; windows two apart touch nothing in common. That geometry decides both parts, but the sheet says justify, so the product test still has to be written out.

Problem 27 - one inequality, two conclusions

Clearing the denominator turns the hypothesis into P(A ∩ B) > P(A) × P(B), a statement symmetric in A and B, and the first conclusion falls straight out of reading it the other way round. The second lives outside B, so it comes from splitting A into its inside-B and outside-B pieces. Both conclusions are strict, so every step has to preserve strictness.

Practice about 35 minutes, with paper

Sheet problem 3 - three events, three tossesfoundation

A fair coin is flipped three times and the result recorded as an ordered triple of H and T, one position per toss. Let A be the event that all three tosses agree, B the event that at least one H and at least one T appear, and C the event that at most one H appears. For each of the pairs A with B, B with C, and A with C, decide whether the pair is independent.

Hint

List all eight triples once, tag each with A, B and C, and read every count off that one table. "At most one H" includes no H.

Show the worked solution
  1. The space. Eight equally likely triples: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT. Every probability is a count over 8.
  2. The three events. A = {HHH, TTT}, so P(A) = 2/8 = 1/4. B = every triple except those two, so P(B) = 6/8 = 3/4. C = triples with zero or one H = {TTT, HTT, THT, TTH}, so P(C) = 4/8 = 1/2.
  3. A with B. A and B are complements, so A ∩ B = ∅ and P(A ∩ B) = 0. The product is P(A) × P(B) = 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16. Since 0 ≠ 3/16, A and B are not independent.
  4. B with C. B ∩ C = triples with exactly one H = {HTT, THT, TTH}, so P(B ∩ C) = 3/8. The product is 3/4 × 1/2 = 3/8. Equal, so B and C are independent.
  5. A with C. A ∩ C = {TTT}, the only all-same triple with at most one H, so P(A ∩ C) = 1/8. The product is 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. Equal, so A and C are independent.

Sanity check. C splits as {TTT} plus the three exactly-one-H triples, so P(A ∩ C) + P(B ∩ C) = 1/8 + 3/8 = 1/2 = P(C) - the two pieces exhaust C, because A and B are complements. If your two overlap counts do not add back to 4 out of 8, one of them is wrong.

Sheet problem 4 - complements stay independentfoundation

Show that if two events A and B are independent, then Ac and Bc are also independent. Here Ac is everything in the sample space outside A, and Bc is defined the same way for B.

Hint

Ac ∩ Bc is the complement of A ∪ B. Price that union with the addition rule and the rest factorises.

Show the worked solution
  1. What is given. P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
  2. Rewrite the target set. By De Morgan's law, Ac ∩ Bc = (A ∪ B)c. So P(Ac ∩ Bc) = 1 - P(A ∪ B).
  3. Price the union. The addition rule gives P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B), and the hypothesis replaces the last term: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A) × P(B).
  4. Substitute and factorise. P(Ac ∩ Bc) = 1 - P(A) - P(B) + P(A) × P(B) = (1 - P(A)) × (1 - P(B)).
  5. Name the factors. 1 - P(A) = P(Ac) and 1 - P(B) = P(Bc), so P(Ac ∩ Bc) = P(Ac) × P(Bc), which is the product test for the complemented pair. Independent.

Sanity check. Test it on P(A) = P(B) = 1/2 independent: the four tiles each carry 1/4, so P(Ac ∩ Bc) = 1/4 and P(Ac) × P(Bc) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. The step that must not be skipped is line 3 - without the hypothesis the factorisation in line 4 does not close.

Sheet problem 13 - reward windowsfrontier

A coin is tossed independently n times, with probability p of heads on every toss. At each time k, for k = 2 to n, a reward is obtained if toss k is a head and toss k-1 was a tail; Ak is the event that a reward is obtained at time k. (a) Are Ak and Ak+1 independent? (b) Are Ak and Ak+2 independent? Justify all answers.

Hint

Write each event as a demand on named toss positions, then ask whether the two demands can be met at the same time.

Show the worked solution
  1. Price one event. Ak demands toss k-1 = T and toss k = H. Two distinct tosses, independent, so P(Ak) = (1 - p) × p. The same for Ak+1 and Ak+2.
  2. (a) The joint demand. Ak needs toss k = H. Ak+1 needs toss k = T. No outcome satisfies both, so Ak ∩ Ak+1 = ∅ and P(Ak ∩ Ak+1) = 0.
  3. (a) The product. P(Ak) × P(Ak+1) = p2(1 - p)2, which is strictly positive whenever 0 < p < 1. Since 0 ≠ p2(1 - p)2, Ak and Ak+1 are not independent. (If p is 0 or 1 both sides are 0 and the test passes trivially; the interesting case is the strict one.)
  4. (b) The joint demand. Ak constrains tosses k-1 and k; Ak+2 constrains tosses k+1 and k+2. Four distinct positions, no shared toss, so the joint event is toss k-1 = T, k = H, k+1 = T, k+2 = H.
  5. (b) Price it. The tosses are independent, so P(Ak ∩ Ak+2) = (1 - p) × p × (1 - p) × p = p2(1 - p)2, which is exactly P(Ak) × P(Ak+2). Ak and Ak+2 are independent.

Sanity check. Put p = 1/2. Each single event has probability 1/4. Part (a): the overlap is 0 against a product of 1/16, so the events are strongly negatively related, as they must be when one forbids the other. Part (b): the overlap is 1/16 = 1/4 × 1/4 exactly. Independent tosses do not make events independent; disjoint windows do.

Sheet problem 27 - one hypothesis, two conclusionsworking

Let A and B be events with P(A | B) > P(A). Show that P(B | A) > P(B), and that P(A | Bc) < P(A).

Hint

Clear the denominator first. The resulting inequality is symmetric in A and B, and A splits into its part inside B and its part outside.

Show the worked solution
  1. Clear the denominator. Writing P(A | B) requires P(B) > 0. Multiplying the hypothesis P(A ∩ B) / P(B) > P(A) by P(B) > 0 preserves the strict sign: P(A ∩ B) > P(A) × P(B). Call this (★).
  2. Both denominators are safe. (★) forces P(A ∩ B) > 0, hence P(A) > 0. And dividing (★) by P(A) gives P(B) < P(A ∩ B) / P(A) ≤ 1, so P(B) < 1 and P(Bc) > 0. Nothing extra needs assuming.
  3. First conclusion. P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A), and by (★) the numerator exceeds P(A) × P(B). So P(B | A) > P(A) × P(B) / P(A) = P(B). Done.
  4. Split A by B. A ∩ B and A ∩ Bc are disjoint and their union is A, so the addition rule gives P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) - P(A ∩ B).
  5. Apply (★) to that piece. Subtracting a larger quantity leaves a smaller one: P(A ∩ Bc) < P(A) - P(A) × P(B) = P(A) × (1 - P(B)) = P(A) × P(Bc).
  6. Second conclusion. Divide by P(Bc) > 0, safe by line 2: P(A | Bc) = P(A ∩ Bc) / P(Bc) < P(A). Done.

Sanity check. Take a fair die, A = {1, 2} and B = {1, 2, 3}. Then P(A | B) = 2/3 > 1/3 = P(A), and the two conclusions read P(B | A) = 1 > 1/2 = P(B), and P(A | Bc) = 0 < 1/3. Both hold, and both are strict - if your argument only delivered "greater than or equal", the step that lost strictness is a multiplication or division by something you did not prove positive.

Carry this away

  • Independence is one equation, never a feeling: price the overlap, price the product, compare. Two of problem 3's three pairs pass, and one of those pairs looks related.
  • Disjointness is the opposite of independence, not a version of it. That is the whole of problem 3's first row and of problem 13's part (a).
  • Independent tosses do not hand you independent events. Events built on disjoint sets of tosses inherit it; events sharing a toss have to be tested.
