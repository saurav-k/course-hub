Choosing a distribution means checking its support first, and only then arguing about its shape

working 11 min Lecture 7, IIT Bombay, delivered 5 September 2026. The last half hour of the session, where the lecturer was asked what an exam would actually ask.

After this page

  • You can rule a candidate distribution out on its support alone, before drawing anything.
  • You can fit an exponential to data by matching the sample average.

Before you start: The uniform, the exponential and the Gaussian. This page compares them and introduces none.

The one-minute version

  • A student asked what an exam would look like. The lecturer answered with a modelling question and then solved it out loud.
  • Is the exponential a good model for daily profit or loss? No. Profit and loss can be negative and the exponential lives only on x ≥ 0.
  • That is the method: check the support first, then the shape. Support is a yes or no and it settles the question before any curve is drawn.
  • Fitting comes last. The exponential's mean is 1/λ, so the sample average fixes the parameter in one step.
  • Nothing here has to be memorised. A formula sheet is provided. What is required is knowing what a density means and how to turn an event into an integral.

1. The lecturer's own worked example

Asked what kind of question might come up, the lecturer built one on the spot. Suppose I am plotting the curve for profit or loss on a particular day. Is the exponential distribution a good model?

He answered it in one sentence. Profit and loss means you can have positive values and negative values, but the exponential distribution only has positive values, so it is not a good model.

Notice what that argument did not need. No mean, no variance, no curve, no data. It compared two sets: the values the quantity can take, and the values the family allows.

fX(x) = λ e−λx for x ≥ 0,  and 0 otherwise Read as: fX(x) is the density of the exponential random variable X at the point x. λ, lambda, is its one parameter, a positive rate. e is the base of the natural logarithm, and e−λx shrinks as x grows. The two cases are the whole point here: the second line says the density is exactly 0 for every negative x, so the family assigns those values no probability whatever. That set of allowed values is the family's support.

2. The two checks, in order

Support first, because it is cheap and it is decisive. Shape second, because it only ever chooses between the families support has already let through.

The last row is not a joke. Part 4 built a complete model out of six click positions and six numbers that summed to one, and no famous name was involved. A quantity whose support is a short list you can write down needs no family at all.

Support rules a family out. It never rules one in. Clearing both support rows and the shape row means only that nothing has contradicted the family yet. A handful of observations cannot identify a distribution family, and this course has said so before, on the Lecture 1 spending data. The lecturer was equally careful about it: if the question is open ended, he said, then it is open ended, and any reasonable answer is graded as a good answer. Treat a passed check as a licence to proceed, not as evidence you are right.

3. Fitting comes last, and it is one line

A student asked how you would actually choose lambda. The lecturer's answer was the shortest thing in the session: the mean of an exponential is one over lambda, so take the average of your dataset and map it to one over lambda-hat.

x̄ = 1 / λ̂,  so  λ̂ = 1 / x̄ Read as: x̄, x-bar, is the plain average of the values you observed, added up and divided by how many there are. λ̂, lambda-hat, is the fitted value of the exponential's rate parameter, and the hat is what marks it as an estimate from data rather than a number that was given to you. The equation says: set the family's mean equal to the data's average, then solve for the parameter.
One deferred word. "Mean" is used here exactly as the lecturer used it, as the average value the family produces in the long run. Expectation has not been defined yet, and this page does not define it. All that is being borrowed is his stated fact that an exponential with rate lambda has mean 1 over lambda.

Run it on the loan question from Part 10. Suppose the observed daily spends average 800 rupees, and we want the chance a day exceeds 2,000.

  1. Take the average. The observed daily spends average x̄ = 800 rupees.
  2. Map it to the parameter. Set 1 / λ̂ = 800, so λ̂ = 1 ÷ 800 = 0.00125 per rupee.
  3. Write the tail. The exponential gives P(X > 2000) = e−λ · 2000, which is the class notes' own expression from page 5.
  4. Put the fitted rate in. The exponent is −0.00125 × 2000 = −2.5.
  5. Evaluate. e−2.5 = 0.0821, so about 8.2% of days go over 2,000 rupees under this fitted model.
This course's arithmeticThe class notes stop at the expression e−λ · 2000 and state no rate and no decimal. The average of 800 rupees is this course's own illustrative figure, and 0.00125 and 0.0821 follow from it. Neither carries a key-number mark.

4. What the assessment actually asks for

Two students asked whether these distributions have to be memorised. The answer was no, twice over, and it is worth quoting because it redraws what studying this material means.

You will be given a sheet with all the distributions listed. You do not have to memorise any expressions. The density will be provided to you.

What is required instead is four things, and the lecturer listed them in a single breath.

For the Gaussian he added the one practical consequence. Since its CDF has no closed form, numerical values would be provided and the work would be the computation done with them.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the exponential ruled out as a model for daily profit or loss?
The argument is set containment and nothing else: profit and loss takes negative values, the exponential's density is zero for every negative x, so the family cannot represent half the quantity. Steepness is a shape argument and shape is never reached, because support has already decided. Its parameter fits perfectly easily from an average. And memorylessness is a true property of the exponential that answers a different question entirely, which is what makes it the strongest distractor here.
The lecturer said no distribution has to be memorised. What does he require instead?
He named four skills and this is the operative one: read an event in words, then write the integral of the density that computes it. Normalisation is a property every density has and was never set as a task. The Gaussian cumulative function has no closed form at all, which is why numerical values would be supplied. And the memorylessness derivation was done at the board as an illustration, not set as something to reproduce.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Four quantities, one candidate family each. Run the support check on every pair, and only where support passes, say whether the shape is plausible.

Judge each pair, in one sentence, naming which check decided it.

  • Time until the next support ticket arrives, modelled as exponential.
  • The change in a server's daily latency against yesterday, modelled as exponential.
  • A random number the scheduler draws between 0 and 1, modelled as uniform.
  • The reading error of a thermometer, modelled as Gaussian.
Hint

For each pair write down two sets before you write a sentence: the values the quantity can take, and the values the family allows. Only one of the four fails on the first comparison.

Show the worked solution
  1. Waiting time, exponential. Support passes: a waiting time is never negative. Shape is plausible too, since short gaps are commoner than long ones. Defensible.
  2. Change in latency, exponential. Support fails: latency can fall as well as rise, so the quantity is two-sided and the family is not. Ruled out on support, exactly as profit and loss was.
  3. A number drawn between 0 and 1, uniform. Support passes, since the draw is inside the interval by construction. Shape passes by the same construction: the generator is built to make every value equally likely. Defensible.
  4. Reading error, Gaussian. Support passes both ways: an error can be positive or negative, and so can the family. Shape is plausible, since small errors should outnumber large ones and neither direction is favoured. Defensible, and it is the noise case from Part 12.

Sanity check. Exactly one of the four should fail, and it should fail on support rather than on shape. If you ruled out more than one, check whether you rejected something on shape alone, which is a weaker argument than it feels.

Carry this away

  • Compare the set of values the quantity can take against the set the family allows. If they disagree, stop there.
  • Shape only chooses among the families support has already let through, and passing both checks is not evidence the family is right.
  • Fitting is the last step, and for the exponential it is one line: the sample average estimates 1 over lambda.
  • The formulas are supplied. The judgement, and the translation from an event to an integral, are not.

Your teacher. The habit worth drilling is the cheap one. Before any modelling conversation, write the quantity's range on one line and the candidate family's support underneath it. If a colleague reaches for a Gaussian, ask what the quantity does at zero. If someone reaches for an exponential, ask whether the quantity can ever be negative. Those two questions cost nothing and settle most of these arguments before anybody opens a notebook.
