Lecture 7 · Part 9 of 13 · the first named density

A flat density turns "equally likely" into a straight-line CDF you can read off

working 11 min The uniform delivered live, with the two applications the lecturer named and the one reading a student worked on the spot.

After this page

  • You can write the uniform density and its CDF from the interval alone, and say why each constant is forced.
  • You can read a CDF value back as a sentence about how often something happens.
  • You can tell when "every value here is equally likely" is a real claim and when it is an excuse.

Before you start: A density is not a probability, for the fact that only area is probability.

The one-minute version

  • The uniform on [a, b] models a number picked at random from an interval with no part of it favoured.
  • Its density is 1/(b − a) inside and 0 outside. The height is forced: a rectangle of that width has to enclose area 1.
  • Its CDF is (x − a)/(b − a) between a and b, clipped to 0 below a and 1 above b: a straight ramp between two flat pieces.
  • A bus every 10 minutes and a random arrival gives a wait that is U[0, 10], and the CDF at 3 reads 0.30.
  • What it costs: "equally likely" is a strong claim, and the ramp has two corners where no slope exists.

1. The height is forced, not chosen

The lecturer introduced the uniform as the model for "choosing a random number in the interval A to B", where every part of the interval is treated alike. Once that is the claim, the density has no freedom left in it.

fX(x) = 1/(b − a) for a ≤ x < b, and 0 otherwise X is the random quantity. fX is its density, the height of the curve at each point. a and b are the two ends of the interval, with a below b. b − a is the width of the interval. The whole line reads as two cases: inside the interval the height is one over the width, and everywhere outside it the height is zero.
  1. Draw the only shape allowed. The density is one constant height h across a width of b − a and zero elsewhere, so the region under it is a rectangle.
  2. Write its area. A rectangle of width b − a and height h has area h(b − a).
  3. Impose the one rule every density obeys. The total area must be exactly 1, so h(b − a) = 1.
  4. Solve for the height. h = 1/(b − a). Nobody picked it. It is what the width leaves.

With a = 0 and b = 10, the height is 0.1. Narrow the interval and the height rises: on a width of half a unit the density is 2, which is legal because a height is not a probability. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis derive the same constant the same way in Example 3.1 of Introduction to Probability.

2. The CDF, and why the integral starts at a

The cumulative function collects area from the far left up to a threshold. Written out, that is an integral running from minus infinity, and a student asked in the session why the lecturer then wrote the lower limit as a instead.

FX(x) = ∫ fX(z) dz, from −∞ up to x FX(x) is the cumulative distribution function at the threshold x, and it means the probability that X lands at or below x. z is a dummy name for the position being swept, so that x can stay the threshold. The integral sign is a running total of area, taken from the far left of the line up to x.
  1. Split the sweep at a. The integral from minus infinity to x is the piece from minus infinity to a plus the piece from a to x.
  2. Kill the first piece. The density is zero everywhere below a, so that piece contributes exactly zero area. This is the student's answer: the lower limit is not being changed, the part below a simply adds nothing.
  3. Integrate a constant. From a to x the density is the fixed number 1/(b − a), so the area is the width x − a times that height.
  4. Read the result. FX(x) = (x − a)/(b − a) for x between a and b.
  5. Check the two ends. Put x = a and it gives 0. Put x = b and it gives 1. In between it is a straight line, because the numerator grows at a fixed rate.
  6. Clip the outside. Below a the value is 0, since X never lands there. Above b it is 1, and stays 1 for ever: the lecturer confirmed to a student that the probability of X being at most anything larger than b is still 1.

That derivation is not repeated here. What the live session added is the part a written note cannot carry: two named applications, the student's question about the lower limit of the integral, and the sentence that turns the number 0.30 back into how often you wait.

3. The bus you just missed

The lecturer's first application: buses are scheduled every 10 minutes, at 10:00, at 10:10, and so on, and you arrive at a moment you did not plan. Your wait is the distance from where you landed to the next scheduled bus, and if no moment in the gap is favoured, that wait is uniform on 0 to 10 minutes.

A student worked the value live. With a = 0 and b = 10, a threshold of three minutes gives (3 − 0)/(10 − 0) = 0.3, and he asked what that number actually means. The lecturer's answer is the sentence worth keeping: 30% of the time you will wait less than three minutes. Not three tenths of a minute, and not a rate of anything. A CDF value is always the share of occasions on which the quantity comes in at or below the threshold.

4. The second use: starting a neural network somewhere

The lecturer's other application comes from machine learning. Before a network is trained, its weights have to hold something, and a common choice is to draw each one from the uniform on minus one to one. The uniform is doing an honest job there: no particular starting value is preferred, and an interval is the whole of what is being claimed. Training then moves the weights; the uniform only decides where they stand at step zero.

5. What the shape costs

"Every value equally likely" is a strong statement, and most real quantities do not obey it. A bus wait is uniform only while the timetable is exact and your arrival is genuinely unplanned; a commuter who checks the timetable does not arrive uniformly, and a delayed bus stretches the gap the model assumed was fixed. When you cannot defend the flat top, you are choosing a different shape, and the next page is one of them.

Two corners, and a claim that is easy to make and hard to earnThe ramp is not differentiable at a or at b: to the left of a it is flat and to the right it climbs, so no single slope exists there. A student raised exactly this in the session and the lecturer agreed, adding that such points are few and carry no mass. That is the "differentiable almost everywhere" clause from the definition of a continuous variable arriving on the first example. The larger cost is the modelling claim itself. A uniform says you know the range and nothing else about the inside of it, so reaching for it because a quantity has bounds, rather than because the inside really is featureless, hides an assumption in a formula that looks like a fact.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Buses run every 12 minutes and you arrive unplanned. What is the chance you wait at most 4 minutes?
The wait is uniform on 0 to 12, so the CDF at 4 is (4 - 0)/(12 - 0) = 1/3. The first option divides by the wrong quantity and would be the answer for a one-minute threshold. The second confuses the threshold with a probability: 4 cannot be a probability at all, since probabilities never exceed 1. The third is arithmetic by eye rather than by division, and four twelfths is a third, not a half.
Why is the uniform density on an interval of width one half equal to 2, which is above 1?
Only area is probability, so a tall narrow rectangle of area one is perfectly legal, and here 2 times 0.5 is 1. The first option applies the ceiling for probabilities to a quantity that is not one. The third gives a single point positive mass, which no continuous variable has. The fourth invents mass at the endpoints, and the endpoints of a continuous variable each carry probability zero like every other point.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A batch job is launched at some unplanned moment inside a maintenance window that runs from minute 20 to minute 50 of every hour. Model the launch minute as uniform on that window.

Find the density height, the probability the job starts by minute 35, and the probability it starts after minute 44.

Hint

Everything comes from the width of the window and the distance of the threshold from its left end. For the last part, remember that a CDF answers "at most", so the complement is one minus it.

Show the worked solution
  1. Get the width. The window runs from a = 20 to b = 50, so b - a = 30 minutes.
  2. Get the height. 1 divided by 30, which is about 0.0333 per minute.
  3. Apply the CDF at 35. (35 - 20)/30 = 15/30 = 0.5. Minute 35 is the midpoint, so half is what it must be.
  4. Apply the CDF at 44. (44 - 20)/30 = 24/30 = 0.8, which is the chance of starting by minute 44.
  5. Take the complement. Starting after minute 44 is 1 - 0.8 = 0.2.

Sanity check. Minute 44 leaves 6 of the 30 minutes above it, and 6/30 is 0.2, which agrees. Both probabilities sit between 0 and 1, and the height 0.0333 is below 1 only because the window is wider than one unit.

Carry this away

  • The uniform's height is one over the width, because a rectangle of that width has to enclose area exactly 1.
  • Its CDF is the fraction of the interval you have passed, clipped to 0 below the interval and 1 above it.
  • A CDF value is a share of occasions: 0.30 at a threshold of three minutes means three arrivals in ten wait under three minutes.
  • The cost is the flat top itself, which says every value in the range is equally likely and is often more than you can defend.
