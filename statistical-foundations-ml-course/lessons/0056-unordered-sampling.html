Lecture 4 · Part 11 of 12 · sampling cases 3 and 4

Throwing away the order divides by the orderings, until repeats make that impossible

11 min frontier Three of the four sampling cases fall out of the multiplication rule. The fourth needs a different picture entirely, and the lecture draws it.

The one-minute version

  • Case 3, without replacement, without ordering: take the ordered count and divide by the n! ways each collection could have been ordered. The result is C(m, n) = m! / (n! (m−n)!). The lecture's example is 2 positions from 4 candidates, giving 6.
  • Case 4, with replacement, without ordering: that division no longer works, because a collection with repeats has fewer distinct orderings than one without. The lecture counts the outcome differently, as a vector of counts.
  • An outcome in case 4 is {n1, n2, ..., nm} with every ni at least zero and the whole lot summing to n. The number of such vectors is C(n + m − 1, m − 1).
  • The lecture's example is popcorn: 2 add-ons chosen from 3 flavours, giving 6, and the notes draw the stars-and-bars picture beside the formula.

1. What "throwing away the order" does

Part 10 counted sequences. This page counts collections. The move from one to the other is a grouping: several sequences describe the same collection, so the count goes down by however many sequences each collection accounts for.

For draws without replacement, every item in the collection is distinct, so a collection of n items can be written down in exactly n! different orders, and every collection accounts for the same number of sequences. Dividing is therefore legal.

  1. Start from case 2. The ordered count without replacement is m! / (m−n)!.
  2. Ask how many sequences make one collection. The n chosen items are all different, so they can be arranged in n × (n−1) × ... × 1 = n! ways, and each of those arrangements is a separate sequence in case 2.
  3. Divide. The number of collections is [m! / (m−n)!] ÷ n! = m! / (n! (m−n)!), written C(m, n) and read "m choose n".
C(m, n) = m! / ( n! × (m−n)! ) m is the basket size and n the number drawn, with m ≥ n as in case 2. The three factorials do three jobs: m! counts every arrangement of the whole basket, (m−n)! cancels the arrangements of the items left behind, and n! cancels the arrangements of the items chosen, which is exactly the ordering being discarded. This quantity is the binomial coefficient, already met as the pair count in Lecture 3, Part 2.

The lecture's example: two positions to fill from four candidates (A, B, C, D), where the two positions are interchangeable. The board lists all six collections: {A,B}, {B,C}, {C,D}, {A,C}, {B,D}, {A,D}, and confirms them with C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! × 2!) = 6.

2. Why the same division fails once the ball goes back

Now allow repeats. The tempting move is to take case 1's count mn and divide by n!. It does not work, and it is worth seeing exactly why rather than being told.

Take the two-song basket from Part 10: m = 2 songs, n = 2 draws, with replacement. Case 1 gives 22 = 4 ordered outcomes: (S1,S1), (S1,S2), (S2,S1), (S2,S2). Now group them by collection:

CollectionOrdered sequences that produce itHow many
{S1, S1}(S1, S1)1
{S1, S2}(S1, S2) and (S2, S1)2
{S2, S2}(S2, S2)1

Three collections, and the groups are of unequal size: 1, 2, 1. Dividing 4 by 2! = 2 would give 2, which is wrong, and no single divisor could be right because the groups are not all the same size. A collection with a repeat in it simply has fewer distinct orderings than one without.

So case 4 is not case 1 with a correction. It needs the outcome to be described differently, and the lecture describes it as a vector of counts.

3. Case 4: count the items, do not list them

{n1, n2, ..., nm} with ni ≥ 0 and ∑i=1..m ni = n One entry per item in the basket, so the vector has length m, not n. The entry ni says how many times item i was drawn, and it may be zero. The constraint says the counts add up to the number of draws. Two draws from three flavours where both are chocolate is the vector (2, 0, 0); one chocolate and one salt is (1, 0, 1). Order has vanished completely, because a vector of counts has nowhere to record it.

Counting those vectors is what the stars-and-bars picture does, and the notes draw it beside the formula. Lay out the n draws as n stars in a row. Insert m − 1 bars to divide the row into m compartments, one per basket item. Whatever falls in compartment i is that item's count.

The row now holds n stars and m − 1 bars, so it has n + m − 1 positions in all. Choosing an arrangement means choosing which of those positions hold the bars, which is a case 3 problem: pick m − 1 positions out of n + m − 1.

C(n + m − 1, m − 1) = (n + m − 1)! / ( (m−1)! × n! ) The top argument is the total number of positions in the row, stars and bars together. The bottom argument is how many of them are bars. The factorial form on the right is the case 3 formula with those two arguments substituted in, and the two factorials underneath are the bars and the stars respectively.

The lecture's example is popcorn. Two add-ons are chosen from three flavours {Ch, Ca, Sa}, so n = 2 and m = 3, and repeats are allowed because both add-ons may be the same flavour.

  1. Fill in the formula. n + m − 1 = 2 + 3 − 1 = 4, and m − 1 = 2, so the count is C(4, 2).
  2. Evaluate it. C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! × 2!) = 24 / 4 = 6.
  3. List them and check. The six orders are {Sa, Ca}, {Ch, Ch}, {Ch, Ca}, {Ch, Sa}, {Sa, Sa}, {Ca, Ca}, exactly as the board lists them. Three of them repeat a flavour and three mix two, and there are six.

The notes underline the point the whole case turns on: {Ch, Sa} and (Sa, Ch) are the same outcome here, and the board marks the ordered version with a cross. Case 4 counts orders of popcorn, not sequences of decisions.

A note on the two argumentsSome books write this count as C(n + m − 1, n) instead. It is the same number: choosing where the m − 1 bars go and choosing where the n stars go are two descriptions of one arrangement, so the two coefficients are equal. In the lecture's own example both readings give C(4, 2), because n and m − 1 both happen to be 2. This equivalence is this course's note; the lecture states the bars form.
A count is not a probability, and case 4 is where that bitesThe C(n + m − 1, m − 1) multisets are counted correctly by this formula, and they are not equally likely, so nothing here licenses dividing over them. The grouping table in section 2 is the proof: with two songs drawn twice, the three multisets carry 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4 rather than a third each, because a collection with a repeat corresponds to fewer ordered sequences than one without. Lecture 5, Part 6 settles this in full, after a question raised against exactly this box.

The two examples both come out at six, which is a coincidence of the numbers the lecture chose and not a rule. The two formulas are genuinely different: with m = 4 and n = 2, case 3 gives C(4, 2) = 6 while case 4 gives C(5, 3) = 10, and the gap is the four collections that repeat an item.

5. Check yourself

Why can case 3 divide the ordered count by n! while case 4 cannot?
In case 3 every collection has exactly n! orderings, so one divisor serves all of them; in case 4 a collection with a repeat has fewer, the groups are unequal, and no single divisor is correct. That n may exceed m in case 4 is true but is a consequence of replacement rather than the reason the division fails. How fast a factorial grows is irrelevant to whether a division is valid. And the vector description is the repair that follows from the failure, not the cause of it.
Three scoops are chosen from five flavours, repeats allowed and order irrelevant. Which count applies?
Repeats allowed and order irrelevant is case 4, so with n = 3 and m = 5 the count is C(3 + 5 - 1, 5 - 1) = C(7, 4) = 35. C(5, 3) = 10 is case 3 and would be right only if a flavour could not be repeated. The 5 cubed option is case 1 and keeps the order, counting the same scoops in different sequences separately. And 5 factorial over 2 factorial is case 2, ordered and without replacement, which gets both switches wrong.
Your teacher. Case 4 is the one that will not stick from reading, so draw it. Take three flavours and two scoops, put two stars on a line, and physically move the two bars through all six positions, writing the order beside each. Five minutes of that installs the picture permanently, and the picture is what you will actually recall under time pressure - the formula is only its shorthand.
