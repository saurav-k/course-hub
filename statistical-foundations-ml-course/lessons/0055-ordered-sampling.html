Lecture 4 · Part 10 of 12 · sampling cases 1 and 2

When order counts, the only question left is whether the ball goes back

10 min two formulas A basket of m balls, n draws, and the first of the lecture's two binary choices settled. Both cases are the basic counting rule with different stage counts.

The one-minute version

  • The set-up for the whole sampling section: a basket holds m distinguishable balls and n of them are drawn. Two yes-or-no questions give four cases; this page settles the two where order matters.
  • Order mattering means (Blue, Red) is a different outcome from (Red, Blue). The draw sequence is part of what is being counted.
  • Case 1, with replacement: every draw sees the full basket, so the count is m × m × ... × m = mn. A playlist of 3 songs drawn twice gives 3 × 3 = 9 ordered pairs.
  • Case 2, without replacement: the basket shrinks by one each time, so the count is m × (m−1) × ... × (m−(n−1)) = m! / (m−n)!, and it needs m ≥ n. Three candidates for two distinct posts gives 6.

1. The set-up the whole section shares

The lecture frames every sampling formula the same way, and it is worth fixing the picture once so the four cases can be told apart by their two switches alone.

  • m is how many distinguishable items are in the basket. Distinguishable matters: two balls of the same colour that cannot be told apart are one item, not two.
  • n is how many draws are made.
  • Switch one: does ordering matter? If yes, the outcome is a sequence and (Blue, Red) differs from (Red, Blue). If no, the outcome is a collection and the two are the same thing.
  • Switch two: with or without replacement? With replacement, the drawn ball goes back before the next draw, so it can come out again. Without replacement, it stays out.

2. Case 1: with ordering, with replacement

Each draw is a stage, and every stage offers the full basket because whatever came out last time went straight back. So all n stages offer m options.

m × m × ... × m = mn There are n factors, one per draw, each equal to m. Writing it as a power is shorthand for the product, not a new idea. Note that n may exceed m here: drawing ten times from a basket of three is perfectly legal when the ball goes back.

The lecture's example is a playlist. Three songs, (S1, S2, S3). Choose a song, hit shuffle, and get another song, which may be the same one again. The outcomes named on the board include (S1, S1), (S1, S2), (S1, S3) and (S3, S1), and the count is 3 × 3 = 9.

The notes also sketch the smallest version: a basket of two balls, one blue and one red, drawn twice with replacement. The four outcomes are (B, B), (B, R), (R, R) and (R, B), and 22 = 4.

3. Case 2: with ordering, without replacement

Now the ball stays out. The first draw sees m balls, the second sees m − 1, and by the time the n-th draw happens, n − 1 balls have already gone.

m × (m−1) × (m−2) × ... × (m − (n−1)) = m! / (m−n)! There are still n factors, but each is one smaller than the last. The exclamation mark is the factorial: m! means m × (m−1) × ... × 2 × 1. Dividing m! by (m−n)! cancels every factor below the n-th, leaving exactly the n falling factors on the left. This case requires m ≥ n: you cannot draw more balls than the basket holds when none go back.

The lecture's example: three candidates (A, B, C) for two distinct positions, so m = 3 and n = 2. The board lists all six outcomes: (C,B), (C,A), (B,A), (A,B), (A,C), (B,C).

  1. Count the stages directly. The first post can be filled 3 ways. Whoever takes it is now unavailable, so the second post can be filled 2 ways. 3 × 2 = 6.
  2. Check against the factorial form. m! / (m−n)! = 3! / 1! = 6 / 1 = 6. The two agree, as they must.
  3. See it as the grid above, minus the diagonal. With replacement there were 9 ordered pairs. Three of them repeat a name, and a person cannot hold both posts, so those three are struck out: 9 − 3 = 6.

The word distinct in "two distinct positions" is what keeps this in the ordered half of the table. Chief architect and deputy are different jobs, so (A, B) and (B, A) are different appointments. If the two posts were interchangeable, the same six outcomes would collapse into three, which is Part 11.

4. The two counts side by side

CaseFormulaConditionThe lecture's exampleCount
1. Ordered, with replacement mn none - n may exceed m 3 songs, 2 draws 9
2. Ordered, without replacement m! / (m−n)! m ≥ n 3 candidates, 2 posts 6

Case 2 is always the smaller of the two, for the reason the grid picture shows: it is case 1 with the repeated-item outcomes removed. When m is much larger than n the two counts come close, because repeats are rare; when n approaches m they separate sharply.

5. Check yourself

A four-digit PIN is drawn from the ten digits. Which count applies, and what is it?
Digits may repeat in a PIN, so every position sees all ten digits and the count is 10 to the fourth, which is 10,000. The first option is case 2, correct only for a PIN forbidden to repeat a digit. The third is case 3, which throws the order away and would count 1234 and 4321 as the same PIN. And the last swaps m and n, which counts sequences of ten choices from four options.
Why does case 2 require m to be at least as large as n, while case 1 does not?
Without replacement the basket loses a ball per draw, so once n exceeds m one draw faces an empty basket and there is no outcome to count. The factorial of a negative argument being undefined is a symptom of that in the notation, not the reason. Ordering is perfectly meaningful with repeats, which is exactly what case 1 counts. And the growth rates differ for a different reason and say nothing about whether the count exists at all.
Your teacher. Before reaching for a formula, say the experiment aloud in the basket language: how many things are in the basket, how many draws, does the ball go back, and does the order of the draws show up in the answer. Four short answers, then the formula is forced. Almost every counting error that survives to a final answer was made before any arithmetic happened, in one of those four sentences.
