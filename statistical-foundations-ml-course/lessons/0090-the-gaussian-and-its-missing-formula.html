Lecture 6 · Part 13 of 14 · the Gaussian

The most used distribution in statistics has a cumulative function nobody can write down

working 12 min Two parameters, one density, and one honest admission the notes make in four words.

The one-minute version

  • The Gaussian, also called the normal, is parameterised by (μ, σ) with μ any real number and σ > 0.
  • Its density is fX(x) = 1/(σ√(2π)) exp(−(x − μ)2 / (2σ2)), and its support is the whole real line: every value is possible, however far out.
  • μ says where the bell sits and σ says how wide it is. The notes draw the bell with a dashed line at μ through its peak.
  • Setting μ = 0 and σ = 1 gives the standard normal distribution, which the notes name in the margin.
  • Then the admission: the CDF does not have a closed form expression and is computed numerically. That is not a footnote, it is the reason statistical tables and library functions exist at all.

1. The density, symbol by symbol

This is the longest formula in the lecture and it is worth taking apart piece by piece before looking at it whole.

fX(x) = 1/(σ√(2π)) · exp(−(x − μ)2 / (2σ2)) μ, mu, is the first parameter and may be any real number, positive, negative or zero. σ, sigma, is the second and must be strictly positive. x − μ is how far the point being priced is from mu, and squaring it makes the formula treat both directions alike, which is why the bell is symmetric. 2σ2 in the denominator sets the scale of that distance: a larger sigma makes the same gap count for less. exp(−...) is e raised to a negative number, so the whole thing shrinks as x moves away from mu, and shrinks fast because the exponent grows with the square of the distance. 1/(σ√(2π)) is the constant in front, doing the same job the leading lambda did for the exponential in Part 12: it is what makes the total area exactly one.

Two structural facts follow just from reading it, with no calculation.

  • The support is all of R. The exponential of a negative number is never zero, so the density is strictly positive at every real x. A Gaussian variable can in principle land anywhere, which is a genuine difference from every other shape in this lecture.
  • The bell is symmetric about μ. Only (x − μ)2 appears, and that is unchanged when x moves the same distance the other way, so the height at μ + d equals the height at μ − d for every d.

2. What sigma does

The parameter mu is easy: it slides the whole picture sideways and changes nothing else. Sigma is the one worth drawing, because it changes two things at once and they are forced to move together.

3. The standard normal

The notes write in the margin: μ = 0, σ = 1, and beneath it, standard Normal dist. Substituting those two values collapses the formula to its simplest form.

fX(x) = 1/√(2π) · exp(−x2 / 2) With μ = 0 the gap x − μ is just x. With σ = 1 the leading constant loses its sigma and the denominator in the exponent becomes 2. Nothing else changes, so the standard normal is the same shape centred at zero and measured in units of one.

It matters because of the second point in section 2. Since sigma is a distance scale, a question about any Gaussian can be re-expressed as a question about the standard one by measuring the gap from mu in units of sigma. That is why a single table or a single library function serves every Gaussian there is, and it is what makes the numerical computation in section 4 a finite job rather than an endless one.

4. The CDF that cannot be written down

Here is the sentence the notes write, in full: CDF doesn't have closed form expression. Computed numerically.

Take that seriously rather than reading past it. Every other shape in this lecture handed its CDF over on request. The uniform's was a ramp. The exponential's was 1 − e−λx. Every discrete shape's was a finite sum. The Gaussian's is ∫−∞x fX(z) dz and there it stays: no combination of the standard functions equals it.

The notes assert this and do not justify itThat an integral has no closed form in terms of elementary functions is a genuine theorem and a hard one, and the notes state the fact without any gesture at why. This course states it the same way and offers no argument, because constructing one would take the course into ground its own MISSION.md puts out of scope. What is worth stressing is what the claim does and does not say. It does not say the integral is unknown, uncomputable or approximate in some troubling sense. The values are known to any precision anybody has ever needed. What is missing is a short algebraic expression for them.

5. Check yourself

A Gaussian has sigma equal to 0.5 rather than 1. What happens to its peak height?
The leading constant is one over sigma root two pi, so halving sigma doubles it, and the reason it must is that the total area is pinned at one however narrow the bell gets. Scaling down with sigma is the wrong direction: a narrower shape enclosing the same area has to be taller, not shorter. Mu slides the curve sideways and never changes its height. And sigma squared appears only in the exponent, where it sets the distance scale, while the height is governed by the single sigma in front.
What does the notes' remark about the CDF having no closed form actually mean?
The claim is about writability: the integral is a perfectly definite function whose values can be computed to any precision, and what is missing is a short expression built from the usual functions. The values are not unknown, and every statistical table in existence is a list of them. The density is written down in full at the top of this page, so it is only the cumulative form that resists. And the total integral is exactly one, which is required of every density by Part 11.
Your teacher. Two habits are worth taking from this page and neither is the formula. First, always read sigma as a distance, in the same units as x. If x is measured in milliseconds then so is sigma, and a question like "how far out is this value" is answered in sigmas rather than in raw units. Second, notice how comfortable the notes are saying that something cannot be written down. That comfort is what a working mathematician has and a nervous reader does not: a function is a perfectly good object whether or not it has a short name, and a lot of applied statistics is exactly this integral being looked up rather than derived.
