Lecture 6 · Part 12 of 14 · two continuous shapes

A flat density with two corners, and a decay that never quite finishes

12 min working The first two named densities, each drawn beside the CDF it integrates to, exactly as the notes draw them.

The one-minute version

  • The uniform on [a, b] has density 1/(b − a) across the interval and zero outside it. Its CDF is a straight ramp from 0 at a to 1 at b.
  • The notes point at the two corners of that ramp and write not differentiable here. That is the "almost everywhere" of Part 11 arriving on its very first example.
  • The exponential with parameter λ has density λe−λx for x ≥ 0 and zero for negative x.
  • Its CDF is the one closed form in this half of the lecture: FX(x) = 1 − e−λx for x ≥ 0, and 0 below.
  • The notes state that the exponential is the only continuous memoryless random variable, write "prove it" beside the identity, and move on. So does this page.

1. The uniform, symbol by symbol

fX(x) = 1/(b − a) for a ≤ x ≤ b, and 0 otherwise a and b are the two parameters, the ends of the interval, with a below b. b − a is the width of that interval. The height 1/(b − a) is forced rather than chosen: a rectangle of width b − a and height h has area h(b − a), and consequence 2 of Part 11 requires that area to be exactly 1, so h can only be one over the width. Note that the density gets larger as the interval gets narrower, and exceeds 1 once the width drops below 1.

Note the contrast with the discrete uniform of Part 7, whose mass was 1/(b − a + 1). The plus one is gone, and its absence is not a slip. There it counted the whole numbers in a run and fenceposts mattered; here it measures the width of a continuous interval and there is nothing to count.

Its CDF follows by area, with no calculus needed. For a threshold x inside the interval, the area collected so far is a rectangle of width x − a and height 1/(b − a), so the CDF is the ratio (x − a)/(b − a). At x = a that is 0 and at x = b it is 1, and in between it is a straight line, which is exactly the ramp the notes draw.

Two corners, and what "almost everywhere" boughtThe notes draw an arrow at the left corner of the ramp and write not diff. here. That annotation is the reason consequence 1 of Part 11 said the CDF is differentiable almost everywhere rather than everywhere. Two points out of the whole real line is exactly the kind of exception the phrase is built to allow, and the notes make the point on the first example rather than leaving it abstract. The values a = 1 and b = 4 are this course's drawing choice; the notes draw the shape with a and b symbolic.

2. The exponential, symbol by symbol

fX(x) = λe−λx for x ≥ 0, and 0 otherwise λ, lambda, is the single parameter and the notes require it to be strictly positive. e−λx decays as x grows, and the larger lambda is the faster it decays. λ in front is the height at x = 0, and like the uniform's height it is forced rather than chosen: it is what makes the total area come to one. The variable is zero on the whole negative half-line, so the support is the non-negative reals.

The notes then write the CDF outright, which makes this the only continuous shape in the lecture whose cumulative function can be written down in closed form.

FX(x) = 1 − e−λx for x ≥ 0, and 0 for x < 0 At x = 0 this reads 1 − 1 = 0, so it joins the negative half smoothly and the whole function is continuous, as consequence 1 of Part 11 requires. As x grows, e−λx shrinks towards zero and the CDF climbs towards 1 without ever reaching it. Differentiating gives λe−λx, which is the density back again.
This course's arithmeticBoth figures use λ = 1, which is this course's choice; the notes draw both shapes with the parameter symbolic and mark no values. The one evaluated point is F(1) = 1 − e−1 = 0.6321 to four places, and for reference F(2) = 0.8647 and F(3) = 0.9502. None of those three numbers is the lecturer's.

3. The memorylessness claim

The notes close this page with one sentence and one instruction: the exponential is the only continuous random variable which is memoryless, satisfying P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s), followed by the bracketed words prove it.

The notes say "prove it" and this page does notThe source states the identity and the uniqueness claim, writes "prove it" in brackets, and moves straight on to the Gaussian. No proof is given in the notes and none is supplied here. Supplying one would look helpful and would in fact be this course teaching material the lecturer deliberately left as an exercise, which is a different thing from filling in a step the lecturer compressed. What can be said, and is said below, is how the claim sits beside a claim this course has already met.

The parallel is exact and it is worth drawing, because the two claims together say something sharp. Part 9 carried the notes' claim that among discrete random variables the geometric is the only memoryless one. This page carries the notes' claim that among continuous random variables the exponential is the only memoryless one. So memorylessness does not merely describe those two shapes: it picks each of them out uniquely in its own half of the world.

4. Check yourself

A uniform density sits on the interval from 3 to 3.5. What is its height?
The width is nought point five, so the height has to be one divided by nought point five, which is two, and a density above one is perfectly legal because a height is not a probability. One half is the width itself rather than its reciprocal, so it would give a total area of a quarter. The left endpoint plays no part in the height at all: shifting the whole interval sideways changes nothing. And the last option mixes an endpoint with a width, which are different quantities.
Why is the uniform CDF not differentiable at the two ends of the interval?
To the left of a the ramp is flat and to the right it climbs at one over the width, so there is no single slope at a and the derivative does not exist there. The CDF is perfectly continuous at both points: it has no jump, and the two pieces meet. The density is defined at both ends, taking the value one over the width there by the formula as written. And the last claim is far too strong; the exponential's support starts at zero and its CDF is differentiable there.
Your teacher. Read every density picture as a shape whose area is pinned at one, and the formulas stop looking arbitrary. The uniform's height is one over the width because that is the only height a rectangle of that width can have. The exponential's leading lambda is there for the same reason: it is what a decay of that speed needs in front of it to enclose one unit of area. Neither constant is a fact to memorise, and if you ever forget one you can recover it by asking what makes the area come out right. One line of serving vocabulary for the exponential, since it is the shape a queue wears: it models the gap between one event and the next - request inter-arrival times, or the wait until the next failure - and its memorylessness, which the next part of the notes asserts, is the same claim the geometric made in Part 9, moved into continuous time.
