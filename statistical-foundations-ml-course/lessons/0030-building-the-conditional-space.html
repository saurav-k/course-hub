TA Session 1 · Part 5 of 8 · Problem 8

Conditioning is not a formula - it is a whole new probability space

frontier 18 min Tutorial problem 8, the sheet's centrepiece, in six parts: shrink the world to B, rebuild its events, renormalise its probabilities, and land back on the familiar formula.

The one-minute version

  • Told that the outcome lies in B, you may treat B as the entire sample space - nothing outside it can happen any more.
  • The new event collection FB consists of the traces A∩B, and parts (i) to (iii) prove it is genuinely a sigma-field of subsets of B.
  • The new measure divides by P(B), which rescales whatever mass B carried up to one - that division is the whole mechanism.
  • Part (vi) is the punchline: fixing B and mapping each event A to P(A|B) is itself a probability measure on the original space, so every probability theorem survives conditioning untouched.

Problem 8, restated

Let (Ω, F, P) be a probability space: outcomes ω live in Ω, the admissible events form the sigma-field F, and P assigns each event its probability. Suppose B ∈ F with P(B) > 0. We are told the outcome of the experiment belongs to B. So B becomes the new sample space, and we define

FB = { A∩B : A ∈ F } Read as: the collection of all sets of the form A intersected with B, where A ranges over every old admissible event. Each member is called a trace of F on B - the shadow the old event casts inside the new smaller world.

Six steps follow. Parts (i) to (iii) show FB is a sigma-field of subsets of B; part (iv) turns division by P(B) into a probability measure on it; part (v) recovers the famous conditional formula; part (vi) shows that, seen from the original space, conditioning is again a probability measure.

Part (i): FB lives inside F, and B heads it

The tool here is closure: sigma-fields are closed under complements and unions, and - by De Morgan's laws applied to A∩B = (Ac∪Bc)c - under intersections too. That last fact is exactly what part (i) needs.

  1. Take a generic member of FB. It has the shape A∩B with both A and B belonging to F.
  2. Close over the intersection. Since F is closed under intersections, A∩B ∈ F. Every member of FB passed this test, so FB ⊆ F.
  3. Capture the head of the new space. The old space Ω certainly belongs to F, so choose A = Ω: then Ω∩B = B, proving B ∈ FB.
  4. Note the promotion. B was one event among many before; it is the entire universe of the new space now. Nothing outside it can occur, because we are told it did not.

Part (ii): complements, taken against B

In the new world the complement of an event C means "the rest of B", written B\C - not the old complement against Ω, which would reach outside the new universe entirely. The claim: if C = A∩B is in FB, then B\C = Ac∩B is also in FB.

  1. Unpack the set difference. B\C means points of B not in C: B ∩ (A∩B)c.
  2. Apply De Morgan inside. (A∩B)c = Ac∪Bc, so B ∩ (Ac∪Bc) = (B∩Ac) ∪ (B∩Bc).
  3. Watch the second piece vanish. B∩Bc = ∅, leaving exactly B\C = Ac∩B.
  4. Recognise membership. A ∈ F makes Ac ∈ F, so Ac∩B has the defining trace shape: it belongs to FB. As a free corollary, taking C = B gives ∅ = B\B ∈ FB: the empty set arrived without being asked for.

Part (iii): countable unions, hence a sigma-field

  1. Take members Cn = An∩B, one trace per index n.
  2. Push the union through the intersection. A point lies in some Cn exactly when it lies in B and in some An; symbolically ∪n(An∩B) = (∪nAn)∩B. This is the distributive law running over infinitely many branches at once.
  3. Appeal to the old field. F is closed under countable unions, so ∪nAn ∈ F, making (∪nAn)∩B a trace and therefore a member of FB.
  4. Conclude part three's verdict. FB contains its universe B, closes under complements relative to B, and closes under countable unions: precisely the sigma-field axioms, with B playing the role Ω played before. FB is a sigma-field of subsets of B.

Part (iv): dividing by P(B) builds a genuine measure

Define PB(C) = P(C)/P(B) for C ∈ FB. The division is legal precisely because the hypothesis P(B) > 0 holds - this is where the hypothesis stops being decoration. Verify the three axioms on the new space:

  1. Non-negativity. P never returns a negative number, and dividing a non-negative quantity by the positive P(B) stays non-negative.
  2. Normalisation, to the new universe. The whole new space is B, and PB(B) = P(B)/P(B) = 1. Whatever modest share B held before, it holds everything now.
  3. Countable additivity. Take pairwise disjoint Cn in FB. Since FB ⊆ F, these are disjoint admissible sets of the old space too, so the old measure adds them: P(∪nCn) = ∑nP(Cn). Dividing both sides by P(B) splits the quotient into ∑nPB(Cn). All three axioms hold, so (B, FB, PB) is a probability space.

Part (v): the famous formula falls out

  1. Start from the new-space definition. For A ∈ F, define P(A|B) := PB(A∩B) - the new measure of A's trace on B.
  2. Unwind PB one step. By part (iv)'s definition, PB(A∩B) = P(A∩B)/P(B).
  3. Read off the result. P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B): the textbook formula, arrived at by construction rather than decree. Everything on the right is measured in the old space; the quotient simply reports how much of B's mass A shares.

Part (vi): the punchline - conditioning is a measure on the old space

Fix B and consider the map sending each old event A to QB(A) = P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B). This lives on the original (Ω, F), not on the shrunken space, and the claim is that it too satisfies all three axioms:

  1. Non-negativity. P(A∩B) ≥ 0 and P(B) > 0, so every value QB(A) is non-negative.
  2. Normalisation. QB(Ω) = P(Ω∩B)/P(B) = P(B)/P(B) = 1: told nothing beyond "B happened", B is certain.
  3. Countable additivity. If A1, A2, ... are pairwise disjoint, then so are their traces A1∩B, A2∩B, ... - cropping disjoint sets cannot make them overlap. Hence P((A1∪A2∪...)∩B) = ∑nP(An∩B), and dividing by P(B) distributes over the sum. So QB(∪nAn) = ∑nQB(An).
  4. State what was earned. For fixed B, the map A ↦ P(A|B) is a bona fide probability measure on (Ω, F). Every identity already proved for ordinary probabilities - complements, inclusion-exclusion, De Morgan consequences - therefore continues to hold verbatim with every probability replaced by a conditional one.
The distinction the notes underlinePB and P(·|B) are related but not identical objects: PB is defined on FB, the traces, with sample space B - while P(·|B) is defined on all of F with sample space still written Ω. They meet through P(A|B) = PB(A∩B). In plain words, conditioning on B means: keep only the outcomes inside B, then divide by P(B). Lecture 3 makes this same claim when it introduces conditional probability; problem 8 is where the claim is actually proved.
Where this goes wrongThree traps. First, taking complements against Ω inside the new space: part (ii)'s algebra shows the old complement overshoots B and the extra piece annihilates - but writing it correctly from the start is faster. Second, assuming FB must contain every subset of B: it contains the traces, and if F was coarse then FB is coarse too; on our small finite examples the two coincide, which quietly misleads. Third, treating P(B) > 0 as boilerplate: delete it and part (iv) divides by zero, which is exactly why conditional probability is silent about impossible conditions.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Part (i) claims every trace A∩B already belongs to F. Which single fact closes that argument?
Membership in F needs closure, and intersection closure follows from complements plus unions via De Morgan - that is the engine. Positivity matters later, in part four's division. Traces equal the full power set only when F was already fine-grained, not in general. Repeating A adds nothing about intersections directly.
What does part (vi) actually establish about the map sending A to P(A|B)?
Its domain is all of F and it passes all three axioms there, so it stands as a legitimate measure - meaning every proven probability identity transfers to conditional ones automatically. It reweights rather than crops events. It disagrees with P almost everywhere except by coincidence. Composition with the identity is bookkeeping, not a theorem.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1 - run the construction on a dieworking

Let Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} with F its power set and P uniform, and let B = {2, 4, 6}. List every member of FB, then compute PB({2}) and PB({4, 6}).

Hint

With F the power set, intersecting with B can produce any subset of B - count them before listing.

Show the worked solution
  1. Size the collection first. Every subset of B is reachable as {a}∩B for a suitable A, so FB = 2B, holding 2³ = 8 sets.
  2. List them. ∅; {2}; {4}; {6}; {2,4}; {2,6}; {4,6}; and B itself.
  3. Compute PB({2}). P({2}) = 1/6 and P(B) = 3/6, so PB({2}) = (1/6)/(3/6) = 1/3.
  4. Compute PB({4,6}). P({4,6}) = 2/6, so the quotient is (2/6)/(3/6) = 2/3.
  5. Check normalisation. The three atoms {2}, {4}, {6} each receive one third, and thirds sum to one over the whole new space B, as they must.

Sanity check. Conditional on an even roll, the three even faces should share the mass equally - three values of one third each, which is exactly what the quotients gave.

Practice 2 - delete the hypothesis and watch it breakfoundation

On the same die, take B = ∅. Where exactly does the six-part construction collapse?

Hint

Walk the parts in order and find the first one that refuses to run.

Show the worked solution
  1. Parts (i) to (iii) survive. FB = {A∩∅} = {∅}, which is a perfectly good sigma-field of subsets of ∅ - degenerate, but lawful.
  2. Part (iv) dies. PB(∅) = P(∅)/P(∅) = 0/0, which is undefined: the division that defines the new measure cannot be performed.
  3. Diagnose. The hypothesis P(B) > 0 is precisely the licence for that division; with B empty there is no mass to rescale, and "told the outcome lies in B" describes an impossible situation.
  4. Moral. Conditional probability is defined exactly when the conditioning event is possible - the formalism fails precisely where intuition does.

Sanity check. Any construction ending in a division by zero was doomed from its hypothesis, and here the hypothesis is the very thing removed.

Your teacher. If part (vi) feels like ceremony, ask the question it answers: after conditioning, must everything be re-proved? The answer this page bought is no, forever. If part (iii)'s infinite distributive step bothers you, test it on two sets first and watch the pattern.
