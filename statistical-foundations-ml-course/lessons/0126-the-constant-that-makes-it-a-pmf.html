The exponential factor in the Poisson is not a decoration: it is whatever makes the total come to one

working 11 min Problem 5 on the sheet, and the only place in the course where a named distribution is derived rather than handed over.

After this page

  • You can find a normalising constant by setting the total mass to one and spending a known series.
  • You can say where the factor e−λ in the Poisson comes from.
  • You can compute a Poisson tail by complementing the first few terms.

Before you start: The Poisson and the shape of N, where the formula was first stated without derivation.

The one-minute version

  • You are handed p(i) = cλi/i! for i = 0, 1, 2, … and asked to find P(X = 0) and P(X > 2).
  • Neither can be answered until c is known, and only one rule fixes c: the masses must sum to 1.
  • The sum Σ λi/i! over all i ≥ 0 is the series for eλ, a fact to memorise.
  • So c eλ = 1 and c = e−λ, which turns the expression into the Poisson PMF.
  • Then P(X = 0) = e−λ and P(X > 2) = 1 − e−λ(1 + λ + λ2/2).

1. The question, restated in full

The probability mass function of a random variable X is given by p(i) = cλi/i!, for i = 0, 1, 2, …, where λ is some positive value. Find (a) P(X = 0) and (b) P(X > 2). The sheet cites Ross, Chapter 4, end-of-chapter Problem 4, p. 142, and the same question appears in the slow sitting's notes as Example 2a of A First Course in Probability. It came onto the sheet because a student raised it on the course portal.

Σi≥0 p(i) = 1 i is the value X takes, running over every non-negative integer. p(i) is the mass at i. c is the unknown constant. λ, lambda, is a fixed positive parameter of the model, supplied by whoever poses the problem. The sigma sign means "add up over every i", and the equation says the total probability across the whole support is exactly one.

A student asked why the sum runs to infinity rather than to some n. The tutor's answer was that the question itself lists the values as 0, 1, 2 and so on with no stopping point stated, so there is none. Had the question said i runs to n, the sum would have stopped at n. That reading is the whole of the setup, and it is why this variable can produce arbitrarily large counts.

2. Finding c

  1. Write the total. Σ over i = 0 to infinity of cλi/i! must equal 1.
  2. Pull the constant out. c does not depend on i, so the total is c × Σ λi/i!.
  3. Recognise the series. The remaining sum is the Taylor series of the exponential at λ: Σ λi/i! = eλ. The tutor called this a fact to keep in memory rather than something to derive here.
  4. Solve. The equation is now c eλ = 1, so c = 1/eλ = e−λ.
  5. Substitute back. p(i) = e−λλi/i!, which is exactly the Poisson mass function from Lecture 6.

That last line is the point of the whole problem. A student asked what the factor e−λ means, and the answer is that it means nothing on its own. It is the number that drags the total down to 1, and it is fully determined by the shape λi/i! that sits beside it.

3. The two answers

  1. (a) P(X = 0). Put i = 0 into the normalised form: e−λλ0/0!. Both λ0 and 0! are 1, so the answer is e−λ.
  2. (b) Turn the tail round. P(X > 2) = 1 − P(X ≤ 2), because the support runs to infinity and three terms are easier to write than infinitely many.
  3. Expand the head. P(X ≤ 2) = P(X = 0) + P(X = 1) + P(X = 2).
  4. Substitute each term. They are e−λ, then e−λλ/1!, then e−λλ2/2!, and 2! is 2.
  5. Assemble. P(X > 2) = 1 − [e−λ + λe−λ + (λ2/2)e−λ], which factors to 1 − e−λ(1 + λ + λ2/2).

The tutor stopped there and said the expression is a complete answer as it stands. λ has no value in this question, and inventing one would be answering a different question. In a problem that supplies a rate, such as a mail-arrivals or a traffic model, you substitute at this point and reach a number.

The series is asserted, not proved, in both sittingsThe step that carries the whole problem is Σ λi/i! = eλ, and the tutor introduced it as something you have to know, saying twice that if you see a sum of that shape you should recognise the exponential. No derivation was offered on either sitting. It is the Taylor series of ex about zero, evaluated at λ, and it converges for every real λ, but nothing in this course establishes that; it is taught here as stated, exactly as it was in the room.

The problem is Example 2a of Chapter 4 in Ross, A First Course in Probability, where the same normalisation produces the Poisson mass function.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What determines the value of the constant c in this problem?
Only one equation is available and it is the total probability, which pins c to e to the minus lambda. The first option is close but backwards: c depends on lambda because the sum does, not because lambda is given. The third is a necessary condition that many constants would satisfy, so it fixes nothing. The fourth changes the sum but is not itself the rule that solves for c.
Why is P(X > 2) computed as one minus a sum of three terms?
The support runs over every non-negative integer, so writing the tail directly means an infinite sum, while its complement is three terms. The first option is false: there is mass at every value. The second is too strong, since a tail on a bounded support is a perfectly good finite sum. The fourth is simply untrue of the Poisson, whose masses are positive everywhere.

Practice about 10 minutes, with a calculator

Practice 1working

A support desk receives tickets at a Poisson rate of λ = 3 per hour.

Find the probability of a quiet hour with no tickets at all, and the probability of more than two tickets in an hour.

Hint

Both answers are the page's two formulas with 3 put in for lambda. You need e−3, about 0.0498.

Show the worked solution
  1. Quiet hour. P(X = 0) = e−3 = 0.0498, about one hour in twenty.
  2. Write the tail. P(X > 2) = 1 − e−3(1 + 3 + 32/2).
  3. Evaluate the bracket. 1 + 3 + 4.5 = 8.5.
  4. Multiply. 0.0498 × 8.5 = 0.4232, which is P(X ≤ 2).
  5. Complement. 1 − 0.4232 = 0.577, so more than half of all hours carry three tickets or more.

Sanity check. Both answers sit between 0 and 1, and P(X ≤ 2) must exceed P(X = 0), which it does by a wide margin. The answer should also feel right against the rate: three per hour on average means two or fewer is the minority outcome, and 0.577 above two says exactly that.

Carry this away

  • An unknown constant in front of a mass function is fixed by one rule: the masses sum to 1.
  • The series for the exponential is what makes that sum solvable here, and it is memorised rather than derived.
  • The factor in the Poisson is the normaliser, so it changes the scale and never the profile.
  • A tail on an unbounded support is computed by complementing a finite head.

Your teacher. Whenever a question hands you a mass function with a letter in front of it, write the sum-to-one equation before you read the rest of the question. It is the only equation available, it always solves the constant, and the sub-questions are almost always unanswerable until it is done. The same move works for a density, with an integral in place of a sum, and you will meet it again the moment continuous variables return.
