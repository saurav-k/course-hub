Lecture 6 · Part 11 of 14 · continuous random variables

Nothing sits on a point any more, so probability has to be measured as area

13 min the crossing The definition that carries the lecture from countable supports to intervals, and the four consequences the notes list under it.

The one-minute version

  • X is a continuous random variable when there is a non-negative function fX whose running integral is the CDF: FX(x) = ∫−∞x fX(z) dz for every real x.
  • fX is the probability density function. The notes define the continuous case by the existence of this function, which is worth noticing: the density comes first and the word "continuous" is a consequence.
  • The notes then list four consequences: the CDF is continuous and differentiable almost everywhere with F' = f; the total area is 1; the probability of an interval is the area over it; and every single point has probability zero.
  • They also write a warning in square brackets: the integral of a density gives a probability, and the density itself does not. A density may be bigger than 1.
  • The last consequence is the one that unsettles readers, and Part 6 already predicted it: no jump in the CDF means no mass at a point.

1. The definition, symbol by symbol

The notes head the section "Continuous RVs" and give the definition in one sentence with one displayed line.

FX(x) = ∫−∞x fX(z) dz, for every x in R fX is a function from the real line to the non-negative numbers, written by the notes as fX : R → [0, ∞). It may be any size at all; only negative values are ruled out. z is the variable being integrated over, and it is a dummy name: it exists only inside the integral. The limits run from minus infinity up to x, so the integral accumulates everything to the left of the threshold. FX is the same CDF as Part 4, defined the same way, and this line says how to build it from f.

Compare that with the discrete case and the correspondence is exact. There, the CDF was a running sum of masses over the support up to x. Here it is a running integral of a density over the line up to x. The word "continuous" is not part of the definition at all: what the notes require is that such a function exists, and continuity of the CDF is then the first thing on their list of consequences.

2. The four consequences the notes list

The notes write them as a lettered list, and each one deserves its own sentence.

  1. The CDF is continuous. No jumps anywhere. It is also differentiable almost everywhere, and where it is differentiable its derivative is the density: d/dx FX(x) = fX(x). So the density and the CDF are two views of one object, one obtained from the other by integrating or differentiating. The phrase "almost everywhere" is the notes' own and it is doing real work: Part 12 shows a CDF with two corners where the derivative simply does not exist.
  2. The total area is one. ∫−∞∞ fX(x) dx = limx→∞ FX(x) = 1, which is property 3 of Part 5 restated in the new language. The notes add the bracketed remark that the integral of the density gives a probability and the density itself does not.
  3. An interval is an area. P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab fX(x) dx. This is the working formula: almost every question you will ask about a continuous variable is this one.
  4. Every point has probability zero. P(X = x) = 0 for every real x, and the notes go further: the probability assigned to any countable set is zero.
The countable-set claim is stated without proofThe notes write "In fact, prob. that X assigns to any countable set is zero" and give no argument. This course teaches it as stated. What can be said from the notes alone is that it does not follow from consequence 4 by simply adding zeros: adding uncountably many zeros is not a legal move, which is exactly why the claim is restricted to countable sets, where Lecture 2, Part 5's countable additivity does apply. That observation is this course's, and no proof is supplied here.

3. Why the height is not a probability

The bracketed warning in the notes is the single most useful line on their page, and it is worth making concrete.

Take a variable spread evenly over the interval from 0 to 0.5 and nowhere else. Its total area has to be 1, and the base is only half a unit wide, so the height has to be 2. A density of 2 is perfectly legal. What would not be legal is a probability of 2, and no probability appears anywhere in that picture until you multiply a height by a width.

So the units differ, and thinking in units settles the whole confusion. A probability is a pure number. A density is a probability per unit of x. Multiply a density by a length and you get a probability; read a density on its own and you have not got one yet.

This course's exampleThe notes give no example at all in this section: they define the density, list the four consequences and move on to the named continuous shapes. The triangular density above is this course's own, chosen because every area under it is a triangle or a trapezium and can be checked without calculus. Its rule is f(x) = x/4 on [0, 2] and f(x) = (4 − x)/4 on [2, 4], and zero elsewhere. Total area = ½ × 4 × 0.5 = 1. The area between 1 and 3 is 0.375 + 0.375 = 0.75, and none of these numbers is the lecturer's.

4. Every point has probability zero

This is the consequence readers push back on, so here it is twice: once as an argument and once as a picture.

The argument is one line and it was already available in Part 6. There, P(X = x) = FX(x) − FX(x−), the height of the jump. Consequence 1 says the CDF is continuous, so there are no jumps, so every one of those differences is zero.

The picture is the interval formula with the interval shrunk to nothing. An interval of width h around a point has an area, and as h goes to zero that area goes to zero with it, because the height stays bounded while the width vanishes.

This course's arithmeticThe three strip areas are this course's own, worked on the triangular density above. A strip of half-width h around 2 has area h − h2/4, giving 0.4375, 0.2344 and 0.0975 at the three widths drawn. The notes state consequence 4 in one line and draw nothing.

5. What survives the crossing

6. Check yourself

A density takes the value 2 at some point. What has gone wrong?
A density is a probability per unit of x, so its height carries no ceiling; the uniform on nought to nought point five has height 2 everywhere and total area one. Being above one is only a problem for a probability, and no probability appears until a height is multiplied by a width. The total area is fixed at one by consequence 2 whatever the heights are, and a tall narrow density is exactly how that is achieved. And masses are the things that genuinely may not exceed one, since each one is a probability outright.
Which earlier result already told you that a continuous variable has no mass at a point?
Part 6 established that the probability of an exact value is the height of the jump there, so a CDF with no jumps has no mass anywhere, and consequence 1 says exactly that this CDF has none. Monotonicity constrains the direction of travel and is satisfied by both jumpy and smooth CDFs alike. The countable support in Part 3 is the definition of the discrete case, so it describes what continuous variables are not rather than what they do. And Part 4's running sum is the discrete construction, which is replaced here rather than reused.
Your teacher. The phrase to keep saying out loud is a density is a rate. It is probability per unit of x, in exactly the way that speed is distance per unit of time. Nobody is troubled that a car can travel at 60 miles per hour without travelling 60 miles, and nobody should be troubled that a density can be 2 without any probability being 2. Once the units are in your head, the height-is-not-a-probability warning stops needing to be remembered, and so does the fact that a single instant covers no distance. The serving read: token latency histograms in production are empirical densities where a high spike at 15 ms means requests cluster tightly there, not that P(latency = 15 ms) is large, while the integral up to the SLO deadline is the compliance percentage that matters.
