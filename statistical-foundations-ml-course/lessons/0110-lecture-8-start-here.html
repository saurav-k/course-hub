Lecture 8 · The map · Pre-class notes, 9 pages

Lecture 8 finally says what mu, sigma and lambda were standing for

foundation 9 min Nine pages of the lecturer's handwritten pre-class notes on expectation, variance and covariance. Every symbol Lecture 7 used and refused to define is defined here, and two of them turn out to be quantities that can fail to exist at all. Nine sittings.

The one-minute version

  • Lecture 7 used mu, sigma and lambda and said the course had not reached them. This lecture reaches them.
  • Expectation is a weighted average of the values a variable takes, and the weights are its own probabilities. The frequency argument is where it comes from, not the definition.
  • Five expectations are worked in full, and the routes matter more than the answers: a substitution for the exponential, a generating-function trick for the binomial, and a symmetry split for the Gaussian.
  • Then two shapes where the average does not exist: the Cauchy, whose two halves diverge in opposite directions, and the Pareto with a slow tail, whose mean is infinite.
  • Variance arrives as a second moment, with the working identity Var(X) = E[X2] − E2(X) proved rather than quoted.
  • The lecture closes on the trap: independent implies uncorrelated, and the converse is false, with one four-value counterexample and the two cases where the converse does hold.

What this lecture is, and what it is not

This is a pre-class document: nine pages the lecturer wrote before delivering the session, in the same shape as the notes behind Lecture 6. It is dense, it works its examples to a closed form rather than to a decimal, and it leaves three results as exercises for the reader.

What it is not is a delivered session. There is no room, no objection, and no correction of a board slip, because none of that has happened yet. Where Lecture 7 could carry the questions a class actually asked, these pages carry only what the notes carry, plus the arithmetic this course works underneath them.

How this lecture is arranged

The notes run in a straight line and these pages keep that order: expectation defined, then five distributions worked, then two where the definition breaks, then the second moment and variance, then the tools that make expectation cheap to compute, and finally what happens with two variables at once.

The nine parts

  1. What an average has to mean. Run the experiment N times, let the observed frequencies settle, and the empirical average turns into a weighted sum of values against probabilities. That sum is the definition, and the uniform gets worked first.
  2. Four expectations worked in full. The exponential by substitution, the binomial by differentiating the binomial theorem, and the Gaussian by splitting off its centre. Three genuinely different routes to three means.
  3. The mean that does not exist. The Cauchy is symmetric about zero and its mean is not zero: it is undefined, because each half of the integral runs away in its own direction and infinity minus infinity is not a number.
  4. Heavy tails and an infinite mean. The Pareto, one integral, and the exponent that decides everything: above one the mean is finite, at or below one it is infinite. Polynomial decay is what makes this possible.
  5. Variance as a second moment. Moments defined, variance defined as the expected squared distance from the mean, and the identity that makes it computable proved in four lines.
  6. LOTUS and linearity. The expected value of a function of X, computed against the distribution of X, with no new distribution derived. Then the property that carries most of the lecture's weight: expectation adds, always, whether or not anything is independent.
  7. The indicator trick. Balls into bins, and the expected number of empty bins in three lines. The variables being summed are strongly dependent, and linearity does not care, which is the whole point of the example.
  8. Uncorrelated is not independent. Independence gives a product rule for expectations. Covariance and correlation are defined from it, and then one four-value example has zero covariance and obvious dependence.
  9. When the converse holds. Two cases, and only two: jointly Gaussian variables, which the notes defer, and binary variables, which the notes prove by filling in a two-by-two table one cell at a time.

The honesty notes, named in advance

Five things need saying before you meet them: one about which lecture this is, three about gaps the notes leave open on purpose, and one about every number on these pages.

This is Lecture 8 of this course, and the source carries no numberThe nine pages are headed Expectation and Moments of RV with no lecture number and no date anywhere on them. This course places them at Lecture 8 because that is the next free position in its own sequence and the material follows Lecture 7 directly. Do not read the position as the lecturer's own numbering: PLOT.md records that his notes and this course's sessions have already drifted apart by one topic, and nothing here reconciles them.
Three results are left as exercises, and this course works all threeThe notes write Show beside the geometric mean 1/p and the Poisson mean λ, and Prove! beside the Gaussian variance σ2. None of the three is worked in the source. Part 2 and Part 5 work them in full and label each one as this course's derivation rather than the lecture's, in the same way Lecture 4, Part 5 finished the arithmetic that lecture left open.
The general definition of expectation is deferred, and it stays deferred herePage 1 of the notes carries a margin question beside the continuous definition: general defn of expectation? The answer is the measure-theoretic one, an integral of the random variable against the probability measure, which covers the discrete and the continuous case in one line and covers variables that are neither. It is named in MISSION.md as out of scope for this course, so these pages name the question, say why the two definitions look different, and stop there.
The balls-and-bins question in the margin is never answeredPage 7 boxes the question Are Xi, Xj ind? beside the empty-bin calculation and moves on. They are not: bin i being empty makes bin j likelier to hold something. Part 7 answers it under this course's own name and shows the answer changing nothing about the calculation, which is the reason the question is worth boxing in the first place.
The notes state no numbers at allEvery example in these nine pages ends at a closed form: 1/μ, np, p(1 − p), (b − a)2/12. There is not one decimal anywhere in the source. Every number on these pages is therefore this course's own arithmetic on the lecture's own formulas, carried in a .callout.key and never marked as a quoted figure.

What the lecture assumes you have

Everything on these pages runs on the machinery of Lecture 6 and Lecture 7: a PMF for a discrete variable, a density for a continuous one, and the five named shapes. If the difference between a PMF and a density is not solid, Lecture 7, Part 7 is the page to reread before starting here, because every definition in this lecture comes in a discrete version and a continuous version that differ only in whether a sum or an integral appears.

What to carry out of it

How to read the pages. Every page leads with a picture, then names the mechanism, then works the numbers with no step skipped. If a step is missing, that is a defect in the page and not in you. The glossary holds one-sentence definitions for every term, and the Lecture 8 formula sheet holds every expression in this lecture on one printable page, with the five means and three variances side by side.
Your teacher. Carry one question through all nine pages: what am I averaging, and against which weights? Every expectation in this lecture, including the ones that look like new theory, is that same weighted sum with something different in the value slot. Put X in the slot and you get the mean. Put X squared in the slot and you get the second moment, which is what variance is built from. Put an indicator in the slot and the sum counts things. Put XY in the slot and you get correlation. Readers who see one machine with four inputs move through the rest of this course much faster than readers who learn four formulas.
