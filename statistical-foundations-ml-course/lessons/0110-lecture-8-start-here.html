Lecture 8 · The map · delivered 8 September 2026

Lecture 8 spends its whole length on one idea: the average of a random quantity

foundation 9 min The session delivered on Tuesday 8 September 2026, from the recording, the class notes taken during it, and the lecturer's own typeset handout. Expectation, defined and then spent on six distributions and one business decision. Seven sittings.

The one-minute version

  • Lecture 7 used mu and lambda and said the course had not reached them. This session reaches them, and this session is about expectation and nothing else.
  • Expectation is a weighted average of the values a variable takes, and the weights are its own probabilities. The frequency argument is where it comes from, not the definition.
  • Six means are worked or stated: uniform (a+b)/2, Bernoulli p, binomial np, exponential 1/λ, geometric 1/p, Gaussian mu. Some are integrals, some are series, and two are handed to the reader as exercises.
  • Two shapes where the average fails: the Cauchy, whose two halves diverge in opposite directions, and the Pareto with a slow tail, whose mean is infinite.
  • The expected-value rule lets you average a function of a variable against that variable's own density, deriving no new distribution, and linearity adds expectations whether or not anything is independent.
  • The session closes on a retailer choosing a stock level against uncertain demand, and one expectation turns that into a quadratic with a single answer: stock 40.

What this lecture is, and what it is not

This is a delivered session, and these pages are built from the recording of it, from the class notes taken in the room, and from the lecturer's own typeset handout on the inventory example. Almost half of the ninety minutes is questions from the floor, and that is where most of this lecture's value sits: the room pushed back on the definition, on the modelling, and once on the mathematics, and the lecturer conceded the last one on the spot.

What it is not is a lecture on variance. The word is not spoken once. Variance, covariance and the failure of the uncorrelated-to-independent converse all belong to the next session, and the three pages this site already carries on them are flagged as a pre-read rather than as a record; they are listed separately at the bottom of this map.

The six parts

  1. What an average has to mean. Run the experiment N times, let the observed frequencies settle, and the empirical average turns into a weighted sum of values against probabilities. That sum is the definition, the uniform gets worked first, and four questions from the floor settle what a support is and how the expectation differs from the mean of a sample.
  2. Four expectations worked in full. The exponential by substitution, the binomial by differentiating the binomial theorem, and the Gaussian by splitting off its centre, plus the Bernoulli in two terms and the dataset example that shows why a mini-batch of one is already right on average.
  3. The mean that does not exist. The Cauchy is symmetric about zero and its mean is not zero: it is undefined, because each half of the integral runs away in its own direction and infinity minus infinity is not a number.
  4. Heavy tails and an infinite mean. The Pareto, one integral, and the exponent that decides everything: above one the mean is finite, at or below one it is infinite. Polynomial decay is what makes this possible.
  5. LOTUS and linearity. The expected value of a function of X, computed against the distribution of X, with no new distribution derived. Then the property that carries most of the lecture's weight: expectation adds, always, whether or not anything is independent.
  6. The indicator trick. Balls into bins, and the expected number of empty bins in three lines. The variables being summed are strongly dependent, and linearity does not care, which is the whole point of the example.

The chain then closes on the session's own worked example, which is a page in its own right because it takes the last half hour of the lecture and answers the question the room kept asking.

  • Stocking a shelf against uncertain demand. Demand uniform on [0, 100], cost 60, price 100, profit 100 min(D, Q) − 60Q. Split the integral where the minimum changes form, reach 40Q − 0.5Q², and maximise it at Q* = 40, with the second-derivative test a student had to ask for.

Three pages that run ahead of the class

This site carries three further pages, written earlier from the lecturer's handwritten pre-class notes, which cover material the delivered session did not reach: variance as a second moment, covariance, and the two cases where uncorrelated does imply independent. They keep their addresses, they keep their content, and they are now labelled for what they are.

Read these as a pre-read for the next session, not as Lecture 8. Nothing in them was said in the room on 8 September, and each one carries a notice at the top saying so. They will be reconciled against the variance session on the day it is delivered, in exactly the way this map has just been reconciled against the expectation session: the room's questions added, any correction from the floor recorded, and any result the class states differently checked against what the page already claims.
  1. Variance as a second moment. Moments defined, variance defined as the expected squared distance from the mean, and the identity that makes it computable proved in four lines.
  2. Uncorrelated is not independent. Independence gives a product rule for expectations. Covariance and correlation are defined from it, and then one four-value example has zero covariance and obvious dependence.
  3. When the converse holds. Two cases, and only two: jointly Gaussian variables, which the notes defer, and binary variables, which the notes prove by filling in a two-by-two table one cell at a time.

The honesty notes, named in advance

Four things need saying before you meet them: one about the class correcting the lecturer, one about the results handed over as exercises, one about a word the class used loosely, and one about every number on these pages.

The lecturer skipped the second-derivative test and a student made him do itHaving set 40 − Q = 0 and written Q* = 40, the board moved on. A student objected that a stationary point is not a maximum until the second derivative is checked, and the lecturer agreed at once: "You are absolutely right. I was a little bit sloppy here." He then worked the test. The lecturer's own typeset handout carries the same admission in its closing remark. The worked example carries the correction as part of the material, because a reader working alone will make exactly the same omission.
Three results are handed over as exercises, and this course works all threeThe exponential mean is left as an integration by parts, the geometric mean as the sum of a series, and the binomial mean as an unstated page of algebra with binomial coefficients. The lecturer's position was explicit and reasonable: "All you need is you need to know how to do integrals, and you need to know how to compute sums of series." Part 2 works them in full and labels each as this course's derivation rather than the lecture's.
The Cauchy was called infinite aloud and undefined in the notesAsked whether an expectation can be infinite, the lecturer named the Cauchy and said its expected value can be infinite too. His own written notes end that block with the words Expectation E[X] undefined, which is the stronger and stricter statement, because both halves of the Cauchy integral diverge rather than one. Part 3 follows the notes and names the gap rather than quietly choosing a side.
The session states almost no numbersApart from the inventory example, which is fully numeric, every result in this lecture ends at a closed form: (a+b)/2, p, np, 1/λ, 1/p, μ. Every other decimal on these pages is this course's own arithmetic on the lecture's own formulas, carried in a .callout.key and never marked as a quoted figure.

What the lecture assumes you have

Everything here runs on the machinery of Lecture 6 and Lecture 7: a PMF for a discrete variable, a density for a continuous one, and the named shapes. The lecturer opened by recapping exactly that and nothing more. He also settled a question left hanging from the previous session: you do not have to memorise the Gaussian density. Understanding what the distribution is and what its two parameters mean is what the course asks for, and this session gives the first of those two meanings.

What to carry out of it

How to read the pages. Every page leads with a picture, then names the mechanism, then works the numbers with no step skipped. If a step is missing, that is a defect in the page and not in you. The glossary holds one-sentence definitions for every term, and the Lecture 8 formula sheet holds every expression in this lecture on one printable page.
Your teacher. Carry one question through all seven pages: what am I averaging, and against which weights? Every expectation in this lecture, including the ones that look like new theory, is that same weighted sum with something different in the value slot. Put X in the slot and you get the mean. Put an indicator in the slot and the sum counts things. Put a day's profit in the slot and the sum tells you how much to stock. Readers who see one machine with three inputs move through the rest of this course much faster than readers who learn three formulas. The student who said he had stopped doing mathematics fifteen years ago and could not see the use case was asking the right question, and the dataset example in Part 2 and the shelf in the worked example are both answers to it.
