TA Session 2 · Part 4 of 7 · two unnumbered Sunday questions

Once the space has shrunk, both of these questions are one count divided by another

working 10 min Two questions from the Sunday notes, taken together because they are the same question twice with different furniture.

The one-minute version

  • The card. Given that a card drawn from 52 is a face card, the chance it is a king is (4/52) / (12/52) = 4/12 = 1/3, about 33.33%.
  • The class. Given that a student from a class of 40 studies maths, the chance they also study physics is (12/40) / (25/40) = 12/25 = 0.48, or 48%.
  • In both, the size of the original space cancels. The deck's 52 and the class's 40 appear in the numerator and the denominator and divide straight out.
  • So each answer is a count within a count: four kings among twelve face cards, twelve physicists among twenty-five mathematicians.
  • The class question hands you a number you never use. Spotting that is part of the exercise.
These two carry no question numberThe Saturday notes number their questions 3 and 10, and the Sunday notes number two of theirs 25 and 30. These two are simply headed Question with no number at all. The card question, restated

A card is drawn at random from a standard deck of 52 playing cards. Given that the card drawn is a face card, find the probability that it is a king.

Which theorem is in play, and what to notice before computing

The governing definition is the conditional probability from Part 3, P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). Name the two events in words first.

  • A is the event that the card is a king. There are 4 kings.
  • B is the event that the card is a face card. There are 12: four jacks, four queens and four kings.

Now the observation that makes the arithmetic short. Every king is a face card. So the event "king and face card" is just the event "king", with nothing removed.

A ∩ B = A A is contained in B, which is stronger than the two merely overlapping. Intersecting a set with something that already contains it changes nothing. In consequence P(A ∩ B) is simply P(A), and the conditional collapses from a ratio of two intersections to a ratio of two plain event sizes.
  1. Write the two probabilities against the full deck. P(A) = 4/52 and P(B) = 12/52.
  2. Use the containment. Since every king is a face card, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) = 4/52.
  3. Form the conditional. P(A | B) = (4/52) / (12/52).
  4. Cancel the 52. P(A | B) = 4/12 = 1/3.
Answer, the card question1/3, which the notes state as approximately 33.33%.

The class question, restated

In a class of 40 students, 25 study mathematics, 18 study physics, and 12 study both. If a student is selected at random from those who study mathematics, what is the probability that the student also studies physics?

Which theorem is in play, and which number is a decoy

The same definition, with the conditioning event named by the phrase "from those who study mathematics". Name the events.

  • M is the event that the student studies mathematics. 25 of the 40.
  • P is the event that the student studies physics. 18 of the 40.

The question asks for P(P | M), so M is the reduced space and the answer is the share of M that also lies in P. What is needed is the overlap, which the question gives directly as 12, and the size of M, which is 25. The figure 18 is never used.

  1. Write what is given as probabilities. P(M) = 25/40 and P(M ∩ P) = 12/40.
  2. Form the conditional. P(P | M) = P(P ∩ M) / P(M) = (12/40) / (25/40).
  3. Cancel the 40. P(P | M) = 12/25.
  4. Evaluate. 12/25 = 0.48.
Answer, the class question0.48, which the notes state as 48%.
This course's checkThe three given numbers fix the whole class, and it is worth completing the split as a check the notes do not run. Twenty-five study maths and twelve of those also study physics, so 13 study maths alone. Eighteen study physics and twelve of those also study maths, so 6 study physics alone. That accounts for 13 + 12 + 6 = 31 students, leaving 9 who study neither. All four groups are non-negative and they total 40, so the data is consistent. Had the overlap been stated as, say, 30, the split would have produced a negative group and the question would have been impossible.

The two questions are one question

Where this goes wrongThree slips. Dividing by the wrong event: answering 4/52 or 12/40 by forgetting that the space has shrunk, which is the mistake the phrase "given that" exists to prevent. Using the decoy: reaching for the 18 in the class question and computing 12/18, which answers a different and perfectly reasonable question, namely how many physicists also take maths. And in the card question, miscounting the face cards as 16 by including aces, which gives 4/16 = 1/4 and is wrong because an ace carries no face. Read what the condition names, count exactly that, and count the overlap inside it.

Check yourself

Why does P(king and face card) equal P(king) in the card question?
The four kings are among the twelve face cards, so the set of kings is contained in the set of face cards and intersecting the two returns the kings unchanged. The reverse containment is false: eight of the twelve face cards are jacks and queens. Independence would give a product rather than a containment, and these two events are in fact strongly dependent, since knowing the card is a face card raises the chance of a king from 1/13 to 1/3. And there are four kings against twelve face cards, which are not equal.
The class question states that 18 students study physics. Where is that number used?
The answer needs the overlap, 12, and the size of the conditioning event, 25, and nothing else, so the 18 never enters the calculation. The numerator is the overlap of maths and physics, which is 12 rather than 18. The conditioning event is mathematics, not physics, so the denominator is 25; using 18 would answer the reversed question. And nothing cancels here, since 18 appears in neither position.
Your teacher. Underline the words that follow "given that" before you write a single symbol. Those words name the denominator, and almost every error on conditional questions is a denominator error rather than an arithmetic one. Then ask what the question would look like reversed, because the reversed version is usually also answerable from the same numbers and is a different number entirely: 12/18 rather than 12/25 for the class, and 4/52 rather than 4/12 for the card. Being able to say which of the three you were asked for is the skill this pair of questions is testing.
