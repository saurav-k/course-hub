One exchange, sliced three ways, gives B two fifths of the game

frontier 9 min The companion to the problem page: the counting done, the infinity absorbed, the equation written.

After this page

  • You can read a target total off the grid of ordered pairs rather than off the eleven sums, which are not equally likely.
  • You can price a process that either ends or hands itself back unchanged, by slicing one round and solving one linear equation.

Before you start: Graded 4: the alternating dice game, which states the question and names its structure, and your own attempt at it.

The one-minute version

  • One throw of a pair totals 9 in 4 of the 36 ordered cells and 4 in 3 of them, so A hits with probability 1/9 and B with 1/12.
  • Slicing the first exchange three ways, the third slice hands back the starting position, so the unknown appears on both sides of one linear equation.
  • The answer is P(B wins) = 2/5 = 0.4, and A wins with 3/5. The two add to exactly 1, so the game ends with probability 1 despite having no last round.
  • Two lines of work: one of setup, one of algebra. No series, no limit, no convergence condition to justify.

1. The two numbers the whole game runs on

One throw of the pair is one cell of the six-by-six grid of ordered pairs: what the first die shows, and what the second shows. All 36 cells are equally likely, which is why the grid is the right sample space and the eleven sums are not.

  1. Count the cells that total 9. The first die can be 3, 4, 5 or 6, and the second is then forced to 6, 5, 4 or 3. No 1 or 2 can be rescued by any face. That is 4 cells.
  2. Turn the count into a probability. Every cell has probability 1/36, so p = 4/36 = 1/9. Read p as: the chance one throw by A totals nine.
  3. Count the cells that total 4. (1,3), (2,2) and (3,1). That is 3 cells, so q = 3/36 = 1/12. Read q as: the chance one throw by B totals four.
  4. Name the two misses. By the complement rule, a consequence of the normalisation and additivity axioms, A misses with probability 1 - p = 8/9 and B with 1 - q = 11/12.

2. The slicing, and the equation it produces

Write β for what is asked: B's chance of winning from a position where A is about to throw and nobody has scored. Slice the first exchange into its three endings and apply the law of total probability. Two endings answer at once; the third gives back β, because the position after a fruitless exchange is the starting position.

β = (1 - p)q + (1 - p)(1 - q) · β Read as: B's chance of winning equals B's chance of winning on this very exchange, plus the chance the exchange settles nothing times B's chance from the position it hands back. β ("beta") is that probability, p A's chance of totalling nine, q B's of totalling four. The third case, A hitting, contributes nothing: B has lost.
  1. Substitute the two coefficients. β = 2/27 + (22/27)β.
  2. Collect the β terms. β - (22/27)β = 2/27, so (5/27)β = 2/27.
  3. Divide. β = (2/27) ÷ (5/27) = 2/5 = 0.4.
  4. Check the other player. With α = P(A wins), the same slicing gives α = p + rα, so α = (3/27) ÷ (5/27) = 3/5. And 2/5 + 3/5 = 1, so nobody-ever-wins has probability 0.
  1. Substitute the two coefficients. (1 - p)q = (8/9)(1/12) = 2/27, and (1 - p)(1 - q) = (8/9)(11/12) = 22/27, so β = 2/27 + (22/27)β.
  2. Collect the β terms. β - (22/27)β = 2/27, so (5/27)β = 2/27.
  3. Divide. β = (2/27) ÷ (5/27) = 2/5 = 0.4.
  4. Check the other player. With α = P(A wins), the same slicing gives α = p + rα with r = 22/27, so α = (3/27) ÷ (5/27) = 3/5. And 2/5 + 3/5 = 1, so nobody-ever-wins has probability 0.

Writing "B wins" as an infinite sum of disjoint events instead gives the geometric series (2/27) × ∑ (22/27)k, which converges to the same 2/5; it is worth knowing because it shows the infinity is legitimate rather than assumed, but it costs a convergence condition the two lines above never need.

Notice what the answer is made of: 2/27 against 3/27, so B takes 2 of the 5 decisive parts. The 22/27 never reaches the final fraction, because a slice that hands back the same position cannot tilt the result.

One argument that does not work hereSymmetry is the classic short proof for an alternating game, and it fails here because nothing about the players matches: A aims at a 4-cell target, B at a 3-cell target, and A throws first. No exchange of labels leaves the game unchanged, so 2/5 against 3/5 is arithmetic rather than balance.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is it legitimate for the unknown to appear on both sides of the equation?
Same thrower, same targets, same blank score: the position after a fruitless exchange is the starting position, so the same symbol is correct on both sides and the result is one linear equation with one solution. The two players are not equally placed, since A has the larger target and throws first. The game has no bound on its length at all. And independence prices each throw but says nothing about why the position repeats.
Which pair of per-throw probabilities does this game actually run on?
Totalling 9 takes 4 of the 36 ordered cells and totalling 4 takes 3 of them, so p = 1/9 and q = 1/12. The second option has copied the target totals themselves into the numerators, which is the commonest slip on this question. The third counts over the eleven possible sums, and those are not equally likely, so the division is meaningless. The fourth simply miscounts both target sets.
The third ending of the slicing is A hitting. What does it contribute?
Conditioned on A hitting, B's chance of winning is zero, so that slice enters the sum with weight p and value 0 and drops out. The three endings are not equally likely, so no third of anything appears. A's hitting chance is the coefficient of a term that is multiplied by zero, not a term in its own right. And nothing cancels: the both-miss term survives as the (22/27) coefficient that makes the equation self-referential.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Same two dice, same two targets, one change: B throws first. A still wins on a total of 9 and B still wins on a total of 4.

Find the probability that B wins now, and say in one sentence what the change was worth to B.

Hint

Nothing about the two per-throw probabilities changes. Only the order of the two throws inside one exchange does, so write the equation for the new first mover and read off the new numerator.

Show the worked solution
  1. Rewrite the exchange. B throws, then A throws only if B missed. B wins the exchange with probability q = 1/12, A wins it with probability (1 - q)p = (11/12)(1/9) = 11/108, and both miss with probability r = (11/12)(8/9) = 22/27, exactly as before.
  2. Write the equation. β′ = q + rβ′ = 1/12 + (22/27)β′.
  3. Solve it. (5/27)β′ = 1/12, so β′ = (1/12)(27/5) = 27/60 = 9/20 = 0.45.
  4. Say what it bought. Throwing first lifted B from 0.40 to 0.45, a gain of 0.05, or one extra win in every twenty games.

Sanity check. The answer must be larger than 2/5, because moving first can only help and it cannot help so much that the weaker target beats the stronger one: 0.4 < 0.45 < 0.5, as it must be. A's new probability is 11/20, and 9/20 + 11/20 = 1.

Carry this away

  • One throw of a pair lives on 36 ordered cells, so a total of 9 is 4 of them and a total of 4 is 3 of them.
  • A fruitless exchange returns the starting position, so the same unknown is correct on both sides and the game costs one equation.
  • The both-miss weight cancels out: B takes 2 of the 5 decisive parts, and 2/5 + 3/5 = 1 means the game ends with probability 1.
