Four routes into the same game, and all four land on two fifths

the full solving 14 min The companion to the problem page: the counting done, the infinity absorbed, one answer reached four ways.

The one-minute version

  • One throw of a pair totals 9 in 4 of the 36 ordered cells and 4 in 3 of them, so A hits with probability 1/9 and B with 1/12.
  • The answer is P(B wins) = 2/5 = 0.4, and A wins with 3/5. The two add to exactly 1, so the game ends with probability 1 despite having no last round.
  • Four routes get there and none needs the others: the axioms and a geometric series, the set picture of one exchange, a recursion from conditioning, and a count of 1,296 cells.
  • The route to write in an exam is the recursion: one line of setup, one of algebra. The route worth understanding is the set picture, which makes the answer obvious before any algebra.

1. The two numbers the whole game runs on

One throw of the pair is one cell of the six-by-six grid of ordered pairs: what the first die shows, and what the second shows. All 36 cells are equally likely, which is why the grid is the right sample space and the eleven sums are not.

  1. Count the cells that total 9. The first die can be 3, 4, 5 or 6, and the second is then forced to 6, 5, 4 or 3. No 1 or 2 can be rescued by any face. That is 4 cells.
  2. Turn the count into a probability. Every cell has probability 1/36, so p = 4/36 = 1/9. Read p as: the chance one throw by A totals nine.
  3. Count the cells that total 4. (1,3), (2,2) and (3,1). That is 3 cells, so q = 3/36 = 1/12. Read q as: the chance one throw by B totals four.
  4. Name the two misses. By the complement rule, a consequence of the normalisation and additivity axioms, A misses with probability 1 - p = 8/9 and B with 1 - q = 11/12.

2. Route one: the axioms, and nothing but the axioms

The game has no last round, so "B wins" is not a finite union of anything. The axioms handle that directly: countable additivity says the probability of a countable union of disjoint events is the sum of their probabilities. So the work is to write "B wins" as such a union, and add.

Let Bk be the event that B wins on exchange k + 1, after k fruitless exchanges, for k = 0, 1, 2 and so on. They are disjoint, because B wins on exactly one identified exchange, and their union is the whole event "B wins".

P(B wins) = ∑k ≥ 0 (1 - p) · q · rk,   where r = (1 - p)(1 - q) Read as: add one term for every k from 0 upwards. p is A's chance of totalling nine on one throw, q is B's chance of totalling four, r the chance a whole exchange ends with both missing. rk prices the k fruitless exchanges first, (1 - p) A missing on the deciding one, q B hitting on it. ∑ ("sigma") means "add up what follows".
  1. Price one term. The throws are independent, so the multiplication rule chains them: k fruitless exchanges, an A miss, then a B hit is rk × (1 - p) × q.
  2. Put the numbers in. (1 - p)q = (8/9)(1/12) = 8/108 = 2/27, and r = (8/9)(11/12) = 88/108 = 22/27.
  3. Sum the geometric series. With |r| < 1 the sum of rk is 1/(1 - r), and 1 - r = 5/27.
  4. Divide. P(B wins) = (2/27) ÷ (5/27) = 2/5 = 0.4.

3. Route two: the set picture, and a ratio of areas

Draw one exchange as a square of area 1. A's throw splits it left from right, and inside the right-hand part B's throw splits it top from bottom. Three disjoint regions covering the square: a partition, which is the object the law of total probability is stated over.

An honest note on the drawing. A two-circle Venn is wrong here: the three endings are mutually exclusive, so the circles would be disjoint and carry nothing the words did not. What matters is relative size, so the regions are drawn to area.

  1. Measure the three regions. A wins with area p = 1/9 = 3/27, B with (1 - p)q = 2/27, nobody with r = 22/27. They sum to 27/27, as a partition must.
  2. Justify discarding the grey. Conditioned on the grey region, the position is the starting position, so B's chance of eventually winning is the same unknown. The grey carries no information about the winner.
  3. Renormalise what is left. The remaining area is 3/27 + 2/27 = 5/27, and B holds 2/27 of it. 2/27 ÷ 5/27 = 2/5.
  4. Say it in one sentence. Given an exchange is decisive, B wins it with probability 2/5, and the first decisive exchange decides the game. So P(B wins) = 2/5 = 0.4.

4. Route three: a recursion that contains its own answer

Write β for what is asked: B's chance of winning from a position where A is about to throw and nobody has scored. Slice the first exchange into the same three cases and apply the law of total probability. Two answer at once. The third gives back β, because the position after a fruitless exchange is the starting position.

β = (1 - p)q + (1 - p)(1 - q) · β Read as: B's chance of winning equals B's chance of winning on this very exchange, plus the chance the exchange settles nothing times B's chance from the position it hands back. β ("beta") is that probability, p A's chance of totalling nine, q B's of totalling four. The third case, A hitting, contributes nothing: B has lost.
  1. Substitute the two coefficients. β = 2/27 + (22/27)β.
  2. Collect the β terms. β - (22/27)β = 2/27, so (5/27)β = 2/27.
  3. Divide. β = (2/27) ÷ (5/27) = 2/5 = 0.4.
  4. Check the other player. With α = P(A wins), the same slicing gives α = p + rα, so α = (3/27) ÷ (5/27) = 3/5. And 2/5 + 3/5 = 1, so nobody-ever-wins has probability 0.

5. Route four: count 1,296 equally likely cells

Give B a throw on every exchange, even where A has won, and ignore it there. That changes nothing about who wins and buys a uniform sample space: one exchange is an ordered pair of throws, 36 × 36 = 1,296 equally likely cells.

  1. Check the count closes. 144 + 96 + 1,056 = 1,296. Every cell is in exactly one row, which is what makes the rows a partition.
  2. Keep only the decisive cells. 144 + 96 = 240 cells settle the game on this exchange. The other 1,056 hand it back unchanged.
  3. Take B's share of them. 96 ÷ 240 = 2/5 = 0.4, since 96 and 240 share a factor of 48.

This route mentions no series, no limit and no recursion. It is whole-number arithmetic, and it is what to reach for when the algebra feels slippery.

6. The four routes, side by side

All four reach P(B wins) = 2/5 = 0.4, and they are not four spellings of one argument: the first sums infinitely many terms, the second sums none, the third solves a linear equation, the fourth divides two integers. Agreement across methods sharing that little is strong evidence nothing slipped.

Which to write for two marks: the recursion. It states the structure, prices it and solves it in four lines a marker can follow. Keep the set picture in your head as you write: it tells you the answer is a ratio of the two decisive weights before you solve anything.

One route that genuinely does not work here: symmetry. It is the classic short proof for an alternating game, and it fails here because nothing about the players matches: A aims at a 4-cell target, B at a 3-cell target, and A throws first. No exchange of labels leaves the game unchanged, so 2/5 against 3/5 is arithmetic rather than balance. Independence alone is no route either: "B wins" is not an intersection of per-throw events, so factorisation has nothing to work on.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the set picture, why can the both-miss region simply be discarded?
The grey region hands back the identical position, so the ratio between A's and B's chances inside it is the ratio we are already computing, and it therefore tilts the answer neither way. Its size is irrelevant to that argument, and at 22/27 it is in fact the largest region on the square. The game has no bound on its length at all. And B wins after a fruitless exchange constantly - that is most of how B wins.
Which pair of per-throw probabilities does this game actually run on?
Totalling 9 takes 4 of the 36 ordered cells and totalling 4 takes 3 of them, so p = 1/9 and q = 1/12. The second option has copied the target totals themselves into the numerators, which is the commonest slip on this question. The third counts over the eleven possible sums, and those are not equally likely, so the division is meaningless. The fourth simply miscounts both target sets.
Change B's target from 4 to 7. Which route needs the least rewriting?
The recursion is written in p and q, so a new target changes the two numbers substituted at the last step and nothing structural. The count has to re-tally the cells and redo both products in the table. The set picture has to be redrawn to the new areas before it says anything. And the series has to be rebuilt and its ratio rechecked for convergence, which is the most work of the four.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Same two dice, same two targets, one change: B throws first. A still wins on a total of 9 and B still wins on a total of 4.

Find the probability that B wins now, and say in one sentence what the change was worth to B.

Hint

Nothing about the two per-throw probabilities changes. Only the order of the two boxes inside one exchange does, so write the recursion for the new first mover and read off the new numerator.

Show the worked solution
  1. Rewrite the exchange. B throws, then A throws only if B missed. B wins the exchange with probability q = 1/12, A wins it with probability (1 - q)p = (11/12)(1/9) = 11/108, and both miss with probability r = (11/12)(8/9) = 22/27, exactly as before.
  2. Write the recursion. β′ = q + rβ′ = 1/12 + (22/27)β′.
  3. Solve it. (5/27)β′ = 1/12, so β′ = (1/12)(27/5) = 27/60 = 9/20 = 0.45.
  4. Say what it bought. Throwing first lifted B from 0.40 to 0.45, a gain of 0.05, or one extra win in every twenty games.

Sanity check. The answer must be larger than 2/5, because moving first can only help and it cannot help so much that the weaker target beats the stronger one: 0.4 < 0.45 < 0.5, as it must be. A's new probability is 11/20, and 9/20 + 11/20 = 1.

Your teacher. The habit worth taking from this page is not any one route. It is solving a problem twice by methods that share nothing, which is the only self-marking a solitary learner has: two agreeing routes catch almost every slip, and a disagreement says exactly where to look. A process that either terminates or hands itself back unchanged is also the shape behind the geometric distribution on page 0086, so you already know four ways into it.
