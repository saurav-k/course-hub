Read the conditional definition backwards and it chains

10 min working One rearrangement of Part 4's definition, applied again and again, prices every path through a random process.

The one-minute version

  • Part 4 defined P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). Multiply both sides by P(B) and the definition reads backwards: P(A ∩ B) = P(B) × P(A | B).
  • Nothing stops you chaining the move: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B | A) × P(C | A ∩ B), and onward for as many events as you have.
  • On a probability tree, the chain rule says one short thing: a path's probability is the product of its edge probabilities.

1. The definition, read backwards

Part 4 defined conditional probability with a fraction. Algebra that a school student could do turns the same line into a product:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) becomes P(A ∩ B) = P(B) × P(A | B) P(A | B) is the probability of A given B; P(A ∩ B) is the probability that both happen; P(B) is the plain probability of B. The second form is the first multiplied through by P(B), nothing more.

The intersection is symmetric in its two events, so the same overlap can also be priced the other way round, which is exactly how the notes write it:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A) = P(B) × P(A | B) The multiplication rule. Read the first form aloud: the probability that A and B both happen is the probability that A happens, times the probability that B happens given that A happened. Either event is allowed to be the "given".

This is one idea, not two: it is the definition of conditioning, solved for the overlap. Its value is practical. You often know the probability of a first stage and can reason out a conditional probability for the second stage given the first, while the overlap itself, the compound event, is hard to price directly. The rule assembles the hard quantity from the easy ones.

2. The chain rule for three events

Now keep going. To price three events happening together, price the first, price the second given the first, price the third given the first two, and multiply. The notes write it exactly this way, and it is just the multiplication rule applied twice with the overlap treated as one event:

P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B | A) × P(C | A ∩ B) A, B and C are three events. P(C | A ∩ B) reads "the probability of C given that both A and B happened", and it is defined exactly as in Part 4, with the overlap A ∩ B playing the role of the given event. The last factor needs P(A ∩ B) > 0 to make sense.
P(A1 ∩ ... ∩ An) = P(A1) × P(A2 | A1) × ... × P(An | A1 ∩ ... ∩ An−1) The general chain rule for n events. Each factor conditions on all the events before it. The notes write the tail as a product running from i = 2 up to n of P(Ai given A1 through Ai−1), which is this line in compact notation.

The notes' example is a coin tossed three times, with A the event that toss 1 lands heads, B that toss 2 lands heads, and C that toss 3 does. For a fair coin, the first factor is P(A) = 1/2. Given heads came first, the coin has no memory and the second factor is P(B | A) = 1/2. Given heads came twice, the third factor is P(C | A ∩ B) = 1/2. The chain rule multiplies them:

  1. First factor. P(A) = 1/2: a fair coin lands heads half the time.
  2. Second factor. P(B | A) = 1/2: knowing toss 1 landed heads leaves toss 2 an untouched fair toss.
  3. Third factor. P(C | A ∩ B) = 1/2: knowing two heads landed leaves toss 3 an untouched fair toss.
  4. Multiply. P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8. This evaluation is this course's, not the notes', which state the formula and leave the factors to the reader; the three halves come from the fair-coin assumption.

Why is the third factor a conditional at all? Because it is Part 4's shrinkage picture, one more time. Learning A ∩ B happened shrinks the eight-outcome world to the two outcomes that begin with heads, heads, and C's footprint in that shrunken world is one outcome out of two:

Where the notes are looseThe notes state the three-event chain rule and then immediately illustrate it with the three-toss setup, writing the factors but not evaluating them. The evaluation above, three halves multiplying to 1/8, is this course's, and it quietly assumes the three tosses are independent fair tosses, a word this lecture has not defined yet. Part 8 defines independence and returns to exactly this example to show what the assumption is doing.

Check yourself

The chain rule prices P(A ∩ B ∩ C) as which of these?
The chain rule conditions each new event on everything already assumed: P(A), then P(B | A), then P(C | A ∩ B). The first option multiplies the plain singles, which is a different and much stronger claim called independence, arriving in Part 8. The third is the start of inclusion-exclusion from Part 2, which prices a union, not an intersection. The fourth conditions on the wrong things in the wrong order; the given-event set grows as you move right along the chain.
In the three-toss example, why does the factor P(C | A ∩ B) stay at 1/2?
Conditioning on A ∩ B shrinks the world to tosses that began with two heads, and inside that shrunken world the third toss is still an untouched fair toss, which is what "no memory" means. The first option is true of the toss but does not address the conditioning; the question is what the two heads do to the third, and the answer is nothing. Disjointness would mean C and A ∩ B cannot co-occur, which is false here since HHH is in all three. And coins have no store of luck to use up; that intuition is exactly the gambler's fallacy this example is built to kill.
Your teacher. Take a process you actually own, a deploy followed by a rollback, or a page view followed by a purchase, and draw its tree with two stages. Price each edge with your best judgement, multiply along a path, and you have produced a joint probability that no dashboard directly reports. This is the everyday use of today's page: joint probabilities are usually assembled from conditionals, never estimated directly. A serving request is the same shape - accepted, routed, then generated - and the end-to-end success rate is the product of the stage conditionals along its path, which is why one flaky stage collapses the whole path's price.
