Lecture 4 · Part 6 of 12 · the n-event definition

Independence for many events is not one equation, it is one equation per subset

10 min a definition with teeth Two events need one check. Three need four. Eight need two hundred and forty-seven, and the lecture is explicit that the biggest one is not the whole condition.

The one-minute version

  • The definition, as the notes give it: A1, ..., An are independent if for every subset I0 of the index set, the probability of their intersection equals the product of their individual probabilities.
  • Every subset. Not just the whole collection, and not just the pairs.
  • The notes call out the three-event case by name: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A)P(B)P(C) is only one of the required equations, not the whole condition. The other three are the pairs.
  • Both directions of that trap are real, and each has its own worked counter-example: TA Session 1, Part 7 shows all three pairs holding while the triple fails, and section 4 of this page shows the triple holding while a pair fails.

1. The definition, symbol by symbol

for every I0 ⊆ {1, 2, ..., n}:   P( ∩i ∈ I0 Ai ) = ∏i ∈ I0 P(Ai) {1, 2, ..., n} is the index set: just the labels of the events, not the events themselves. I0 is any subset of those labels - it might be {1, 3}, or {2, 5, 7}, or the whole thing. The symbol ∩i ∈ I0 Ai means "intersect the events whose labels are in I0", so for I0 = {1, 3} it is A1 ∩ A3. The symbol ∏, a capital pi, means "multiply everything that follows", exactly as ∑ means add. So the whole line reads: however you pick some of these events, the chance they all happen together is the product of their separate chances.

Subsets of size zero and one are free: the empty intersection convention makes the first trivially true, and a single event's probability trivially equals itself. So the content lives in the subsets of size two and above.

For three events A, B and C, that is four equations, and the notes list the situation explicitly:

SubsetEquation that must holdSize
{A, B}P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)a pair
{A, C}P(A ∩ C) = P(A) × P(C)a pair
{B, C}P(B ∩ C) = P(B) × P(C)a pair
{A, B, C}P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B) × P(C)the triple

All four. The lecture's own remark is that the fourth line is one of the required equations rather than the requirement itself, and that is the sentence this page exists to make concrete.

2. How many equations, in general

The lecture does not count the equations. This course does, because the count is what makes the definition feel like a definition rather than a formality.

  1. Count all subsets. Each of the n labels is either in I0 or out of it, independently of the others, so there are 2n subsets in total. That is the basic counting rule from later in this same lecture, applied to n binary choices.
  2. Discard the free ones. The empty subset is one of them, and the n singletons are n more.
  3. What is left. 2n − n − 1 equations carry real content.
  4. Sanity-check it on the cases you know. For n = 2 that is 4 − 2 − 1 = 1, matching Part 5 where one product settled the table. For n = 3 it is 8 − 3 − 1 = 4, matching the four rows above.
Why this matters to an engineerReliability arithmetic runs on this definition. "Three replicas each fail with probability 0.001, so all three fail with probability 10-9" is the n-event product equation, asserted rather than checked. It is true only if all four equations hold for those three events, and a shared rack, a shared network segment or a shared deploy makes at least one of them false. The number that comes out is then wrong by orders of magnitude, and nothing in the calculation warns you. Part 7 is about the standard repair for exactly this situation.

4. Triple product holding while a pair fails

This course's constructionThe notes state that the triple equation is not the whole condition and leave it there. The eight-outcome example below is this course's own, built to make that statement concrete. It is not from the board.

Take a sample space of eight equally likely outcomes, numbered 1 to 8, so every outcome has probability 1/8 and the probability of any event is its size over eight - the finite uniform model of Part 8. Define three events:

  • A = {1, 2, 3, 4}, so P(A) = 4/8 = 1/2.
  • B = {1, 2, 3, 5}, so P(B) = 4/8 = 1/2.
  • C = {1, 5, 6, 7}, so P(C) = 4/8 = 1/2.

Now the four equations, one at a time.

  1. The triple. A ∩ B ∩ C = {1}, a single outcome, so P = 1/8. The product is (1/2) × (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/8. It holds.
  2. The pair A and B. A ∩ B = {1, 2, 3}, three outcomes, so P = 3/8. The product is (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4 = 2/8. It fails, and by a wide margin.
  3. The pair A and C. A ∩ C = {1}, so P = 1/8, against a product of 1/4. It fails too.
  4. The pair B and C. B ∩ C = {1, 5}, so P = 2/8 = 1/4, against a product of 1/4. This one holds, which shows the failures are not uniform.

So the largest equation, the one that looks most like a statement about all three events at once, is satisfied, while two of the three pairs are not. Anybody who had checked only the triple would have concluded that A, B and C are independent and would then have been free to multiply any of their probabilities together, getting the wrong answer for two of the three pairs.

5. Check yourself

Four events are claimed to be independent. How many product equations does the definition require?
The count is 2 to the fourth, minus 4, minus 1, which is 11: six pairs, four triples, and the single quadruple. Six alone would be pairwise independence, which is strictly weaker, and TA Session 1 shows why. Four equations would be one per event, and those are the free singletons that say nothing. And one equation over the full overlap is precisely the trap this page's section 4 breaks.
In section 4's example, what does the failure of the A-and-B equation cost you in practice?
The concrete cost is that one specific product is wrong: 1/4 instead of 3/8, an error of fifty percent on a quantity you thought you had. The triple holding is exactly what makes the failure dangerous rather than harmless, because it is the reassurance that stops you checking. Not every product is barred either - B and C do satisfy their equation, so that one pair may be multiplied. And the uniform sample space is a fixed property of the setup that no dependence between events can disturb.
Your teacher. The useful reflex from this page is small: whenever you see probabilities being multiplied, count how many events are in the product and ask whether anybody checked 2n minus n minus 1 equations, or whether independence was assumed because the events felt unrelated. Almost always it was assumed. Sometimes that assumption is fine and worth stating out loud; the failures are the times nobody noticed there was an assumption at all.
