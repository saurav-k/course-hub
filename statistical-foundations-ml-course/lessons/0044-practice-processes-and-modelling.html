Sheet problem 1 - build two worlds with opposite signs

The question, in plain English. Part (a): exhibit a sample space and two events A and B for which the probability of the intersection is strictly bigger than the product of the individual probabilities. Part (b): exhibit a space and two events for which the intersection is strictly smaller than the product. The sheet adds a demand in words: explain the sample space and the events properly, because only assigning numerical values satisfying the conditions will not suffice. The construction is the answer; the numbers merely witness it.

(a)  P(A ∩ B) > P(A)·P(B)      (b)  P(A ∩ B) < P(A)·P(B) Read as: A and B are two events in a sample space you get to choose. P(A ∩ B) is the probability that both occur; P(A)·P(B) is the product of their individual probabilities - the number the two would obey if they were independent. Part (a) wants a pair pushed strictly above that product, part (b) strictly below, and each with its space and events explained.

What it is really testing. Whether independence, defined on Independence as a numerical coincidence between two quantities, can be steered from both sides by hand. The raw materials are the earliest pages of the course: The sample space and Events are subsets say what a construction even is, and A discrete space worked is the model for how to write one up. The problem looks like a calculation and is really a design exercise.

Where the wording bitesParts (a) and (b) are two separate constructions - one space cannot generally do both jobs at once. Second, the sheet's own warning is the trap: two numbers satisfying each inequality, with no space and no events, score nothing even if correct. Third, "strictly" matters - the laziest symmetric sketch produces exact equality for one of the signs, which satisfies neither part. Fourth, both events need positive probability; a zero-probability event trivialises a part.

How to start. Pick the smallest space that can carry a mood - a coin flipped twice is already rich enough. Let the arrangement of outcomes, not the arithmetic, create the sign. Then verify both sides in your own space, and write the space, the events and the explanation in full sentences. Stop before hunting a cleverer example; a small honest one you can explain beats a large one you cannot.

Weight. The sheet prints 2 marks per part, though the problem sits in the practice half and nothing is submitted. Difficulty: looks routine, and the discipline of explaining the space is where the work actually is.

Sheet problem 6 - every teacher must appear at least once

The question, in plain English. A course is taught by four instructors. Before every lecture, all four draw lots, and one of the four is chosen at random to teach that day - a fresh draw each day, from the same four names. Across N classes, what is the probability that every one of the four instructors has taught at least once? And how does the answer generalise when there are k instructors instead of four?

What it is really testing. Whether you define the sample space before reaching for a formula, and whether "at least one of each" flips cleanly into the complement "some name never appears". The space-of-runs view is The sample space applied to a whole sequence of draws; the flip is complement thinking from What the axioms force; and the pricing of "some name is missing" is Inclusion-exclusion over the four instructors, with the union bound of The union bound and Bonferroni as the sanity check on direction.

Where the wording bitesThe draws are with replacement across days - the same four names enter the hat every time, which is what "before every lecture, the instructors draw lots" means here; a write-up should state that reading once. Second, "all four taught at least once" needs N to be at least four to be possible at all, and any formula worth its ink says so when N is smaller. Third, the generalisation to k instructors should fall out of the argument for free - if you find you hard-coded the number four anywhere, the generalisation is telling you where.

How to start. Define the sample space as the ordered sequence of N names drawn, and say why its points are equally likely. Name the complement: "at least one instructor never teaches", which is a union over instructors of "instructor i is absent from all N classes". Price one instructor's absence, then let inclusion-exclusion collect the overlaps. Keep every step symbolic in k from the start, and the generalisation is already done. Stop once the complement is priced in symbols; the sheet's arithmetic is the tidy-up.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: moderate; the modelling is one decision, and the rest is an inclusion-exclusion you have run before.

Sheet problem 8 - a dice game with a fork in it

The question, in plain English. A and B each play with a die. A rolls once and is assigned the number he rolled. B also rolls once and is assigned the number she rolled - unless that roll is a one, in which case she rolls a second time and is assigned whatever the second roll shows, even if it is another one. The two assigned numbers are compared: the higher one wins, and a tie goes to A. What is the probability that A wins the game?

What it is really testing. Whether you can condition cleanly on one player's outcome and characterise the other player's distribution through a rule. Conditioning on A's assigned value is Conditional probability; slicing B's behaviour into its two branches is The law of total probability; and the space itself - pairs of assigned values, with B's side carrying the fork - is a construction in the sense of The sample space. Nothing here needs a tool the course has not taught; it needs the rules read exactly.

Where the wording bitesThe reroll replaces the first roll even when the second roll is also a one - B does not roll again, and a one is a live final value for B. Second, ties go to A, so "B does not beat A" is not the same as "B wins"; the tie region belongs to A. Third, the comparison is between assigned values, and B's assigned value comes from a different mechanism than A's - treating the two dice as symmetric is the silent error. Fourth, the sheet's phrasing "A gets is assigned" is a typo for "A is assigned"; the restatement above is the intended rule.

How to start. Condition on A's assigned value - six cases, each with probability one sixth. For a fixed value of A, work out which of B's possible assignments lose, which tie and which win, remembering B's fork: her value is an ordinary roll unless a one forced a reroll. The answer is then the total probability over A's six cases. Stop once the per-case comparison is set up as a sentence; the counting inside each case is the exercise.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: moderate; no new machinery, and all the risk lives in the fork and the tie.

Sheet problem 11 - the urn that grows by what it shows

The question, in plain English. An urn starts with one black ball and one brown ball. Each round: draw one ball at random, note its colour, put it back, and add one more ball of that same colour. After the first round the urn holds three balls, and the rule repeats - N rounds in total, leaving N plus two balls in the urn. Find the probability that the urn ends up with exactly k black balls.

This one has a name: the Polya urnThe draw-it-then-reinforce-it mechanism is a classic model called a Polya urn, and it is famous precisely because intuition misfires on it. The obvious guess is that the black share should drift towards some fixed value and the final counts should cluster around an even split, because the urn started even. Refuse the guess. The sheet asks for the exact probability of each final count k, and the process's real behaviour is stranger than the obvious story - which is the entire educational point of the problem. What you may rely on is bookkeeping: the black count starts at 1, each round adds exactly one ball of one colour, so the final count runs from 1 up to N plus 1, and every value in between is reachable.

What it is really testing. Whether the multiplication rule can chain conditionals through a process whose probabilities change at every step. The chance of any full history is a product of conditional chances, one per round, exactly as The multiplication rule sets out; and each round's conditional chance is read straight off the urn's current contents, in the sense of Conditional probability. The modelling decision - track the urn by its pair of counts - is the whole setup; after that the sheet is careful bookkeeping.

How to start. Define the state of the urn after round t as its pair of counts. Write the conditional chance that round t plus one adds a black ball, in terms of the state. Then price one complete history - a specific sequence of colours - as a product of these conditional chances, and notice that many different histories can land on the same final count k. The answer for a given k is the sum over all histories that reach it. Stop there: the phrase "sum over all histories that reach k" is the destination, and finding a clean way to carry out that sum is the problem's real work, which is yours.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: hard, and honestly so - the mechanism is one line to learn and the bookkeeping punishes every shortcut.

Sheet problem 12 - best of five, stopped early

The question, in plain English. A tennis match is best of five sets: the first player to win three sets wins the match, and play stops at that instant. The players are evenly matched, meaning each set is a fair coin flip between them, independent set to set. Find the probability that the loser of the match won exactly k sets - and the answer should cover each way the match can end.

What it is really testing. Whether you respect a stopping rule when counting. The machinery is small: equally likely complete sequences, priced by the independence of sets through The multiplication rule, and enumerated with the systematic-counting habits of Geometry and counting. The modelling decision is that an outcome is a whole stopped match - a sequence of set results that ends the instant someone reaches three - and half the traps live in forgetting it.

Where the wording bitesThe match stops early: a scoreline is only an outcome if no earlier prefix of it already ended the match, so sequences that wander past a third win are not things that can happen. Second, the final set of any legal outcome must have gone to the eventual winner - a stopped match cannot continue after its deciding set. Third, the loser cannot have won three sets, by definition of "loser"; k has a short list of possible values and part of the check is noticing them. Fourth, "evenly matched" makes every complete legal sequence of the same length equally likely - say so explicitly once, then count.

How to start. Draw the stopping structure - the picture above is exactly it. For each possible value of k, list the terminal scorelines that have the loser on k sets. Fix the last set of each such scoreline to the winner, and count the arrangements of the earlier sets - this is where a count-the-arrangements reflex from the counting pages pays off. Each complete sequence is then priced the same way. Stop once one scoreline is fully priced by hand; the pattern across scorelines is the pattern the sheet wants you to find.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: moderate; the stopping rule does the thinking, and the arithmetic is small once the scorelines are listed.

Sheet problem 17 - a random graph, with a warning

The question, in plain English. The Erdos-Renyi model G(n, p) builds a random graph on n nodes - in a social network, one node per person - where every pair of nodes is joined by an edge with probability p, independently of every other edge. Let Bn be the event that a graph generated this way has at least one isolated node, a node with no edges at all. Two things are asked: show that P(Bn) is at most n times (1 − p) to the power n − 1; and conclude that for any ε > 0, if p is chosen as pn = (1 + ε) times the natural logarithm of n, over n, then P(Bn) tends to 0 as n grows without bound.

Past this course so farThe first half of this problem is course material: pricing one node's isolation uses edge independence, and collecting the n nodes uses the union bound - both taught. The conclusion is not: it needs a limit argument about n times a power whose exponent involves the natural logarithm, and limits of that shape - logarithms racing powers - have not been taught in this course. If that final step feels foreign, the gap is the course's, not yours. Do the first half with full confidence and treat the limit as a guided stretch.

What it is really testing. The union bound as a reflex for "at least one of many", from The union bound and Bonferroni, plus the independence of edges stated in the problem and defined on Independence. The sheet even prints the inequality it wants, so this is not a guessing game: the task is the argument, and the honest difficulty is concentrated in the limit step flagged above.

Where the wording bites"Isolated" means no edges at all touching the node - not "few edges" or "not in the biggest group". Second, the n minus 1 in the target bound is the count of potential edges at one node - every other node is a candidate neighbour, whether or not the edge appears. Third, the edges are independent of one another, which is stated in the problem and is what lets a node's isolation be priced as one compound event. Fourth, the limit claim holds for the specific choice of p printed there, with the ε strictly positive - the bound alone does not deliver it without that substitution.

How to start. Fix one node and define its isolation as a compound event: n minus 1 specific edges all absent. Price that event using edge independence. Then name the n events "node i is isolated" and let the union bound sweep them into the bound the sheet prints. For the conclusion, substitute the printed p and ask how fast the resulting quantity decays as n grows - that is the flagged step, so budget patience for it rather than alarm. Stop once the sweep is written; the decay argument is the stretch the warning describes.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: hard overall, but split - the bound is a routine union-bound rep, and the limit is genuinely past the course so far.

Sheet problem 18 - the coupon drawer

The question, in plain English. Each pack of Gold Cookies contains one coupon. There are n coupon types, and coupons arrive independently of one another: each new coupon is of type i with probability pi, where the pi across all types sum to one. You have already collected k coupons. Let Ai be the event that at least one of those k coupons is of type i. Three questions, with i and j distinct: (a) find P(Ai); (b) find P(Ai ∪ Aj); (c) find P(Ai | Aj).

P(Ai) = ?,   P(Ai ∪ Aj) = ?,   P(Ai | Aj) = ?   for i ≠ j Read as: Ai is "at least one of the k already-collected coupons has type i". The union asks for "at least one coupon of type i or at least one of type j". The conditional asks for the chance type i appeared, given that type j appeared, and the condition i ≠ j says the two types are different - so the two events are related but not equal.

What it is really testing. The complement habit - "at least one" is priced most easily as one minus "none" - and the definition of conditional probability, both from Conditional probability. The independence across the k draws is the problem's own statement, the pattern defined on Independence; and part (b) is a two-event union, the oldest trick in Inclusion-exclusion. Nothing new is needed; the discipline is keeping the three answers built from the same complement block rather than reinventing each from scratch.

Where the wording bitesThe k coupons are already in hand - the randomness is over what those k draws were, not over a future collection. Second, every draw faces the same type distribution, independently of the others; that is what lets a "none of the k" event be priced by multiplying one draw's dodge chance k times. Third, in part (c) the numerator is the joint event "both types appeared", which is again most easily priced through its complement - neither type appeared. Fourth, i and j are distinct throughout; nowhere do you need the case i equals j, and importing it confuses the conditional.

How to start. For (a), price the complement: all k independent draws dodge type i, and one draw's dodge chance is read straight off pi. For (b), either run inclusion-exclusion over the two events or price "neither type appeared" and complement it - both doors open. For (c), write the conditional definition and notice its numerator is "type i appeared and type j appeared", whose complement is "i missing or j missing". Stop after (a) is set up; the same block then assembles all three, and the assembling is the practice.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: moderate; one complement block, used three times, with the conditional definition as the final turn.

Sheet problem 19 - the three doors, stated formally

The question, in plain English. You are a contestant. Three doors lead to three rooms: two are empty and one holds a Mercedes. You choose one room; a correct guess wins the car. After you choose, the game master opens the door of one of the rooms you did not select, picking one that does not contain the Mercedes. The claim to prove: changing your choice to the remaining closed door improves your chance of winning. Formally, with E1 the event of winning with the initial guess, E2 the event of winning if you change your initial guess, and C the event that a room not containing the Mercedes is revealed - prove that P(E1) is strictly less than P(E2 | C).

This one has a name: the Monty Hall problemThis is the Monty Hall problem, stated with the sheet's own event names, and it is famous for one reason: almost everyone's first instinct is wrong. The instinct says that with one door open and two closed, the car is equally likely behind either closed door - a clean-sounding argument that this problem exists to demolish. So the sheet asks for a proof, not an intuition, and that demand is the whole assignment: write the spaces and events down, condition properly, and let the inequality fall out of the arithmetic rather than out of how you feel about doors. This page will not reveal which side of the inequality the numbers land on - proving it is the point.

What it is really testing. Conditioning as a genuine shrinkage of the sample space, which is the content of Building the conditional space, and the information reading of a reversal that Bayes rule formalises. The modelling decision is to lay out every case of "your initial door" against "where the prize is" before asking either question - the same table-first discipline as Conditioning in practice. The mathematics is first-course level throughout; the difficulty is psychological, which is why the sheet says prove.

Where the wording bitesThe master's behaviour is part of the model: he never opens your door and never opens the prize door, and both rules must appear in your write-up as stated facts, not as vibes. Second, C is the event "a non-prize door you did not pick gets revealed" - read it against the rules just described, because the rules are what make the conditioning meaningful. Third, the claim compares P(E1) with P(E2 | C) - one side is conditional and the other is not, and the asymmetry is deliberate; flattening both to unconditional throws away the problem. Fourth, "the remaining closed door" is well defined only because exactly one door stays closed - worth one sentence.

How to start. Build the case table: your initial door against the prize's door, three rows by three columns, all cases equally likely - that is the sample space, and it is small. For each case, the master's two rules pin down exactly which door he can open, so write that into the table too. Then read E1 off the table, read E2 given C off the same table, and compare. Stop after the table is built and labelled; reading it correctly is the proof, and the sheet is asking for exactly that reading in writing.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: formally medium, psychologically hard - the table is three by three, and the discipline is in believing it over your gut.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing. None of these hands you an answer to any sheet problem.

Sheet problem 1 says numerical values alone will not suffice. What is the actual deliverable?
The sheet asks for the space and the events, explained - the numbers only witness the inequality inside your construction. Two decimals with no home answer nothing about where they came from. A worked example of numbers is exactly the thing the sheet rules out. And a simulation estimates rather than exhibits, which cannot satisfy a strict inequality claim at all.
The urn of sheet problem 11 - draw, return, add a twin - has a standard name. What is it?
Draw-then-reinforce is Polya's urn, and its fame rests on how badly intuition fares against it - which is why the sheet asks for exact probabilities per final count. A Galton board drops balls through pegs and models nothing like reinforcement. Martingale is a property some processes have, not this mechanism's name. And the urn's state is a pair of counts, not a composition sequence, so the last name mislabels the state space.
Beyond an answer, what does the sheet demand for the three-door game of problem 19?
The sheet says prove, and the demand is deliberate - instinct is famously unreliable on exactly this problem, so a feeling, however confidently argued, is not the assignment. A simulation would estimate the sides of the inequality without proving anything. A vote measures the instinct, which is the thing under suspicion. The proof is the deliverable.
Sheet problem 17 is flagged as reaching past this course. Which ingredient is the overreach?
The limit step - n times a power with a logarithm in the exponent, tending to zero - needs machinery the course has not taught, which is what the warning flags. The union bound is Lecture 3 material. Edge independence is stated inside the problem itself. And counting a node's potential edges is bookkeeping from the setup, not an argument.
Your teacher. These eight live or die at the modelling step, so if one will not start, close the sheet and write out only the sample space or the process rule in words - no probabilities. If the space is right and it still will not move, rework the linked lesson's own example, then ask for a smaller cousin of the same shape. Ask for cousins, never for answers; the asking-without-the-answer is the homework.
