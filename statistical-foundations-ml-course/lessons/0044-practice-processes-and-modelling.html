Before you start: The sample space and The multiplication rule.

The one-minute version

  • On every problem here the decisive move is modelling: deciding what an outcome is. After that the tool is one you already own.
  • Problem 11 is the Polya urn, and the final count turns out uniform - the guess that it clusters near an even split is wrong.
  • Problem 19 is the three-door problem, proved from a nine-cell table rather than argued.
  • Every problem below is solved in full, one method each, in the practice section.
Practice, not submittedThese belong to the practice half of Practice Set 1. The four graded problems have their own pages and are due Sunday 6 September for 15 marks.

A process that remembers its own history

Problem 11's urn changes its own odds at every round, so no single draw has a fixed probability. The only honest object is the whole history, priced as a product of conditional chances - and the punchline is that the histories collapse into a very simple answer.

Where the model is the whole answer

Problem 19 needs no machinery past a table of nine equally likely cells. What it needs is the game master's two rules written down as facts: he never opens your door, and he never opens the prize door. A proof that omits either rule is proving a different game.

Past this course so farThe second half of problem 17 needs a limit of the shape "n times a power whose exponent carries a logarithm", and limits of that kind have not been taught here. The solution below uses the standard inequality 1 − p ≤ e−p to get there. If that step feels foreign, the gap is the course's and not yours.

Sheet problem 1 says numerical values alone will not suffice. What is the actual deliverable?
The sheet asks for the space and the events, explained - the numbers only witness the inequality inside your construction. Two decimals with no home answer nothing about where they came from. A worked example of numbers is exactly the thing the sheet rules out. And a simulation estimates rather than exhibits, which cannot settle a strict inequality claim.
The urn of sheet problem 11 - draw, return, add a twin - has a standard name. What is it?
Draw-then-reinforce is Polya's urn, and its fame rests on how badly intuition fares against it - the final count is uniform, not clustered. A Galton board drops balls through pegs and models nothing like reinforcement. Martingale is a property some processes have, not this mechanism's name. And the urn's state is a pair of counts, not a composition sequence.
Sheet problem 17 is flagged as reaching past this course. Which ingredient is the overreach?
The limit step - n times a power with a logarithm in the exponent, tending to zero - needs machinery the course has not taught, which is what the warning flags. The union bound is Lecture 3 material. Edge independence is stated inside the problem itself. And counting a node's potential edges is bookkeeping from the setup, not an argument.

Sheet 1 - two worlds with opposite signsfoundation

Part (a): exhibit a sample space and two events A and B for which the probability of the intersection is strictly bigger than the product of the individual probabilities. Part (b): exhibit a space and two events for which the intersection is strictly smaller than the product. The sheet adds a demand in words: explain the sample space and the events properly, because only assigning numerical values satisfying the conditions will not suffice.

(a)  P(A ∩ B) > P(A)·P(B)      (b)  P(A ∩ B) < P(A)·P(B) Read as: P(A)·P(B) is the number the two would obey if they were independent. Part (a) wants a pair pushed strictly above that product, part (b) strictly below, and each with its space and events explained.
Hint

Three equally likely outcomes is enough for both. Avoid four outcomes with two-element events - that sketch lands on exact equality.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix one space for both parts. Let Ω = {1, 2, 3} model a single roll of a three-sided die, with each outcome having probability 1/3. This is a legitimate probability space: the three probabilities are non-negative and sum to 1, and every subset is an event.
  2. Part (a), choose the events. A = {1, 2}, the roll is 1 or 2. B = {1}, the roll is 1. Both have positive probability, so neither part is trivialised.
  3. Price them. P(A) = 2/3 and P(B) = 1/3, so P(A)·P(B) = 2/9. A ∩ B = {1}, so P(A ∩ B) = 1/3 = 3/9.
  4. Compare. 3/9 > 2/9, so P(A ∩ B) > P(A)·P(B) strictly. The reason in words: B is contained in A, so B occurring guarantees A - the strongest possible positive association, and it must beat the independent product.
  5. Part (b), choose different events in the same space. A = {1, 2} again, and B = {2, 3}, the roll is 2 or 3. Both again have positive probability.
  6. Price them. P(A) = 2/3 and P(B) = 2/3, so P(A)·P(B) = 4/9. A ∩ B = {2}, so P(A ∩ B) = 1/3 = 3/9.
  7. Compare. 3/9 < 4/9, so P(A ∩ B) < P(A)·P(B) strictly. The reason in words: A and B are two two-element events that share only one outcome, so they overlap less than chance alone would arrange.

Sanity check. Independence would require P(A ∩ B) to equal 2/9 in part (a) and 4/9 in part (b), and in this space every intersection has probability 0, 1/3 or 2/3 - none of which is 2/9 or 4/9, so strictness is guaranteed rather than lucky. Contrast the tempting four-outcome sketch: two fair coin flips with A = "first is heads" and B = "both match" gives P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 = P(A)P(B) exactly, satisfying neither part.

Sheet 6 - every teacher appearsworking

A course is taught by four instructors. Before every lecture, all four draw lots, and one of the four is chosen at random to teach that day - a fresh draw each day, from the same four names. Across N classes, what is the probability that every one of the four instructors has taught at least once? And how does the answer generalise to k instructors?

Hint

An outcome is the whole sequence of N names. Complement the event and run inclusion-exclusion over the instructors who are missing.

Show the worked solution
  1. Define the sample space. An outcome is the ordered sequence of N names drawn, one per class. With k instructors there are kN such sequences, and independent uniform draws make them equally likely.
  2. Complement the event. "Every instructor teaches at least once" fails exactly when some instructor is absent from all N classes. Let Mi be the event "instructor i never appears". The bad event is M1 ∪ ... ∪ Mk.
  3. Price one absence. Each class dodges instructor i with probability (k − 1)/k, independently, so P(Mi) = ((k − 1)/k)N.
  4. Price a joint absence. For any j named instructors, all N classes must be filled from the remaining k − j, so that intersection has probability ((k − j)/k)N. There are C(k, j) such choices.
  5. Run inclusion-exclusion. P(some instructor absent) = ∑j=1k (−1)j+1 C(k, j) ((k − j)/k)N.
  6. Complement back. P(all k appear) = ∑j=0k (−1)j C(k, j) ((k − j)/k)N, the j = 0 term supplying the 1.
  7. Substitute k = 4. P(all four appear) = 1 − 4(3/4)N + 6(2/4)N − 4(1/4)N, the final j = 4 term vanishing because (0/4)N = 0 for N ≥ 1.

Sanity check. Put N = 4: the formula gives 1 − 4(0.31640625) + 6(0.0625) − 4(0.00390625) = 0.09375, and the direct count agrees - only the 4! = 24 orderings of four distinct names work, out of 44 = 256, and 24/256 = 0.09375. Put N = 3: 1 − 108/64 + 48/64 − 4/64 = 0, which is right, since three classes cannot cover four instructors. Nothing in the derivation hard-coded the number four, which is why the k-version came out first.

Sheet 8 - a dice game with a forkworking

A and B each play with a die. A rolls once and is assigned the number rolled. B also rolls once and is assigned that number - unless the roll is a one, in which case B rolls a second time and is assigned whatever the second roll shows, even if it is another one. The higher assigned number wins, and a tie goes to A. What is the probability that A wins?

Hint

Work out B's assigned-value distribution first. It is not uniform, and one is much rarer than the rest.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find B's distribution. B ends on 1 only by rolling 1 twice: P(B = 1) = (1/6)(1/6) = 1/36. For any value v from 2 to 6, B ends on v either by rolling it first (1/6) or by rolling a one and then v (1/6 × 1/6): P(B = v) = 1/6 + 1/36 = 7/36.
  2. Check it is a distribution. 1/36 + 5 × 7/36 = 1/36 + 35/36 = 1. Good.
  3. Build B's cumulative totals, in thirty-sixths. P(B ≤ 1) = 1; P(B ≤ 2) = 8; P(B ≤ 3) = 15; P(B ≤ 4) = 22; P(B ≤ 5) = 29; P(B ≤ 6) = 36.
  4. State A's winning condition. A wins when A's value is at least B's, since ties go to A. A's value is uniform on 1 to 6, independent of B.
  5. Slice over A's value. P(A wins) = ∑a=16 (1/6) P(B ≤ a) = (1/6)(1/36)(1 + 8 + 15 + 22 + 29 + 36).
  6. Add and divide. The bracket is 111, so P(A wins) = 111/216 = 37/72 ≈ 0.514.

Sanity check. Two forces pull against each other and the answer sits between them. With no reroll at all, ties alone would give A the win with probability (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/36 = 21/36 ≈ 0.583. The reroll lifts B's distribution - it removes almost all of B's chance of the worst value - and drags A down to 0.514. A still leads, because the tie rule is worth more than the reroll, and the margin over a half is small, which matches how narrow B's advantage is.

Sheet 11 - the urn that growsfrontier

An urn starts with one black ball and one brown ball. Each round: draw one ball at random, note its colour, put it back, and add one more ball of that same colour. After N rounds the urn holds N plus two balls. Find the probability that the urn ends up with exactly k black balls.

Hint

Price one full history as a product. Notice the numerator does not depend on the order of the colours.

Show the worked solution
  1. Set the range of k. The black count starts at 1 and rises by at most one per round, so the final count runs from 1 to N + 1. Write k = 1 + j, where j is the number of rounds that drew black, and j runs from 0 to N.
  2. Write the urn's state. Before round t (counting t = 0, 1, ..., N − 1) the urn holds 2 + t balls in total. If it currently holds b black, the chance the next draw is black is b/(2 + t).
  3. Price one specific history. A history is a sequence of N colours. By the multiplication rule its probability is the product of the N conditional chances. The denominators are 2, 3, ..., N + 1 whatever the order, so they multiply to (N + 1)!/1! = (N + 1)!.
  4. Price the numerator. The j black draws meet black counts 1, 2, ..., j in order, contributing j!. The N − j brown draws meet brown counts 1, 2, ..., N − j, contributing (N − j)!. So every history with j black draws has probability j!(N − j)!/(N + 1)!, regardless of the order the colours came in.
  5. Count the histories. The number of sequences with exactly j black draws among N is C(N, j).
  6. Multiply and simplify. P(j black draws) = C(N, j) · j!(N − j)!/(N + 1)! = [N!/(j!(N − j)!)] · j!(N − j)!/(N + 1)! = N!/(N + 1)! = 1/(N + 1).
  7. State the answer. P(exactly k black balls at the end) = 1/(N + 1), the same value for every k from 1 to N + 1. The final count is uniform.

Sanity check. The N + 1 possible values each carry 1/(N + 1), so the probabilities sum to 1. Test N = 1: the single draw is 50-50, giving k = 1 or 2 with probability 1/2 each, and the formula gives 1/2. Test N = 2 at one value: k = 1 needs both draws brown, with probability (1/2)(2/3) = 1/3, and the formula gives 1/3. This is why the guess about clustering fails - reinforcement makes the process remember, and a lead compounds instead of correcting.

Sheet 12 - best of five, stopped earlyworking

A tennis match is best of five sets: the first player to win three sets wins the match, and play stops at that instant. The players are evenly matched, so each set is a fair coin flip, independent set to set. Find the probability that the loser of the match won exactly k sets.

Hint

The last set of any legal match belongs to the winner. Count the arrangements of the sets before it.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix the possible values of k. The loser won fewer than three sets, so k is 0, 1 or 2. A match in which the loser won k sets lasted exactly 3 + k sets.
  2. Pin the last set. Play stops the instant someone reaches three, so the final set of any legal sequence was won by the eventual match winner. Before that final set the score stood at 2 to k.
  3. Count the arrangements. The first 2 + k sets contain exactly 2 wins for the eventual winner and k for the loser, in any order, giving C(2 + k, k) sequences. Each such sequence, extended by the winner's final set, is a legal stopped match and no other sequence is.
  4. Price one sequence. Every set is a fair independent flip, so a sequence of length 3 + k has probability (1/2)3+k.
  5. Account for both players. Either player can be the winner, and the two cases are disjoint, so multiply by 2: P(loser won k) = 2 · C(2 + k, k) · (1/2)3+k = C(2 + k, k) · (1/2)2+k.
  6. Evaluate the three cases. k = 0: C(2, 0)/4 = 1/4. k = 1: C(3, 1)/8 = 3/8. k = 2: C(4, 2)/16 = 6/16 = 3/8.

Sanity check. The three values sum to 1/4 + 3/8 + 3/8 = 1, so the case list is complete - which also confirms that no fourth value of k was missed. The straight-sets result is the rarest, which matches the intuition that a coin-flip match usually runs long. Note that the naive count of all 25 = 32 five-set sequences would double-count matches already decided in three or four sets, which is exactly the stopping rule doing its work.

Sheet 17 - a random graph and its lonely nodefrontier

The Erdos-Renyi model G(n, p) builds a random graph on n nodes where every pair is joined by an edge with probability p, independently of every other pair. Let Bn be the event that the graph has at least one isolated node, a node with no edges at all. Show that P(Bn) is at most n times (1 − p) to the power n − 1; and conclude that for any ε > 0, if pn = (1 + ε) times the natural logarithm of n, over n, then P(Bn) tends to 0 as n grows without bound.

Hint

One node, then all n by the union bound. For the limit, replace 1 − p by e to the minus p and read the exponent.

Show the worked solution
  1. Price one node's isolation. Fix node i. It has n − 1 potential edges, one to each other node, and each is absent with probability 1 − p, independently. So P(node i is isolated) = (1 − p)n−1.
  2. Sweep all n nodes. Bn is the union of the n events "node i is isolated". The union bound gives P(Bn) ≤ ∑i=1n (1 − p)n−1 = n(1 − p)n−1. That is the printed bound, and note that no independence between the n events was needed - the union bound never asks for any.
  3. Substitute the given p. With pn = (1 + ε) ln(n) / n, the bound reads P(Bn) ≤ n(1 − pn)n−1.
  4. Replace the base by an exponential. For every real x, 1 − x ≤ e−x. Applying it: (1 − pn)n−1 ≤ e−pn(n−1), so P(Bn) ≤ n e−pn(n−1).
  5. Compute the exponent. pn(n − 1) = (1 + ε) ln(n) (n − 1)/n = (1 + ε) ln(n) − (1 + ε) ln(n)/n. So e−pn(n−1) = n−(1+ε) · n(1+ε)/n.
  6. Collect the powers of n. P(Bn) ≤ n · n−(1+ε) · n(1+ε)/n = n−ε · n(1+ε)/n.
  7. Take the limit. The second factor is e raised to (1 + ε) ln(n)/n, and ln(n)/n tends to 0, so that factor tends to 1. The first factor n−ε tends to 0 because ε > 0. The product tends to 0, and since P(Bn) is squeezed between 0 and it, P(Bn) tends to 0.

Sanity check. The strict positivity of ε is doing the work: at ε = 0 the bound becomes n0 times something tending to 1, which does not go to zero at all - and indeed ln(n)/n is the known threshold at which isolated nodes stop appearing. Check the direction too: raising p makes 1 − p smaller and shrinks the bound, which is right, since denser graphs have fewer lonely nodes.

Sheet 18 - the coupon drawerworking

Each pack contains one coupon. There are n coupon types, and coupons arrive independently: each is of type i with probability pi, the pi summing to one. You have collected k coupons. Let Ai be the event that at least one of those k is of type i. With i and j distinct: (a) find P(Ai); (b) find P(Ai ∪ Aj); (c) find P(Ai | Aj).

P(Ai) = ?,   P(Ai ∪ Aj) = ?,   P(Ai | Aj) = ?   for i ≠ j Read as: Ai is "at least one of the k already-collected coupons has type i". The union asks for at least one of type i or at least one of type j. The conditional asks for the chance type i appeared, given that type j appeared.
Hint

Build one block - "none of the k draws lands in this set of types" - and reuse it for all three parts.

Show the worked solution
  1. Build the complement block. For any set T of types with total probability q, a single draw avoids T with probability 1 − q, and the k draws are independent, so P(no draw lands in T) = (1 − q)k.
  2. Part (a). Ai fails exactly when all k draws avoid type i, so P(Ai) = 1 − (1 − pi)k.
  3. Part (b). Ai ∪ Aj fails exactly when all k draws avoid both types, a set of total probability pi + pj because distinct types are disjoint. So P(Ai ∪ Aj) = 1 − (1 − pi − pj)k.
  4. Get the joint event from those two. The addition rule rearranges to P(Ai ∩ Aj) = P(Ai) + P(Aj) − P(Ai ∪ Aj) = 1 − (1 − pi)k − (1 − pj)k + (1 − pi − pj)k.
  5. Part (c). By the definition of conditional probability, P(Ai | Aj) = P(Ai ∩ Aj) / P(Aj), that is [1 − (1 − pi)k − (1 − pj)k + (1 − pi − pj)k] / [1 − (1 − pj)k].

Sanity check. Test with two types, pi = pj = 1/2 and k = 2: (a) gives 3/4, (b) gives 1 − 0 = 1, the joint gives 1/2, and (c) gives (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3. That 2/3 is below the unconditional 3/4, which is the right direction - knowing type j appeared means a draw was spent on it, leaving fewer chances for type i. Two boundary cases also hold: at k = 0 every answer collapses to 0, and as k grows all three tend to 1.

Sheet 19 - the three doors, provedworking

Three doors lead to three rooms: two are empty and one holds a Mercedes. You choose one room. After you choose, the game master opens the door of one of the rooms you did not select, picking one that does not contain the Mercedes. With E1 the event of winning with the initial guess, E2 the event of winning if you change your choice, and C the event that a room not containing the Mercedes is revealed - prove that P(E1) is strictly less than P(E2 | C).

Hint

Build the nine-cell table of your door against the prize door. Then ask whether C can ever fail.

Show the worked solution
  1. Build the sample space. An outcome is a pair: the door you pick and the door hiding the prize. The prize is placed uniformly and independently of your pick, so the three-by-three table has nine cells, each of probability 1/9.
  2. Write the master's two rules as facts. He opens a door you did not pick, and it is not the prize door. On the three diagonal cells - your pick is correct - two doors satisfy both rules and he takes either. On the six off-diagonal cells - your pick is wrong - exactly one door satisfies both, and he must take it.
  3. Show C is certain. In every one of the nine cells at least one door satisfies both rules, so a non-prize unpicked door is always revealed. Hence P(C) = 1.
  4. Read E1 off the table. Sticking wins exactly on the three diagonal cells, so P(E1) = 3/9 = 1/3.
  5. Read E2 off the table. Exactly one door stays closed besides yours. On a diagonal cell that door is empty, so switching loses. On an off-diagonal cell your door is empty and the opened door is empty, so the remaining closed door holds the prize and switching wins. Switching therefore wins on the six off-diagonal cells: P(E2) = 6/9 = 2/3.
  6. Condition on C. Since P(C) = 1, the event E2 ∩ C equals E2 up to a null set, so P(E2 | C) = P(E2 ∩ C)/P(C) = (2/3)/1 = 2/3.
  7. Compare. P(E1) = 1/3 < 2/3 = P(E2 | C), and the inequality is strict. Switching doubles the chance of the car.

Sanity check. The two strategies are exhaustive on each outcome - exactly one of sticking and switching wins - and 1/3 + 2/3 = 1, so no cell was counted twice or missed. The instinct that says one half each is refuted by step 3: the reveal is certain, so it cannot carry news about your own door, whose chance stays at the 1/3 it had before the game master moved. Change the master's rules and the answer changes - if he opened a door at random and it happened to be empty, the two closed doors really would be even.

Carry this away

  • Name the outcome first. A run of N draws, a stopped match and a whole history are outcomes; a single step usually is not.
  • "At least one" is priced through its complement, in problems 6, 17 and 18 alike.
  • A process that reinforces itself must be priced path by path, and problem 11 shows the paths can collapse into something simple.
  • Write the rules of the game into the model. In the three-door problem the master's two constraints are the entire content.
