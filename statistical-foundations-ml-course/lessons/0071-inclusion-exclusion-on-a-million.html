TA Session 2 · Part 2 of 7 · Question 10

Seven counts, and the only hard part is knowing what "divisible by both" means

working 13 min Question 10. Inclusion-exclusion on a set of a million integers, where every term is one floor division.

The one-minute version

  • Pick a number uniformly from 1 to 1,000,000. How likely is it to be divisible by at least one of 4, 6 and 9?
  • Three events, so inclusion-exclusion has seven terms: three singles added, three pairs subtracted, one triple added back.
  • Every term is a count of multiples, and a count of multiples is a floor division. The pairs use the lowest common multiple, not the product.
  • The counts are 250,000, 166,666, 111,111, then 83,333, 27,777, 55,555, then 27,777.
  • They combine to 388,889, so the answer is 0.388889, which the notes round to 38.8889%.

Question 10, restated

A number is chosen uniformly at random from the range [1, 1000000]. Using the inclusion-exclusion principle, determine the probability that the chosen number is divisible by one or more of 4, 6 and 9.

Which theorem is in play, and why counting comes before probability

The word "uniformly" does the same job it did throughout Lecture 5: every one of the million integers is equally likely, so the counting formula applies and the probability is a count over a million. The question then becomes purely a counting question, and the tool it names is inclusion-exclusion from Lecture 3.

Name the three events first, in words.

  • A is the event that the number is divisible by 4.
  • B is the event that the number is divisible by 6.
  • C is the event that the number is divisible by 9.
P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A ∩ B) − P(A ∩ C) − P(B ∩ C) + P(A ∩ B ∩ C) A ∪ B ∪ C, read "A or B or C", is the event that at least one of the three divisions comes out whole, which is exactly what "one or more of 4, 6 and 9" asks for. The alternating pattern is bookkeeping: adding the three singles counts anything in two of them twice, so the three pairs come off, and that removes anything in all three one time too many, so the triple goes back on. Every term here is a probability, but each is a count over the same million, so the million can be carried along and divided out at the very end.

Step 1: the three individual counts

How many numbers from 1 to a million are divisible by d? They are d, 2d, 3d and so on up to the largest multiple that does not pass a million, so the count is a million divided by d, rounded down. The rounding down matters whenever d does not divide a million exactly.

  1. Divisible by 4. |A| = ⌊1,000,000 / 4⌋ = 250,000. Here the division is exact.
  2. Divisible by 6. |B| = ⌊1,000,000 / 6⌋ = 166,666. Not exact: 6 × 166,666 = 999,996, and the next multiple would be 1,000,002, which is out of range.
  3. Divisible by 9. |C| = ⌊1,000,000 / 9⌋ = 111,111. Also not exact: 9 × 111,111 = 999,999.

Step 2: the pairwise counts, and the one thing to get right

A number lies in both A and B when it is divisible by 4 and by 6. That does not mean divisible by 24. It means divisible by the smallest number that both 4 and 6 divide, which is their lowest common multiple.

  1. A and B together. lcm(4, 6) = 12, so |A ∩ B| = ⌊1,000,000 / 12⌋ = 83,333.
  2. A and C together. lcm(4, 9) = 36, so |A ∩ C| = ⌊1,000,000 / 36⌋ = 27,777. Here 4 and 9 share no factor, so the lcm really is the product.
  3. B and C together. lcm(6, 9) = 18, so |B ∩ C| = ⌊1,000,000 / 18⌋ = 55,555. They share a 3, so the product 54 would again be wrong.

Step 3: the triple count

A number divisible by all three of 4, 6 and 9 must be divisible by the lowest common multiple of the three.

  1. Take the lcm of all three. lcm(4, 6, 9) = 36. Build it up: lcm(4, 6) = 12, and lcm(12, 9) = 36.
  2. Count the multiples. |A ∩ B ∩ C| = ⌊1,000,000 / 36⌋ = 27,777.
This course's observationThat triple count is the same number as |A ∩ C|, and it is not a coincidence. A number divisible by 4 and by 9 is divisible by 36, and 36 is itself divisible by 6, so every number in A ∩ C is already in B. In set language, A ∩ C is contained in B, which makes A ∩ B ∩ C exactly equal to A ∩ C rather than merely the same size. In the seven-term sum the two therefore cancel: −27,777 + 27,777 = 0, and the answer could have been reached with five terms instead of seven. The notes carry both numbers and do not remark on it; the containment is this course's own reading.

  2. Subtract the three pairs. 527,777 − 83,333 = 444,444, then − 27,777 = 416,667, then − 55,555 = 361,112.
  3. Add the triple back. 361,112 + 27,777 = 388,889.

Answer0.388889, which the notes state as approximately 38.89% and give exactly as 38.8889%. Roughly two numbers in every five, in this range, are divisible by at least one of 4, 6 and 9.
Where this goes wrongThree slips, in order of how often they happen. Using the product instead of the lcm for a pair: 24 for 4 and 6, or 54 for 6 and 9, both of which undercount badly. Forgetting to round down, which matters for every divisor here except 4 and quietly inflates five of the seven terms. And losing a sign in the alternating pattern, most often by subtracting the triple instead of adding it, which here would give 333,335 rather than 388,889. The sign pattern is not arbitrary: the singles overcount the doubles, and removing the doubles overcorrects the triple.

A check you can run without redoing the work

  1. Bound it from below. The answer must be at least P(A) = 0.25, since every multiple of 4 is in the union. 0.3889 clears that.
  2. Bound it from above. The answer cannot exceed the sum of the three singles, 0.527777, because that sum counts everything at least once. 0.3889 is comfortably under.
  3. Sanity-check the density. Multiples of 4 alone give one number in four. Adding two more divisors that overlap heavily with it should push that up but not double it, and 0.3889 against 0.25 is exactly that shape of increase.

Check yourself

Why is the count for A and B together computed with 12 rather than 24?
The smallest number that both 4 and 6 divide is 12, so the numbers divisible by both are exactly the multiples of 12, and there are 83,333 of them. The product 24 works only when the two divisors share no factor, and 4 and 6 share a 2; the number 12 is the immediate counterexample. The size of 24 relative to anything is irrelevant. And the rule is never "use the smaller divisor": for 4 and 9 the correct divisor is 36, which is larger than both.
Why do the subtracted 27,777 and the added 27,777 cancel each other exactly?
A number divisible by 4 and 9 is divisible by 36, and 36 is a multiple of 6, so A and C overlapping already puts you inside B: the set A and C is contained in B, which makes the triple intersection identical to it, not merely equal in size. The equal rounding is the symptom rather than the cause. Inclusion-exclusion has no such general pairing rule; with different numbers the two terms would differ. And that 36 appears twice restates the observation without explaining it.
Your teacher. Before computing any intersection of divisibility events, write the lcm down on its own line and then test it against a small number by hand. For 4 and 6, ask: is 12 divisible by 4? Yes. By 6? Yes. Is there anything smaller that works? No. Three seconds, and it immunises you against the product habit permanently. The same discipline catches the containment on this page for free, because once you have written lcm(4, 9) = 36 and lcm(4, 6, 9) = 36 on adjacent lines, the cancellation is impossible to miss.
