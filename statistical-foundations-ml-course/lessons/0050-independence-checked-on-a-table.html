Independence is a product you can check on a table in about a minute

working 11 min A thousand users, four cells, two margins. Independence stops being a definition here and becomes a procedure.

The one-minute version

  • The definition, exactly as the notes give it: A and B are independent if and only if P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • When P(B) > 0 that is the same statement as P(A) = P(A | B), which reads: knowing that B happened does not move the probability of A.
  • The lecture checks it on a table of 1000 app users. P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.4, P(A ∩ B) = 0.24, and 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24. They agree, so A and B are independent.
  • The margins say the same thing the other way: P(B | A) = 240/600 = 0.4 = P(B). Premium subscribers use the app at exactly the rate everybody else does.

1. What is new on this page

Lecture 3, Part 8 already defined independence, showed that it is a numerical fact rather than something a Venn diagram can display, and proved that disjoint is not the same as independent. None of that is repeated here.

What this page adds is the shape the check actually takes in practice. Independence is almost never handed to you as a probability statement; it arrives as a table of counts, and turning that table into a verdict is a fixed four-step procedure. The lecture works one such table in full, and so does this page.

2. The definition, and the reading that makes it obvious

A and B are independent  ⇔  P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B) A and B are events, meaning subsets of the sample space, and both must live in the sigma-field F so that they have probabilities at all. A ∩ B is the event that both happen. The double arrow ⇔ means the two sides define each other: this equation is not a consequence of independence, it is independence. Nothing is being assumed about causation, timing or mechanism.

The notes then give the equivalent form, and it is the one worth carrying in your head. Assume P(B) > 0, so that conditioning on B is legal at all, and start from the definition of conditional probability:

  1. Start from the conditional definition. P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B), which is Lecture 3, Part 4.
  2. Substitute the independence equation. If P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B), then P(A | B) = P(A) × P(B) ÷ P(B).
  3. Cancel. The P(B) cancels, leaving P(A | B) = P(A).

So independence says: the world shrinking to B does not change A's share of it. Learning that B happened is worth nothing as evidence about A. That is what the product equation means, and it is why the product equation is the definition rather than a rule about independent things.

3. The lecture's table, in full

An e-commerce app has 1000 users. Two facts are recorded about each of them, and both are yes-or-no.

  • A is the event "this user uses the app", meaning they were active in the period being measured.
  • B is the event "this user has a premium subscription".

Cross-tabulating gives four counts and two sets of margins.

Premium, BNo premium, BcRow total
Uses the app, A240360600
Does not, Ac160240400
Column total4006001000

Every one of those numbers is stated in the lecture. Now the four-step check.

  1. The first margin. 600 of the 1000 users are active, so P(A) = 600/1000 = 0.6.
  2. The second margin. 400 of the 1000 hold a premium subscription, so P(B) = 400/1000 = 0.4.
  3. The corner cell. 240 users are active and premium, so P(A ∩ B) = 240/1000 = 0.24.
  4. Compare against the product. P(A) × P(B) = 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24. The two agree exactly, so A and B are independent.

The lecture also reads the verdict off the margins the other way round, which is the conditional form in action: P(B | A) = 240/600 = 0.4, and that is P(B) unchanged. Among the six hundred active users, the premium rate is forty percent; among all thousand users, the premium rate is forty percent. Knowing somebody is active tells you nothing about whether they pay.

4. The check runs in either direction, and on every cell

The lecture checks one cell and one margin. It is worth knowing that this was not a lucky choice: on a two-by-two table, one cell passing forces all four to pass, and both conditional readings follow too. The four lines below are this course's own extension of the lecture's check, using the lecture's own counts.

That every cell agrees is not a coincidence and it is not four separate facts. The row totals are fixed and the column totals are fixed, so once one cell is pinned the other three are forced by subtraction. This is also why TA Session 1, Part 7 matters: with only two events, one equation settles everything, and the comfortable feeling that comes from that does not survive the move to three events, where four separate equations are needed and checking one proves nothing about the rest.

Here P(A) is still 0.6 and P(B) is still 0.4, so the product P(A) × P(B) is still 0.24. But P(A ∩ B) is now 300/1000 = 0.3, and 0.3 is not 0.24, so these two events are not independent. The conditional reading agrees: P(B | A) = 300/600 = 0.5, against P(B) = 0.4. Among active users the premium rate is one in two; overall it is two in five. Being active is now evidence of paying.

6. Check yourself

A report gives you P(A) = 0.6 and P(B) = 0.4 and nothing else. What can you conclude about independence?
The two tables in section 5 have identical margins and opposite verdicts, which settles it: margins alone are never enough, and the joint count is the whole answer. The product being computable says only that both probabilities exist. Dependence cannot be concluded from missing data either - the lecture's own table has these exact margins and is independent. And overlap is a red herring: A and B here overlap in both tables, and one is independent while the other is not.
Why does the lecture give P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) as the definition rather than P(A | B) = P(A)?
The conditional form divides by P(B), so it is undefined the moment P(B) is zero, while the product form remains a perfectly good statement - and it is symmetric in A and B, which the conditional form hides. Ease of computation is not the reason; on a table both take one division. Conditional probability was in fact taught first, in Lecture 3. And disjoint events are almost never independent - if both have positive probability, disjointness makes P(A and B) zero while the product is not, so they are dependent.
Your teacher. Take a real two-by-two from your own systems this week - requests that errored against requests from a particular client version, say - and run the four steps on it. Two margins, one corner cell, one product. Most engineering intuitions about "these two things go together" evaporate on contact with that product, and the ones that survive it are worth acting on.
