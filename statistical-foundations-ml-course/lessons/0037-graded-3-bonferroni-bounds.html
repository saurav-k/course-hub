One die, three rolls, and one truth bracketed three ways

working 12 min Four marks: one for exactly one six, one for at least one six, and two for bracketing the second answer with the first three Bonferroni inequalities and comparing.

The one-minute version

  • A fair die is rolled independently three times. E1 is the event that exactly one 6 occurs; E2 is the event that at least one 6 occurs. Part (a) prices E1, part (b) prices E2.
  • Part (c) asks for bounds on P(E2) from the first-, second- and third-order Bonferroni inequalities, and a comparison of the three bounds with each other and with part (b)'s exact value.
  • The three inequalities are reference material: all three are stated below, each with the direction it points. The first and third are ceilings; the second is a floor.
  • This page states the tools and the structure. It computes no probability and evaluates no bound.

The problem, restated in full

A fair six-sided die is rolled independently three times. Define:

  • E1 = the event that exactly one 6 occurs among the three rolls.
  • E2 = the event that at least one 6 occurs among the three rolls.

Three questions, in the sheet's own numbering:

  1. Compute P(E1). [1 mark]
  2. Compute P(E2). [1 mark]
  3. Compute bounds on P(E2) using the first-order, second-order, and third-order Bonferroni inequalities. Compare the bounds with each other and with the true value computed in the previous part. [2 marks]

Every symbol, named: fair means each of the six faces is equally likely on any single roll, so any one specified face, a 6 included, shows with probability one in six. Independently means the rolls tell each other nothing: what roll one shows changes no probability about rolls two and three. Exactly one means one 6 and no more than one; at least one means one, two, or three, with no upper disqualification. The two events are therefore nested: everything in E1 is also in E2, and E2 holds strictly more besides.

One definition is worth making before anything else, because every part of the question runs on it. Let Ai be the event that roll i shows a 6, for i = 1, 2, 3. Then E2 is the union of the three Ai - at least one six means some roll shows one - and E1 is the exactly-one pattern across the same three events. Naming the Ai turns both questions into statements about a union of three events, which is exactly the situation the bounds of part (c) are built for.

What it is really testing

Three tools, one per part. Part (a) tests whether you can split an exactly-one event into disjoint pieces by position and price each piece with the multiplication rule - the three rolls are independent, so a statement about all three chains from single-roll facts. Part (b) tests whether you reach for the right exact tool for an at-least-one event: the complement rule against no sixes at all, or the exact inclusion-exclusion formula over the three Ai. Part (c) tests whether you can take the three Bonferroni truncations from Lecture 3, Part 3, specialise them to three events, point each one the right way, and say something intelligent about how tight they land.

Parts (a) and (b): the structure, then a stop

How to start part (a)

Split E1 by which roll carries the single 6: six in roll 1 and not elsewhere, six in roll 2 and not elsewhere, six in roll 3 and not elsewhere. Say in one sentence why the three pieces are disjoint. Price one piece by chaining three independent roll facts with the multiplication rule. Then stop - the arithmetic, and assembling the pieces, is the homework.

How to start part (b)

Two honest routes exist, and either earns the mark when justified. Route one is the complement rule: the opposite of "at least one 6" is "no sixes at all", which is a clean event to price, and P(E2) = 1 minus that. Route two is the exact inclusion-exclusion formula over the three events A1, A2, A3, which for three events is short enough to run by hand. Pick a route, say in a sentence why it is legitimate, and then stop. The computation is the homework.

Part (c): the three inequalities, stated as reference

Here is the reference material part (c) assumes you can produce. For events A1, ..., An - here the three events A1, A2, A3 - the Bonferroni inequalities are the inclusion-exclusion sum stopped early, and the side of the truth you land on alternates with the number of levels kept. All three, with their directions:

OrderThe inequalityDirectionReads as
First-order
the union bound		 P(∪ Ai) ≤ ∑i P(Ai) Ceiling - points up The union is never more likely than the sum of the singles, because outcomes in several events get counted several times.
Second-order P(∪ Ai) ≥ ∑i P(Ai) − ∑i<j P(Ai ∩ Aj) Floor - points down Subtracting every pairwise overlap once corrects the double counting and overshoots downward.
Third-order P(∪ Ai) ≤ ∑i P(Ai) − ∑i<j P(Ai ∩ Aj) + ∑i<j<k P(Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak) Ceiling - points up again Adding the triple intersections back in climbs above the truth once more.

Two things worth noticing before you compute, because the sheet says them in its wording. First, the bounds are asked for P(E2) only - the union of the three Ai is exactly the event the inequalities are built for, and part (a)'s E1 is a different event that they do not directly price. Second, the comparison the question asks for has two halves: the bounds against each other, and each bound against the true value from part (b). Part (b) hands you the truth; the bracket tells you how much accuracy each extra level bought. With only three events, every sum involved is short - the singles sum has three terms, the pairs sum has three, and the triples sum has one - so the whole of part (c) is a handful of small prices and one honest paragraph.

A naming trap, before it costs youTextbooks are not unanimous about what "first-order" names. Some call the union bound itself the first-order Bonferroni inequality, which is the convention this sheet and Lecture 3, Part 3 share: keep one level of inclusion-exclusion and you have the first order, keep two for the second, keep three for the third. If you have met a book that counts differently, follow the lecture's convention here, and state your three inequalities explicitly in your answer - then the marker can see exactly which three objects you used, whatever convention they carry in your head.

How to start part (c)

Write the three inequalities from the table with n = 3, so each sum is over the events A1, A2, A3 you defined at the start. Price each term that appears - single events, pairwise intersections, the triple - from single-roll facts and the independence of the rolls. Assemble the three truncation values, mark which are ceilings and which is the floor, and line all three up against part (b)'s exact value. Then stop and write the comparison; that paragraph is where the second mark lives.

The traps

  • E1 is not the complement of E2. The complement of "at least one 6" is "no sixes at all". E1 is one slice of E2, the exactly-one slice, and confusing the slice with the opposite wrecks both part (a) and any attempt at part (b).
  • The bounds in part (c) apply to P(E2). The question says so, and the union structure is why. Producing bounds for P(E1) answers a question nobody asked.
  • Each inequality is one number, and each points one way. Writing the first-order expression without its direction, or reporting a floor as a ceiling, throws away the understanding the two marks are testing - the alternation is the content.
  • "Compare" is a marked instruction, not a courtesy. The sheet asks how the bounds stand relative to each other and to the true value. Three bare numbers with no ordering and no tightness remark is an incomplete answer to a two-mark question.
  • Independence of the rolls is doing work in every part. It is what lets each term - a statement about all three rolls - be priced from single-roll facts. If your working never uses it, check what you actually computed.

Check yourself

What is the complement of E2, the event that at least one 6 occurs among the three rolls?
The opposite of "at least one" is "none at all", so the complement is the event that all three rolls show something other than six. The first option is E1, which sits inside E2 rather than outside it - the trap the question exists to catch. The third is the strongest possible version of E2, and the fourth is another slice of it; neither is outside.
The second-order Bonferroni inequality keeps the singles sum and subtracts the pairwise overlaps. Which way does it point?
Keeping two levels of the alternating sum lands below the truth, so the second-order truncation is a floor - the alternation runs ceiling, floor, ceiling. Calling it a ceiling points it the wrong way and would put the truth outside your bracket. Exactness is not on offer: the truncation equals the truth only under special structure, and independence of the events is not what delivers it. The direction comes from the parity of the levels kept, which is fixed, not from the event count.
Part (c) asks you to compare the three bounds with each other and with the true value. What does a complete comparison actually do?
The comparison the sheet wants places the floor below the ceilings, all of them around the exact value from part (b), and remarks on which truncation landed closest - that ordering and tightness discussion is where the marks sit. Averaging bounds of different directions produces a number that is none of the three, reporting only the largest discards the bracket, and the bounds were never claimed for E1.
Your teacher. Part (c) is the sheet quietly teaching a habit you will meet again in Lecture 10, tail inequalities: when an exact answer is cheap, use it to calibrate the cheap bounds, so that later - when the exact answer is out of reach - you already know how much a union bound costs you. Notice, in your own working, which order earned its keep and which did not. That observation, written in one sentence, is the difference between a computation and an argument.
