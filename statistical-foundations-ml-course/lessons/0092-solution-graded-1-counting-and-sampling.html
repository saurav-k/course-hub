Homework 1 · Solution to graded problem 1 · both parts, three routes each

Three roads reach 3/64, three more reach 1/32, and the agreement is the check

working 15 min The companion to Graded 1, which states the problem and refuses to solve it. Open this one after your own attempt.

The one-minute version

  • Part (a) is 3/64, which is 0.046875. Part (b) is 1/32, which is 0.03125. Both are exact.
  • Part (a) is solved from the axioms, by counting fillings, and by a chain of conditionals.
  • Part (b) is solved by counting subsets, by the set picture with the complement rule, and by ten coin flips.
  • The Venn diagram belongs to part (b) and not to part (a), and the page says why rather than drawing one that argues nothing.
  • Two routes agreeing is the only self-check a probability answer has. That is why there are six of them.

1. Part (a): nine cells are constrained and sixteen are free

Write the three demands as three events. R is the event that the second row of the grid totals 4. C is the event that the second column totals 3. E is the event that the shared entry A2,2 equals 1. The sheet asks for P(R ∩ C ∩ E), where ∩ is read "and": the outcomes lying in all three events at once. Every route below starts from one drawing.

Route 1: from the axioms

The sheet's phrase independently across entries is a licence, and this route spends it. Lecture 3, Independence defines it: a family of events is independent when the probability of any collection of them holding together is the product of their separate probabilities. The part used here is the grouping property that comes with it - events read off disjoint blocks of independent entries are themselves independent, because no entry appears in two of them.

P(R ∩ C ∩ E) = P(E) × P(R′) × P(C′) Read as: three probabilities multiplied. P is the probability measure. E is the event that A2,2 holds a 1. R′ is the event that row 2's four remaining cells total 3. C′ is the event that column 2's four remaining cells total 2. The three are read off blocks that share nothing, which is what makes the multiplication legal.

Each factor is now a statement about a handful of fair independent entries, and finite additivity - the third axiom restricted to a finite union of disjoint events, stated in What the axioms force - is what assembles each one by adding up the outcomes it contains.

  1. Price the pinned cell. A2,2 is one fair entry, so P(E) = 1 ÷ 2 = 1/2.
  2. Price the row's remainder. Four fair entries have 24 = 16 equally likely fillings, and C(4, 3) = 4 of them total 3. So P(R′) = 4 ÷ 16 = 1/4.
  3. Price the column's remainder. The same sixteen fillings, and C(4, 2) = 6 of them total 2. So P(C′) = 6 ÷ 16 = 3/8.
  4. Multiply, by independence of the three blocks. (1/2) × (1/4) × (3/8) = 3 ÷ 64.
  5. Read the answer. P(R ∩ C ∩ E) = 3/64, which is 0.046875.

Route 2: counting the fillings

This route uses no independence at all, only the finite uniform model from Geometry and counting: when every outcome of a finite space is equally likely, a probability is the count of outcomes in the event over the count in the space. The sheet says every complete filling is equally likely, so the model applies exactly.

P(A) = |A| ÷ |Ω| Read as: a probability is a ratio of two counts. Ω is the sample space, here every complete filling of the 25 cells with zeros and ones, and |Ω| is how many there are. A is the event, here the fillings meeting all three demands, and |A| is how many of those there are. Only valid when every element of Ω is equally likely, which the sheet states.
  1. Count the whole space. Twenty-five cells, two values each, chosen freely: |Ω| = 225 = 33,554,432.
  2. Count the favourable fillings, block by block. The shared cell has 1 admissible value. Row 2's other four have C(4, 3) = 4 admissible fillings. Column 2's other four have C(4, 2) = 6. The remaining sixteen cells are named by no condition, so all 216 = 65,536 of their fillings qualify.
  3. Multiply the block counts. This is the rule of product, and it is legal here because the four blocks partition the 25 cells: choosing one filling from each block produces every favourable grid exactly once. |A| = 1 × 4 × 6 × 65,536 = 1,572,864.
  4. Take the ratio. The numerator is 24 × 216 and the denominator is 29 × 216, so the ratio is 24 ÷ 512.
  5. Reduce. 24 ÷ 512 = 3/64, which is 0.046875 - the same number, reached without once using the word independent.

Route 3: the conditioning chain

This route builds the answer one demand at a time with the multiplication rule from Lecture 3. It is the route that survives when the blocks are not disjoint, which is why it is worth running here, where they are.

P(R ∩ C ∩ E) = P(E) × P(R | E) × P(C | R ∩ E) Read as: pin the shared cell, then ask for the row given that, then the column given both. P(R | E) is read "the probability of R given E" - the probability that row 2 totals 4 in the shrunken world where A2,2 is already known to be 1. P(C | R ∩ E) is the same idea with two facts granted. The order is a choice, not a rule: any order of the three gives the same product.

Only the third factor needs an argument rather than a calculation. P(C | R ∩ E) asks about column 2's four remaining cells, while the two granted facts concern A2,2 and row 2's four remaining cells. Those are different cells and the entries are independent, so P(C | R ∩ E) = P(C | E), which is 6/16.

  1. First factor. P(E) = 1/2, the pinned cell.
  2. Second factor. P(R | E) = 4/16 = 1/4, the row's four remaining entries totalling 3.
  3. Third factor. P(C | R ∩ E) = P(C | E) = 6/16 = 3/8, the column's four remaining entries totalling 2.
  4. Multiply along the chain. (1/2) × (1/4) × (3/8) = 3/64, which is 0.046875. Three for three.

Why there is no Venn diagram in part (a)

Three overlapping circles are the right picture when the answer is a union, a complement or a difference, because those are the regions the overlaps make readable. Part (a) asks for the triple intersection itself: drawing it puts the answer on the page without saying how large it is, and inclusion-exclusion runs the wrong way to help, since it prices a union using the intersections. Applying it here would mean computing every pairwise intersection and solving backwards for the region already wanted - more work for no insight, so this page does not pad itself with it. The set picture that does carry part (a) is the figure above: the space as four independent blocks, and the event as a box unrestricted in one and narrow in the other three. Part (b) is where circles earn their place.

Why may the three factors 1/2, 1/4 and 3/8 be multiplied together?
Independent entries in blocks that share no cell give independent events, and independent events multiply. The first option is false of the third demand, which names a single entry and is multiplied in anyway. Equal lengths are a coincidence of a square grid and would not change the argument in a 5 by 7 one. And every entry being equally likely is what makes each individual factor computable, not what licenses the multiplication: the same fair entries arranged in overlapping blocks would not multiply.
Why does this page draw no Venn diagram for part (a)?
Overlap regions are what a Venn diagram makes readable, and inclusion-exclusion prices a union out of intersections rather than the reverse, so the picture adds nothing to a question that already asks for the middle region. The three events are not mutually exclusive: they hold together with probability 3/64, which is the whole answer. Three circles are the standard Venn drawing, so the third option is simply false. And the demands do share a cell, A(2,2), which is the observation the section opens with.

3. Part (b): ten decisions, and only five of them matter

A subset is drawn uniformly at random from all subsets of {1, 2, ..., 10}. The sample space Ω is the family of all those subsets, each equally likely. A product of whole numbers is odd exactly when every factor is odd, because one even factor anywhere makes the whole product even, so the event is a condition on membership: the subset contains no even number. The evens are 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10, and there are five of them.

The empty subset, decided on purposeThe empty subset is a legitimate draw and has no elements to multiply. The convention is that an empty product equals 1, for the same reason an empty sum equals 0: it is the value that leaves any product unchanged. One is odd, so the empty subset is inside the event. It needs no special handling in any route below, because "contains no even number" is true of it by inspection and it is already counted wherever the odd-only subsets are. Stating the convention is still worth a line of your solution: a marker cannot tell a decision from an oversight.

Route 1: counting the subsets

  1. Count the whole space. Each of the ten numbers is in or out, and each complete run of ten decisions gives one distinct subset, so |Ω| = 210 = 1,024.
  2. Count the favourable subsets. A subset with an odd product contains no even number, so it is entirely a subset of the five odd numbers {1, 3, 5, 7, 9}. Those have 25 = 32 subsets, the empty one among them.
  3. Take the ratio. 32 ÷ 1,024 = 1/32, which is 0.03125.

Route 2: the set picture, read with the axioms

Here the circles earn their place, because the event is the complement of a union. For each even number k, let Ek be the event that the drawn subset contains k. "The product is odd" is exactly "none of E2, E4, E6, E8, E10 happens", which is the region of Ω outside all five circles.

P(no even) = 1 − P(E2 ∪ E4 ∪ E6 ∪ E8 ∪ E10) Read as: the chance of avoiding every even number is one minus the chance of catching at least one. Ek is the event that the drawn subset contains the even number k. ∪ is read "or", so the union is the event that at least one even is in. The complement rule, P(Ac) = 1 − P(A), follows from the axioms: A and its complement are disjoint and together make Ω, which has probability 1.

The union is priced by inclusion-exclusion: add the five singles, subtract the ten pairs, add the ten triples, subtract the five quadruples, add the one quintuple. Each Ek has probability 1/2, and any j of them holding together has probability (1/2)j, because j named numbers being in is j independent decisions going one way. The number of ways to pick j of the five circles is C(5, j).

  1. Write the alternating sum. P(union) = C(5,1)(1/2) − C(5,2)(1/2)2 + C(5,3)(1/2)3 − C(5,4)(1/2)4 + C(5,5)(1/2)5.
  2. Put the numbers in. 5(1/2) − 10(1/4) + 10(1/8) − 5(1/16) + 1(1/32).
  3. Work it over a common denominator of 32. 80/32 − 80/32 + 40/32 − 10/32 + 1/32 = 31/32.
  4. Take the complement. P(no even) = 1 − 31/32 = 1/32, which is 0.03125.
  5. Notice the shortcut the arithmetic hides. That alternating sum is the binomial expansion of 1 − (1 − 1/2)5, so it was always going to be 1 − 1/32.

Route 3: ten independent halvings

This route replaces the draw with an equivalent experiment and never counts past two. Walk the ten numbers and flip a fair coin for each: heads it joins, tails it stays out. Every subset comes from exactly one run of flips and every run gives exactly one subset, so the two experiments share a sample space and a uniform measure. The draw is ten independent fair decisions. That each is exactly a half deserves one line rather than an assumption: pair every subset with the one got by toggling the number 2. Toggling twice returns the original, so the pairing is one for one with nothing over, and two equal piles inside 1,024 subsets are 512 each. The same works on any of the ten numbers.

P(product odd) = (1/2)5 = 1/32 Read as: five independent fair decisions all going one way. Each 1/2 is one even number staying out. The exponent 5 is the count of even numbers in {1, ..., 10}, not the count of numbers in the subset. The five odd numbers contribute no factor at all, because each is free to be in or out either way.
  1. Restate the event per decision. The product is odd exactly when the five decisions about 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 all come out "stays out".
  2. Price one decision. Each is a fair coin by the pairing argument above, so 1/2.
  3. Multiply, by independence of the ten decisions. (1/2)5 = 1 ÷ 32.
  4. Confirm the odd numbers cost nothing. Each of 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 may go either way, contributing a factor of 1. So the answer is 1/32, which is 0.03125. Three for three, again.

Answer from memory before revealing.

The draw comes up empty. Which side of the event does it fall on?
The empty product is 1 by the convention that makes it the neutral value for multiplication, and 1 is odd, so the empty subset sits inside the event and is one of the 32 counted. The first option treats a genuine outcome as no outcome, which loses a case the uniform draw really can produce. The second names the empty sum's value rather than the empty product's. And the third makes the answer depend on the odd numbers, which contribute nothing here: the empty subset qualifies by containing no even number, not by containing anything.
Which fact about the ten numbers fixes the answer at one over thirty-two?
Five even numbers, each of which has to be excluded, give five forced fair decisions and a factor of one half apiece. The first option states the same census from the other side, but it is the evens that are forced and the odds that are free, so counting the odds names the numbers that cost nothing. The third confuses the size of the subset with the parity of its product, and a one-element subset holding 3 is a counterexample. The fourth is true and is the denominator, but on its own it says nothing about how many subsets qualify.

5. Six routes, one pair of answers

Part (a) is 3/64 and part (b) is 1/32, each reached three separate ways. That agreement is not decoration: an arithmetic slip shows up as two routes disagreeing, and in a problem whose answer nobody has told you there is no other way to catch one.

Your teacher. If you got 3/64 and 1/32 by one route each, you have the marks. The exercise worth your evening is the second route: pick one you did not use, run it cold without looking at the first, and see whether it lands. That is the habit the rest of Homework 1 rewards, and in an exam hall it is the only error check available to you. If two routes disagree, resist the urge to hunt for the arithmetic slip first - check whether the two are answering the same question, because a misread condition disguises itself as a calculation error remarkably well.
