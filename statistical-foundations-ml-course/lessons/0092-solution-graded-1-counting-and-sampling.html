Homework 1 · Solution to graded problem 1 · both parts worked

Three blocks of cells give 3/64, and five forced decisions give 1/32

working 9 min The companion to Graded 1, which states the problem and refuses to solve it. Open this one after your own attempt.

After this page

  • You can cut a joint demand on a grid into blocks no condition shares, and multiply their separate probabilities.
  • You can turn an "odd product" or similar membership condition into a count of favourable subsets over a count of all of them.

Before you start: Graded 1: counting and sampling, which states both parts and names their traps, and your own attempt at them.

The one-minute version

  • Part (a) is 3/64, which is 0.046875. Part (b) is 1/32, which is 0.03125. Both are exact.
  • Part (a): pinning A2,2 splits the nine constrained cells into three blocks that share nothing, so their probabilities multiply.
  • Part (b): an odd product means no even element, so the favourable subsets are the 32 subsets of {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} out of 1,024.
  • Both parts are the finite uniform model: every outcome equally likely, so a probability is a ratio of counts.

1. Part (a): nine cells are constrained and sixteen are free

Write the three demands as three events. R is the event that the second row of the grid totals 4. C is the event that the second column totals 3. E is the event that the shared entry A2,2 equals 1. The sheet asks for P(R ∩ C ∩ E), where ∩ is read "and": the outcomes lying in all three events at once. Everything below starts from one drawing.

The sheet's phrase independently across entries is a licence, and the solution spends it. Lecture 3, Independence defines it: a family of events is independent when the probability of any collection of them holding together is the product of their separate probabilities. The part used here is the grouping property that comes with it - events read off disjoint blocks of independent entries are themselves independent, because no entry appears in two of them.

P(R ∩ C ∩ E) = P(E) × P(R′) × P(C′) Read as: three probabilities multiplied. P is the probability measure. E is the event that A2,2 holds a 1. R′ is the event that row 2's four remaining cells total 3. C′ is the event that column 2's four remaining cells total 2. The three are read off blocks that share nothing, which is what makes the multiplication legal.

Each factor is now a statement about a handful of fair independent entries, and finite additivity - the third axiom restricted to a finite union of disjoint events, stated in What the axioms force - is what assembles each one by adding up the outcomes it contains.

  1. Price the pinned cell. A2,2 is one fair entry, so P(E) = 1 ÷ 2 = 1/2.
  2. Price the row's remainder. Four fair entries have 24 = 16 equally likely fillings, and C(4, 3) = 4 of them total 3. So P(R′) = 4 ÷ 16 = 1/4.
  3. Price the column's remainder. The same sixteen fillings, and C(4, 2) = 6 of them total 2. So P(C′) = 6 ÷ 16 = 3/8.
  4. Multiply, by independence of the three blocks. (1/2) × (1/4) × (3/8) = 3 ÷ 64.
  5. Read the answer. P(R ∩ C ∩ E) = 3/64, which is 0.046875.

Counting the fillings instead reaches the same 3/64, since 1 × 4 × 6 × 216 favourable grids over 225 total reduces to 24/512, but it spends seven-figure integers to say what the three factors above say in one line, and it never uses the independence the sheet went out of its way to state.

2. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why may the three factors 1/2, 1/4 and 3/8 be multiplied together?
Independent entries in blocks that share no cell give independent events, and independent events multiply. The first option is false of the third demand, which names a single entry and is multiplied in anyway. Equal lengths are a coincidence of a square grid and would not change the argument in a 5 by 7 one. And every entry being equally likely is what makes each individual factor computable, not what licenses the multiplication: the same fair entries arranged in overlapping blocks would not multiply.
Why do the sixteen unconstrained cells not change the answer at all?
No condition names them, so every one of their 216 fillings is favourable, and that factor sits in the numerator and the denominator alike. They are not forced to anything, which is exactly the point. Lying outside the two strips is true but is the reason no condition names them rather than the reason they cancel. And enlarging the sample space is what they do to both counts equally, which is again the cancellation rather than an effect on the ratio.

3. Part (b): five decisions are forced and five are free

A subset is drawn uniformly at random from all subsets of {1, 2, ..., 10}. The sample space Ω is the family of all those subsets, each equally likely. A product of whole numbers is odd exactly when every factor is odd, because one even factor anywhere makes the whole product even, so the event is a condition on membership: the subset contains no even number. The evens are 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10, and there are five of them.

The empty subset, decided on purposeThe empty subset is a legitimate draw and has no elements to multiply. The convention is that an empty product equals 1, for the same reason an empty sum equals 0: it is the value that leaves any product unchanged. One is odd, so the empty subset is inside the event. It needs no special handling below, because "contains no even number" is true of it by inspection and it is already counted among the odd-only subsets. Stating the convention is still worth a line of your solution: a marker cannot tell a decision from an oversight.
P(A) = |A| ÷ |Ω| Read as: a probability is a ratio of two counts. Ω is the sample space, here every subset of the ten numbers, and |Ω| is how many there are. A is the event, here the subsets with an odd product, and |A| is how many of those there are. Only valid when every element of Ω is equally likely, which the sheet states.
  1. Count the whole space. Each of the ten numbers is in or out, and each complete run of ten decisions gives one distinct subset, so |Ω| = 210 = 1,024.
  2. Count the favourable subsets. A subset with an odd product contains no even number, so it is entirely a subset of the five odd numbers {1, 3, 5, 7, 9}. Those have 25 = 32 subsets, the empty one among them.
  3. Take the ratio. 32 ÷ 1,024 = 1/32, which is 0.03125.

The same 1/32 arrives as five independent halvings, one per even number ruled out, which is worth seeing once because it shows the five odd numbers costing nothing; the counting above is what the two marks are for, because it needs no independence argument to be written down first.

4. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

The draw comes up empty. Which side of the event does it fall on?
The empty product is 1 by the convention that makes it the neutral value for multiplication, and 1 is odd, so the empty subset sits inside the event and is one of the 32 counted. The first option treats a genuine outcome as no outcome, which loses a case the uniform draw really can produce. The second names the empty sum's value rather than the empty product's. And the third makes the answer depend on the odd numbers, which contribute nothing here: the empty subset qualifies by containing no even number, not by containing anything.
Which fact about the ten numbers fixes the answer at one over thirty-two?
Five even numbers, each of which has to be excluded, give five forced decisions and leave only the 32 subsets of the odd half. The first option states the same census from the other side, but it is the evens that are forced and the odds that are free, so counting the odds names the numbers that cost nothing. The third confuses the size of the subset with the parity of its product, and a one-element subset holding 3 is a counterexample. The fourth is true and is the denominator, but on its own it says nothing about how many subsets qualify.

5. Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

The same 5×5 grid of fair, independent zero-or-one entries, with three new demands: the 3rd row sums to 4, the 3rd column sums to 2, and A3,3 = 0.

Find the probability that all three hold at once.

Hint

The pinned cell now holds a 0, so it contributes nothing to either total. Ask what each strip's remaining four cells must total.

Show the worked solution
  1. Price the pinned cell. A3,3 = 0 is one fair entry, so its probability is 1/2.
  2. Price the row's remainder. The pinned cell adds 0, so row 3's other four must total 4. Only one of their 16 fillings does, so 1/16.
  3. Price the column's remainder. Column 3's other four must total 2, which C(4, 2) = 6 fillings do, so 6/16 = 3/8.
  4. Multiply over the three disjoint blocks. (1/2) × (1/16) × (3/8) = 3/256 ≈ 0.0117.

Sanity check. This must come out below the page's 3/64, because pinning the cell to 0 forces the row's other four cells to be all ones instead of three of four. 3/256 is a quarter of 3/64, as it must be: 1/16 replaced 1/4.

Carry this away

  • Pinning the shared cell is what turns two overlapping strips into three blocks that share nothing, and only then may the probabilities multiply.
  • Cells no condition names contribute the same factor to the favourable count and the total, so they cancel and can be ignored from the start.
  • An odd product means no even factor, so part (b) is a membership condition and the answer is 25 subsets over 210.
