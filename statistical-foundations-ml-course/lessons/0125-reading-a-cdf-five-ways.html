TA Session 3 · Part 5 of 10 · Problem 4

The height of a jump is the probability of that point, and every question here is that one sentence

working 13 min Problem 4 on the sheet, worked on both sittings and the one that took the longest. Five sub-questions, one staircase, and one identity behind all of them.

After this page

  • You can plot a piecewise distribution function with its jumps closed on the correct side.
  • You can answer a strict inequality, a half-open interval and a point probability off that plot.
  • You can recover the whole mass function from the jumps and check it by summing to one.

Before you start: Reading probability off the staircase, which is where the differencing rule was first stated.

The one-minute version

  • The distribution function is 0 below 0, then 1/2, then 2/3, then 11/12, then 1 from 3 upward.
  • Everything here comes from one identity: P(X = a) = F(a) − F(a−), the height of the jump at a.
  • The five answers: 1/2, 1/12, 11/12 and 1/6, with the plot as the first.
  • The whole mass function follows: 1/2, 1/6, 1/4, 1/12 at 0, 1, 2 and 3.
  • The trap, walked into repeatedly in the room: the values on a CDF are already cumulative, so you difference them and never add them.

1. The question, restated in full

The distribution function of the random variable X is given by F(x) = 0 for x < 0; 1/2 for 0 ≤ x < 1; 2/3 for 1 ≤ x < 2; 11/12 for 2 ≤ x < 3; and 1 for 3 ≤ x. Then: (a) plot this distribution function; (b) what is P(X > 1/2); (c) what is P(2 < X ≤ 4); (d) what is P(X < 3); (e) what is P(X = 1). The sheet cites Ross, Chapter 4, end-of-chapter Problem 4, p. 142, and the tutor said he had modified the wording to match what the class had covered.

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)   and   P(X = a) = F(a) − F(a−) X is the random variable. x and a are ordinary real numbers you choose. F is the distribution function: at any x it reports the total probability at or below x. F(a−), read "F at a minus", is the limit of F approaching a from the left, which is the total strictly below a. Their difference is therefore the mass sitting exactly at a, which is the height of the jump.

2. (a) The plot

Below 0 the function is flat at 0. At x = 0 it jumps to 1/2 and stays there until 1, where it jumps to 2/3, then to 11/12 at 2 and to 1 at 3. Each jump is closed on the right, meaning the value at the jump point is the new higher one, because F is defined with ≤. A filled circle marks the value taken and an open circle marks the level just left of it.

3. (b) to (e), worked

  1. (b) P(X > 1/2). The complement of X ≤ 1/2, so it is 1 − F(1/2). Half sits inside the step [0, 1), where F is 1/2. So the answer is 1 − 1/2 = 1/2.
  2. (c) P(2 < X ≤ 4). A half-open interval is a straight difference: F(4) − F(2). F(4) = 1 and F(2) = 11/12, so the answer is 1 − 11/12 = 1/12.
  3. (d) P(X < 3). Strictly less than, so it is the left limit F(3−), which is the height of the step just before 3. That step is [2, 3), where F is 11/12.
  4. (e) P(X = 1). The jump at 1: F(1) − F(1−) = 2/3 − 1/2. Over six, that is 4/6 − 3/6 = 1/6.

Sub-question (d) is the one worth slowing down for. Two readings of it were offered in the room and both were wrong: one added 1/2, 2/3 and 11/12 together, and one gave 2/3 on the grounds that 3 should be excluded by dropping a step. The correct move needs no arithmetic at all. Strictly below 3 is everything up to the last step before 3, and that step's height is already the total of everything below it, so you read 11/12 off the plot and stop.

The heading on the source says PMF and the object is a CDFA student pointed out that the working was headed probability mass function while every value in it was cumulative, and said that the mismatch was exactly what was causing the confusion. The tutor agreed on the spot and said he would change the heading to cumulative distribution function. The observation is fair and the correction is right: nothing in the list is a mass, and the second student's remark that the values themselves prove it is the fastest test, since 1/2 plus 2/3 plus 11/12 already exceeds 1 and no mass function can do that.

4. Recovering the whole mass function

The Sunday sitting did not stop at (e). It expanded the sub-question into all four point probabilities, because the same identity gives each one, and because the result then permits a check that the earlier answers cannot get on their own.

  1. P(X = 0) = F(0) − F(0−) = 1/2 − 0 = 1/2.
  2. P(X = 1) = F(1) − F(1−) = 2/3 − 1/2 = 1/6.
  3. P(X = 2) = F(2) − F(2−) = 11/12 − 2/3 = 11/12 − 8/12 = 1/4.
  4. P(X = 3) = F(3) − F(3−) = 1 − 11/12 = 1/12.
  5. Check the total. Over twelve: 6/12 + 2/12 + 3/12 + 1/12 = 12/12 = 1. Every jump has been found and nothing is left over.

The tutor's own reason for going this far was to make one point: this variable takes the values 0, 1, 2, 3, exactly like the head count of three coin tosses, and it is not a binomial. Its mass falls, rises and falls, which no Bin(3, p) ever does. A cumulative function must climb; a mass function is under no such obligation.

Chapter 4 of Ross, A First Course in Probability states the same jump identity as a property of the distribution function, and works a staircase of this shape as its own example.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is P(X < 3) equal to 11/12 rather than to 1?
Strictly below 3 means the left limit at 3, which is the height of the step running from 2 up to 3, namely 11/12; the last twelfth arrives only when 3 itself is admitted. The first option is a description of the number rather than a reason. The second wrongly drops 2 as well, when 2 is comfortably below 3. The third subtracts the wrong thing: it is the mass at 3, not at 2, that is being excluded.
What is P(X = 2.5) for this variable?
A point carries mass only where the staircase jumps, and F is flat across 2.5, so the mass there is zero. The second option confuses a step with a jump. The third reads a cumulative value as a point probability, which is the trap the whole page is about. The fourth invents a rounding rule that no random variable obeys anywhere.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A variable Y has F(y) = 0 for y < 1; 1/5 for 1 ≤ y < 2; 7/10 for 2 ≤ y < 5; and 1 for 5 ≤ y.

Find the whole mass function of Y, then P(Y > 2) and P(Y < 5).

Hint

Three jumps means three points of mass. For the two inequalities, decide first whether the endpoint is included, then read a height or its complement.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the jump points. F changes value at 1, 2 and 5, so the support is {1, 2, 5}.
  2. P(Y = 1) = 1/5 − 0 = 1/5.
  3. P(Y = 2) = 7/10 − 1/5 = 7/10 − 2/10 = 1/2.
  4. P(Y = 5) = 1 − 7/10 = 3/10.
  5. P(Y > 2). The complement of Y ≤ 2, so 1 − F(2) = 1 − 7/10 = 3/10.
  6. P(Y < 5). Strictly below, so the left limit F(5−), the height of the step [2, 5), which is 7/10.

Sanity check. Over ten the three masses are 2, 5 and 3, which add to 10, so they sum to 1. The two inequalities must also be consistent with the masses: P(Y > 2) should equal P(Y = 5), and 3/10 matches; P(Y < 5) should equal P(Y = 1) plus P(Y = 2), and 2/10 plus 5/10 is 7/10.

Carry this away

  • The mass at a point is the size of the jump there, F at the point minus its left limit.
  • A half-open interval is a difference of two heights; a strict inequality is a left limit or a complement.
  • CDF values are already totals, so you difference them and never add them.
  • The recovered masses must sum to 1, which finds any jump you missed.

Your teacher. Draw this staircase on paper, mark each filled and open circle by hand, and then answer all five sub-questions with a ruler rather than a formula. The identity you are learning is one sentence long and completely obvious in a picture, and completely slippery in symbols, which is why every wrong answer given in the room was given by someone reasoning symbolically. Once you have done it once by ruler, the symbols stop being a separate skill.
