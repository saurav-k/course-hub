TA Session 1 · Part 3 of 8 · Problems 4 and 5

Fill the seven regions once, and six answers fall out

working 16 min Tutorial problems 4 and 5. Inclusion-exclusion done as geography rather than algebra, then the identity that measures distance from independence.

The one-minute version

  • With P(A), P(B), P(C) and all four overlaps given, fill all seven Venn regions with numbers first; every quantity asked for is then read straight off the picture.
  • The six answers: 0.9, 0.1, 0.3, 0.7, 0.4, 0.7, each confirmed a second way by inclusion-exclusion or De Morgan.
  • "Exactly one of A or B" costs P(A) + P(B) − 2P(A∩B): the intersection is subtracted once per parent.
  • The four expressions of problem 5(ii) are one number wearing four costumes - the gap between P(A∩B) and P(A)P(B).

Problem 4, restated

A probability space carries three events A, B and C with these probabilities, quoted from the sheet:

P(A)P(B)P(C)P(A∩B)P(A∩C)P(B∩C)P(A∩B∩C)
0.60.50.40.30.20.20.1

Here A∩B is read "both A and B occur", the outcomes the two events share. Find: (i) P(A∪B∪C), at least one of the three occurring, and P(Ac∩Bc∩Cc), none of them; (ii) P((A∪B)∩C) and P(A∪(B∩C)); (iii) P((Ac∪Bc)∩Cc) and P((Ac∩Bc)∪Cc).

Which tools are in play, and why these

Four instruments, each pulled for a stated reason. The difference rule, P(A∩Bc) = P(A) − P(A∩B), peels an overlap off an event, which is how regions get filled. The inclusion-exclusion formula computes a union of overlapping events without needing them disjoint. De Morgan's laws convert "none of them" into the complement of "at least one", converting unknowns into things already known. The distributive law, (A∪B)∩C = (A∩C)∪(B∩C), pulls intersections through unions so the given overlaps become visible.

Step one: fill the picture

The triple overlap holds 0.1. Every pairwise overlap includes the triple, so subtracting peels it off: the A-and-B-only region holds 0.3 − 0.1 = 0.2, and similarly for the other two pairs. Each single-event probability counts three regions, so subtracting what is already placed leaves the exclusive remainder:

  1. Place the triple. All-three region = P(A∩B∩C) = 0.1.
  2. Peel the pairs. A-and-B-only = P(A∩B) − 0.1 = 0.3 − 0.1 = 0.2. Likewise A-and-C-only = 0.2 − 0.1 = 0.1, and B-and-C-only = 0.2 − 0.1 = 0.1.
  3. Peel the solos. A alone = P(A) − (its three interior regions) = 0.6 − 0.2 − 0.1 − 0.1 = 0.2. Likewise B alone = 0.5 − 0.2 − 0.1 − 0.1 = 0.1, and C alone = 0.4 − 0.1 − 0.1 − 0.1 = 0.1.
  4. Total the interior. 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.9. The outside region therefore holds 1 − 0.9 = 0.1, since probabilities over the whole space sum to one.

Steps two to four: read the six answers, then confirm them

  1. (i) At least one occurs is the whole flower: 0.9. Confirmed by inclusion-exclusion, adding singles, subtracting pairwise overlaps because they were counted twice, re-adding the triple because it was counted three times and removed three times: 0.6 + 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.3 − 0.2 − 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.9. Match.
  2. (i) None occurs is De Morgan in action: "not (A or B or C)" = Ac∩Bc∩Cc, which is the outside region: 1 − 0.9 = 0.1.
  3. (ii) (A∪B)∩C distributes into (A∩C)∪(B∩C): the two lower petals plus the gold middle, 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.3. By the addition rule, 0.2 + 0.2 − 0.1 = 0.3. Match.
  4. (ii) A∪(B∩C) covers A together with the B-C lens: 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.7. By the addition rule, P(A) + P(B∩C) − P(A∩B∩C) = 0.6 + 0.2 − 0.1 = 0.7. Match.
  5. (iii) (Ac∪Bc)∩Cc: De Morgan turns the first bracket into (A∩B)c, so the whole expression is the complement of (A∩B)∪C - everything outside the A-B capsule and outside C: A alone, B alone, and the outside, 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.4. Confirmed by computing P((A∩B)∪C) = 0.3 + 0.4 − 0.1 = 0.6 and taking 1 − 0.6 = 0.4. Match.
  6. (iii) (Ac∩Bc)∪Cc: De Morgan turns the brackets into (A∪B)c, so this is the complement of (A∪B)∩C, whose value 0.3 came from step 3: 1 − 0.3 = 0.7. On the diagram it is five of the seven interior regions plus the outside: 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.7. Match.
Where this goes wrongThe classic error is forgetting that a pairwise overlap already contains the triple, which produces regions like 0.3 sitting beside 0.1 instead of 0.2 beside it, and every downstream number inherits the damage. The second is treating (Ac∪Bc)∩Cc as something exotic: two lines of De Morgan reduce it to a complement of a union, and the diagram had already priced it. Fill regions before touching formulas, and use the formulas to confirm rather than to discover.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the inclusion-exclusion formula for three events, why does the triple intersection come back with a plus sign?
An outcome in all three events is added once per single (three times) and subtracted once per pair (three times), netting zero until the triple is added back once. It lies inside all three events, not outside them. De Morgan has nothing to do with the signs here. And the singles are added, never subtracted.

Problem 5, restated

Part (i): show that the probability that exactly one of the events A or B occurs equals P(A) + P(B) − 2P(A∩B). Part (ii): show that four expressions are equal - P(A∩B) − P(A)P(B); P(A)P(Bc) − P(A∩Bc); P(Ac)P(B) − P(Ac∩B); and P((A∪B)c) − P(Ac)P(Bc).

Part (i): the picture shows where the 2 comes from

  1. Name the target event. Exactly one of A or B is (A∩Bc) ∪ (Ac∩B): A fires while B does not, or B fires while A does not.
  2. Note the two pieces cannot collide. Their intersection demands A ∉ B and B ∉ A simultaneously - impossible - so the union is disjoint and its probability is the sum.
  3. Convert each crescent with the difference rule. P(A∩Bc) = P(A) − P(A∩B): everything in A that survives deleting the overlap. Likewise P(Ac∩B) = P(B) − P(A∩B).
  4. Add them. P(exactly one) = P(A) − P(A∩B) + P(B) − P(A∩B) = P(A) + P(B) − 2P(A∩B), as required.

Part (ii): four costumes, one number

Give the star of the show a name:

Δ = P(A∩B) − P(A)P(B) Read as: delta, the gap between how often A and B actually occur together and how often they would if independence held - independence being exactly the case where the product rule pins the joint probability to the product of the marginals. Delta is zero precisely when the pair is independent.

The first expression is Δ itself. The other three are proved as a cycle: each folds back into the previous face, and the last one unfolds straight into Δ, closing the loop.

  1. Face two, the A-side view. Substitute the two complement facts P(Bc) = 1 − P(B) and P(A∩Bc) = P(A) − P(A∩B): P(A)(1 − P(B)) − (P(A) − P(A∩B)) = P(A) − P(A)P(B) − P(A) + P(A∩B) = Δ. The P(A) terms cancel in pairs.
  2. Face three, the B-side view. Swap the roles of A and B in face two - the statement is symmetric under the swap - giving P(Ac)P(B) − P(Ac∩B) = (1 − P(A))P(B) − (P(B) − P(A∩B)) = P(B) − P(A)P(B) − P(B) + P(A∩B) = Δ. Same cancellation, other side.
  3. Face four, the outside view. By De Morgan (A∪B)c = Ac∩Bc, so P((A∪B)c) = 1 − P(A) − P(B) + P(A∩B) using the addition rule. Meanwhile P(Ac)P(Bc) = (1 − P(A))(1 − P(B)) = 1 − P(A) − P(B) + P(A)P(B). Subtracting, everything cancels except P(A∩B) − P(A)P(B) = Δ.
  4. The cycle closes. Faces one to four equal Δ, Δ, Δ, Δ. Four separate-looking quantities are one statement: how far A and B sit from independence, measurable from either event's own perspective or from the world where neither occurs.
Where this goes wrongThe reflex is proving the four faces independently, which quadruples the algebra and hides the point. The second slip is sign hygiene in face four: expanding (1 − P(A))(1 − P(B)) produces four terms, and dropping even one makes delta look negative. Expand slowly, cancel deliberately, and let the diagram carry the meaning of part (i) so the algebra only confirms what the crescents already showed.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

If A and B are independent, what value does the exactly-one probability P(A) + P(B) − 2P(A∩B) take?
Independence swaps the joint term for the product P(A)P(B); it does not erase anything. Zero would mean exactly one never happens, false whenever either event alone can fire. One half is a coincidence of special numbers, not a theorem. And doubling the joint is backwards: the joint is subtracted, not added.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1 - refill the regions with new numbersworking

New data: P(A) = 0.7, P(B) = 0.6, P(C) = 0.5, P(A∩B) = 0.4, P(A∩C) = 0.3, P(B∩C) = 0.3, P(A∩B∩C) = 0.2. Find P(A∪B∪C) and P(Ac∩Bc∩Cc), and say what is unusual about the second answer.

Hint

Run the same peel: triple first, then pairs, then solos, then total.

Show the worked solution
  1. Regions. Triple = 0.2. Pairs only: AB = 0.2, AC = 0.1, BC = 0.1. Solos: A = 0.7 − 0.2 − 0.1 − 0.2 = 0.2; B = 0.6 − 0.2 − 0.1 − 0.2 = 0.1; C = 0.5 − 0.1 − 0.1 − 0.2 = 0.1.
  2. Interior total. 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.2 = 1.0, so P(A∪B∪C) = 1.
  3. None occurs. 1 − 1.0 = 0.
  4. What is unusual. The outside region has probability exactly zero: at least one of the three events is certain, without any of them being certain alone. Inclusion-exclusion agrees: 0.7 + 0.6 + 0.5 − 0.4 − 0.3 − 0.3 + 0.2 = 1.0.

Sanity check. Seven non-negative regions summing to exactly one, and every given marginal reproduced - both checks pass, so the fill is consistent.

Practice 2 - run delta on real numbersfoundation

Take P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.5, P(A∩B) = 0.3 from problem 4. Compute Δ, the exactly-one probability, and all four faces of part (ii) numerically, checking they land on the same value.

Hint

Delta comes straight from its definition; each face needs one line of substitution.

Show the worked solution
  1. Delta. 0.3 − 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3 − 0.30 = 0.
  2. Exactly one. 0.6 + 0.5 − 2 × 0.3 = 0.5.
  3. Face two. 0.6 × 0.5 − 0.3 = 0.30 − 0.30 = 0, since P(A∩Bc) = 0.6 − 0.3 = 0.3.
  4. Face three. Symmetrically, P(Ac∩B) = 0.5 − 0.3 = 0.2, so 0.4 × 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.
  5. Face four. P(neither) = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2 and P(Ac)P(Bc) = 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2, so the difference is 0.
  6. Read the verdict. Delta is zero: A and B are independent in problem 4's data, and all four faces report the same fact.

Sanity check. Independence means P(A∩B) equals P(A)P(B) exactly, so a delta of zero was the expected landing place, not a surprise.

Your teacher. If the region-filling felt mechanical, push it one step further: ask what constraint stops the outside region from going negative, and notice that the seven regions plus the outside always sum to one no matter what numbers you start from - which is exactly why inconsistent givens expose themselves as a negative region somewhere.
