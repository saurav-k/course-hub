Lecture 6 · Part 6 of 14 · what the CDF buys you

Once you have the staircase, four other probabilities cost one subtraction each

11 min the payoff Why the notes bother proving a theorem about a function nobody asked for: it answers four questions at once.

The one-minute version

  • The notes write four lines under the heading the CDF allows computation of probabilities of interest, and each one is a single subtraction.
  • Upper tail. P(X > x) = 1 − FX(x).
  • Half-open interval. P(X ∈ (a, b]) = FX(b) − FX(a). Note the shape of the bracket: open at a, closed at b.
  • Strict inequality. P(X < x) = FX(x−), the value the staircase approaches from the left rather than the one it takes.
  • An exact value. P(X = x) = FX(x) − FX(x−), and the notes add the consequence: a value has positive probability exactly when the CDF is discontinuous there, with a jump of exactly that size.

1. The one new piece of notation

Three of the four lines use FX(x−), with a minus sign written after the argument. It is not a subtraction and it is not a negative number. It names the left limit: the height the staircase is approaching as the threshold comes up to x from below, without ever reaching it.

FX(x−) = the limit of FX(t) as t rises towards x from below t is a threshold strictly below x that is being moved rightwards towards it. The value the CDF settles on along the way is the left limit. On a staircase this is simply the height of the tread you are standing on just before the step. Compare it with FX(x) itself, which by Part 5 is the height after the step, because a CDF is right continuous.

Where the CDF has no jump at x the two agree and the distinction costs nothing. Where it does jump, the two differ by exactly the height of the jump, and that gap is the subject of section 4.

2. The upper tail

The first line is a complement, and nothing about it is new: it is the complement rule from Lecture 3, Part 1 applied to the event the CDF already prices.

P(X > x) = 1 − FX(x) X > x is the event that the value comes out strictly above the threshold. It is the complement of X ≤ x, because a real number is either at most x or greater than x and never both. Since the two probabilities must add to one, subtracting gives the tail.

3. A half-open interval, and why the bracket is shaped that way

The second line prices an interval, and the notes are precise about which endpoints are in it.

P(X ∈ (a, b]) = FX(b) − FX(a) (a, b] is a half-open interval: the round bracket at a means a is excluded and the square bracket at b means b is included. So the event is "the value is strictly above a and at most b". FX(b) is everything at or below b and FX(a) is everything at or below a; subtracting removes the part that was already collected by a.

The bracket shape is not a stylistic choice. Both terms in the subtraction are of the form "at most", so what survives the subtraction is "at most b, but not at most a", which is exactly "above a and at most b". Any other bracket combination needs a left limit somewhere, which is why the notes state this one and only this one.

The interval (0, 2] contains the support points 1 and 2 and excludes 0, so its probability should be 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4, and the difference of CDF values gives 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. They agree. The notes state the formula and run no such check; this one is this course's own and is here because a formula you have verified once on a case you can see is a formula you will trust under pressure.

4. The strict inequality, and the exact value

The last two lines are where the left limit earns its place. Both are stated in the notes in one line each.

P(X < x) = FX(x−) The event is now strictly below the threshold, so whatever mass sits exactly at x must be excluded. The left limit is precisely the total collected before arriving at x, so it is the answer.
P(X = x) = FX(x) − FX(x−) "At most x" minus "strictly below x" leaves "exactly x". FX(x) is the height after the step and FX(x−) is the height before it, so the difference is the height of the step itself.

The notes then state the consequence explicitly, and it is the most quotable line in the whole theorem: P(X = x) > 0 exactly when FX is discontinuous at x, and the jump there has size P(X = x).

Read both directions of that. A point carrying mass forces a jump. A point carrying no mass forces no jump. So the discontinuities of the CDF are not an awkward feature to work around; they are a complete list of the places where the random variable puts positive weight on a single value.

  1. Take the two-toss example at x = 1. The staircase is at 1/4 just before and at 3/4 at the point.
  2. Left limit. FX(1−) = 1/4, so P(X < 1) = 1/4. That is the mass at 0 alone, which is right, since 0 is the only support point strictly below 1.
  3. Value at the point. FX(1) = 3/4, by right continuity.
  4. Subtract. P(X = 1) = 3/4 − 1/4 = 1/2, which is exactly the mass the PMF puts at 1.
  5. Take a point off the support, say x = 1.5. There is no jump there, so the left limit and the value agree, both at 3/4, and the difference is zero. The formula reports P(X = 1.5) = 0, agreeing with the PMF's "zero everywhere else".

5. Where this is heading

The last formula is the hinge of the whole lecture, and it is worth flagging now what happens to it later.

For a discrete random variable, the CDF is all jumps and flat treads, and the jumps recover the PMF exactly. Nothing is lost by describing the variable one way rather than the other. In Part 11 a random variable arrives whose CDF has no jumps at all. Apply the last formula to it and you get P(X = x) = 0 at every single point, which sounds like a contradiction and is not. The formula is doing its job; it is the assumption that a variable must put mass on individual points that turns out to be wrong.

6. Check yourself

For the two-toss head count, what is the probability that X is strictly less than 2?
A strict inequality asks for the left limit, and just before 2 the staircase stands at three quarters, which is the masses at 0 and 1 added. One would be the answer to "at most 2", which includes the value 2 and its quarter of mass, and confusing the two is exactly what the left-limit notation exists to prevent. One quarter is the mass at 2, so it is what the strict inequality leaves out rather than what it collects. And one half is the mass at 1 alone, which ignores the mass at 0.
A CDF is continuous at the point x, with no jump there. What does that say about X?
The last formula makes P(X equals x) the size of the jump, so no jump means zero probability at that exact point, and that is all it means. It does not make the value impossible: Part 11 has variables that are certain to land somewhere in an interval while every individual point in it carries probability zero. Flatness on both sides is a stronger claim than continuity, and a CDF can climb steadily through a point without jumping at it. And the support is about which values occur, which is a different question from whether a single one of them carries positive mass.
Your teacher. Every one of these four formulas is a subtraction of two "at most" quantities, and that is not a coincidence: the CDF only knows how to answer "at most", so anything else has to be assembled out of two of them. When a probability question arrives in an unfamiliar bracket shape, do not hunt for a formula. Rewrite the event as a difference of two "at most" events and read the answer off. That habit outlasts the four lines and handles the shapes the notes never listed.
