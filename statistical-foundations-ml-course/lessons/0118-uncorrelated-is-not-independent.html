Independence buys you a product rule, and the product rule does not buy independence back

working 12 min Four definitions arrive in one paragraph of the notes - uncorrelated, correlation, covariance, orthogonal - and then a four-value example knocks the arrow between them down to one direction.

After this page

  • You can prove E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] for independent discrete variables by factoring the joint PMF, without skipping the step where the double sum splits.
  • You can define covariance both ways, say what the diagonal of a covariance matrix holds, and tell uncorrelated apart from orthogonal.
  • You can walk the counterexample cell by cell and name the single cell that destroys independence while the covariance stays exactly zero.

Before you start: Variance as a second moment, the first pre-read page, and Lecture 8, Part 6, which showed that linearity of expectation never needs independence. This page is about the property that does.

The one-minute version

  • If X and Y are independent, the joint PMF factors, the double sum splits, and E[XY] = E[X]E[Y].
  • That equation is promoted to a definition in its own right: variables satisfying it are called uncorrelated, whether or not they are independent.
  • E[XY] is the correlation. Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − μXμY is the covariance, so uncorrelated is the same statement as zero covariance.
  • For a vector of n variables the covariances form an n by n matrix whose diagonal is the list of variances.
  • The arrow runs one way only. Independent implies uncorrelated; uncorrelated does not imply independent, and the notes settle it with X on four values, Y on two, and their product.

1. The mental model: independence lets the sum split in two

Linearity of expectation adds variables and never asks whether they are related. Multiplying them is different. To say anything about the average of a product you need to know that the two variables do not steer each other, and independence is exactly that permission.

The picture is a rectangle. When X and Y are independent, the joint probability of a cell is the row probability times the column probability, so the two-dimensional table is generated by its two margins. A double sum over a table built like that separates into a sum over rows times a sum over columns, and each of those is an ordinary one-variable expectation.

2. The mechanism, every symbol named

If X and Y are independent, then E[XY] = E[X] E[Y] X and Y are two random variables defined on the same experiment. XY is the new random variable formed by multiplying whatever the two of them happen to take on the same run. E[·] is expectation, the probability-weighted average from Lecture 8, Part 1. Independent means the joint PMF factors: pXY(x,y) = pX(x) pY(y) at every pair of values.
  1. Start from LOTUS on two variables. Lecture 8, Part 5 gave the rule for a function of a pair, so with g(x,y) = xy we get E[XY] = Σx Σy xy pXY(x,y). No new distribution for the product is needed anywhere.
  2. Use independence once. Replace the joint PMF by the product of the marginals: E[XY] = Σx Σy xy pX(x) pY(y). This is the only place independence is used, and everything after it is bookkeeping.
  3. Group each factor with its own variable. The summand is [x pX(x)] · [y pY(y)]. Nothing in the first bracket depends on y and nothing in the second depends on x.
  4. Pull the x terms out of the inner sum. The inner sum runs over y, so E[XY] = Σx x pX(x) · ( Σy y pY(y) ).
  5. Notice the inner sum is a constant. Σy y pY(y) is just E[Y], a number, so it comes out of the outer sum too.
  6. Read off the result. What is left is ( Σx x pX(x) ) · E[Y] = E[X] E[Y]. The double sum has become a product of two single sums, which is the whole theorem.

3. Four definitions, all built on that one equation

The notes now do something worth noticing: they take the conclusion of the theorem and make it a definition, deliberately detached from the hypothesis that produced it.

Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − μXμY μX is E[X], the mean of X, and μY is E[Y]. X − μX is the deviation of X from its own mean on a given run. Cov(X,Y) is the average product of the two deviations. The second form is what you actually compute with: expand the brackets, use linearity, and the cross terms collapse to −μXμY. Setting Y = X turns this into E[X2] − μX2, the variance identity from the previous pre-read page.
  • Uncorrelated: E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]. Equivalently, by the second form above, Cov(X,Y) = 0.
  • Correlation: the notes use this word for the raw quantity E[XY] itself, not for the normalised coefficient between −1 and 1.
  • Covariance: the mean-centred version, the two forms above.
  • Orthogonal: E[XY] = 0. Not the same as uncorrelated - the two coincide only when at least one of the means is zero.

4. Stacking the covariances into a matrix

For a random vector X = (X1, ..., Xn) there are covariances between every pair, and the natural place to put them is a square array. Entry Mij = Cov(Xi, Xj). On the diagonal, i = j, and the covariance of a variable with itself is its variance, so the diagonal reads off the n variances of the vector.

5. The counterexample, cell by cell

Here is the construction the notes use. Let X be uniform on the four values {−2, −1, 1, 2}, each with probability 1/4. Let Y be uniform on {−1, 1}, each with probability 1/2. X and Y are independent by construction. Now define a third variable, Z = XY, which is X with a random sign attached.

Two questions are asked about the pair X and Z: are they uncorrelated, and are they independent? The answers come apart, and that is the point.

  1. Get the mean of X. E[X] = (1/4)(−2) + (1/4)(−1) + (1/4)(1) + (1/4)(2). The values pair up as −2 with 2 and −1 with 1, so E[X] = 0.
  2. Get the mean of Y. E[Y] = (1/2)(−1) + (1/2)(1) = 0. This zero is the hinge of the whole example.
  3. Get the mean of Z. Z is the product of independent X and Y, so by the theorem in section 2, E[Z] = E[X]E[Y] = 0 × 0 = 0.
  4. Write the correlation of Z and X. ZX = (XY)X = X2Y, so E[ZX] = E[X2Y]. This is where the algebra pays: no joint table is needed yet.
  5. Factor it. X2 is a function of X alone and Y is independent of X, so X2 and Y are independent too and the product rule applies: E[X2Y] = E[X2] E[Y].
  6. Kill it with the zero from step 2. Whatever E[X2] turns out to be, it is multiplied by E[Y] = 0. So E[ZX] = 0.
  7. Compare with the product of the means. E[Z]E[X] = 0 × 0 = 0 as well. So E[ZX] = E[Z]E[X], and by the definition in section 3, Z and X are uncorrelated. Their covariance is 0 − 0 × 0 = 0. They are also orthogonal, since E[ZX] is not merely equal to the product of the means but equal to zero.
  8. Now go to a single cell. Ask for P(X = 2, Z = 1). If X is 2 then Z = 2Y, which is either −2 or 2. It can never be 1. So P(X = 2, Z = 1) = 0.
  9. Compute the two margins for the same cell. P(X = 2) = 1/4. And Z = 1 happens on exactly two of the eight equally likely runs, namely X = 1 with Y = 1 and X = −1 with Y = −1, so P(Z = 1) = 1/8 + 1/8 = 1/4.
  10. Read the contradiction. Independence would demand P(X = 2, Z = 1) = P(X = 2) P(Z = 1) = (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16, which is positive. The joint is 0. One cell disagrees, so X and Z are not independent, and one cell is all it takes.
This course's arithmetic, not the lecture'sThe notes stop at E[X2]E[Y] = 0 and never evaluate the second moment, because the zero makes it unnecessary. For completeness: E[X2] = (1/4)(4 + 1 + 1 + 4) = 2.5, so Var(X) = 2.5 − 0 = 2.5. Squaring erases the sign that Y attaches, so Z2 = X2 exactly and Var(Z) = 2.5 as well. The covariance matrix of the pair is therefore 2.5 on both diagonal entries and 0 everywhere off it - the matrix of two independent variables, produced by two variables that are not independent at all. Every figure in this paragraph is this course's own.

The reason the covariance is blind here is worth stating once. Covariance measures the average of a product of deviations, which detects a linear tendency for the two to move together. The relationship between X and Z is |Z| = |X|, a symmetric, entirely non-linear tie. Every positive contribution to the average product is cancelled by an equally likely negative one, and one number cannot report what two mirrored halves cancel.

The product rule is proved for the discrete case onlyThe notes write the proof exactly once, as a double sum over a joint PMF, and never state the continuous version. It does hold there, by the same argument with the double sum replaced by a double integral and the factorisation fXY(x,y) = fX(x) fY(y), and the definitions of covariance, correlation and the covariance matrix are written with no discrete or continuous marking at all, as if the general case had been established. It has not been, here. Treat the continuous statement as an unproved extension in this course until you have run the double integral yourself, in the same way Lecture 8, Part 5 kept LOTUS's two versions separate rather than pretending one proof covered both.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the counterexample, what actually forces the covariance of Z and X to be zero?
The product ZX equals X squared times Y, the two factors are independent, and E of Y is zero, so the whole thing collapses regardless of the second moment. The first option is true of this construction but points the wrong way: being a function of X alone is a reason to expect dependence, not zero covariance. The third is also true here, since Z and X are both uniform on the same four values, and it still says nothing about their joint behaviour. The fourth is simply false: ZX takes the values 4 and 1 with sign, never a constant zero, and only its average is zero.
What does a covariance of zero actually rule out between two random variables?
Covariance is the average product of the two deviations, so it reports a linear tendency and nothing else. The first option is the exact claim this page destroys: X and Z have zero covariance and are heavily dependent. The third fails on the same example, where X fixes the magnitude of Z completely. The fourth invents a carve-out that does not exist, and gets it backwards as well, since a strictly increasing relationship is precisely the kind covariance does detect.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Keep X uniform on {−2, −1, 1, 2} and Y uniform on {−1, 1}, independent, and set W = X + Y instead of the product.

Find Cov(W, X), then decide whether W and X are uncorrelated, and whether they are independent. Say what has changed from the page's example and why.

Hint

Expand E[WX] = E[X2 + XY] with linearity, then use the product rule on the second term. You will need E[X2] = 2.5 from the callout above.

Show the worked solution
  1. Means first. E[X] = 0 and E[Y] = 0 as on the page, so E[W] = E[X] + E[Y] = 0 by linearity.
  2. Write the correlation. WX = (X + Y)X = X2 + XY, so E[WX] = E[X2] + E[XY].
  3. Handle the second term. X and Y are independent, so E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] = 0.
  4. Evaluate the first. E[X2] = (1/4)(4 + 1 + 1 + 4) = 2.5.
  5. Assemble the covariance. Cov(W,X) = E[WX] − E[W]E[X] = 2.5 − 0 = 2.5.
  6. Answer both questions. The covariance is not zero, so W and X are correlated, and correlated variables cannot be independent, since independence would have forced the covariance to zero. So they are dependent as well.
  7. Say what changed. Adding Y shifts X by a symmetric jolt and leaves the linear tie to X fully intact, so the covariance sees it. Multiplying by Y flips the sign at random, which cancels the linear tie exactly while leaving the dependence untouched. The difference is not how strong the dependence is, but whether it is linear.

Sanity check. The covariance of W with X should equal Var(X) whenever the added variable is independent of X and the sum is the second argument, and 2.5 is indeed Var(X) from the callout. It must also be positive, since W is X plus noise and the two must move together.

Carry this away

  • Independence is used exactly once in the product-rule proof, at the step where the joint PMF is replaced by the product of the marginals; everything after that is splitting a double sum.
  • Uncorrelated means E[XY] = E[X]E[Y], which is the same as zero covariance; orthogonal means E[XY] = 0, and the two agree only when a mean vanishes.
  • The covariance matrix of a vector holds every pairwise covariance, is symmetric, and carries the n variances on its diagonal.
  • X on four values, Y on two signs, and Z = XY: zero covariance, and one cell with joint probability 0 against a marginal product of 1/16. Uncorrelated is strictly weaker than independent.

Your teacher. If step 8 of the counterexample feels like a trick, do not fight the algebra - draw the eight points instead, then cover the plot with your hand except for the column at X = 2. Two points, at Z equal to 2 and to −2, and a gap at every other height. Now uncover the column at X = 1: the two points have moved. A joint distribution whose column shape changes with the column cannot factor, and that is independence failing, seen directly. Then ask an agent to generate ten thousand draws of the pair and report the sample covariance and a cross-tabulation side by side: the covariance will sit near zero while the table shows half its cells empty.
