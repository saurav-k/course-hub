TA Session 3 · Part 7 of 10 · Problem 10

More engines only help once each engine is reliable enough to be worth having

working 13 min Problem 10 on the sheet, worked on the slow Saturday sitting. The answer is not a probability but a threshold, and it is not where intuition puts it.

After this page

  • You can turn a comparison between two systems into an inequality between two binomial tails.
  • You can factor and solve that inequality without expanding the polynomials.
  • You can say what the threshold means in engineering terms.

Before you start: The binomial as a distribution, for the mass function this page uses twice.

The one-minute version

  • A plane flies if at least half its engines work. On four engines that means at least 2; on two engines it means at least 1.
  • Model working engines as X ~ Bin(4, p) and Y ~ Bin(2, p), and ask when P(X ≥ 2) > P(Y ≥ 1).
  • Both sides are easier as complements: 1 − (1−p)4 − 4p(1−p)3 against 1 − (1−p)2.
  • The ones cancel, (1−p)2 divides out, and what is left is p(2 − 3p) < 0.
  • The answer is a threshold, not a probability: p > 2/3. Below it, the two-engine plane is safer.

1. The question, restated in full

At least one-half of an airplane's engines are required to function for it to operate. If each engine independently functions with probability p, for what values of p is a 4-engine plane more likely to operate than a 2-engine plane? The sheet cites Ross, Chapter 5, end-of-chapter Problem 5, p. 212.

Two readings need pinning down before the algebra. "At least one-half" of four is two, and of two is one, so the two thresholds are different numbers even though the fraction is the same. And p is the same for every engine on both aircraft: the question is about the count, not about better engines.

2. Setting up the two tails

X ~ Bin(4, p), Y ~ Bin(2, p), and we want P(X ≥ 2) > P(Y ≥ 1) X is the number of working engines on the four-engine plane and Y the number on the two-engine plane. p is the probability that any one engine works, the same for all six engines. Bin(n, p) means n independent trials each succeeding with probability p, counting the successes. The inequality asks when the bigger aircraft is the more likely one to fly.

Both tails have short complements, which is why the working goes that way. The four-engine plane fails when 0 or 1 engines work; the two-engine plane fails only when 0 work.

  1. The four-engine tail. P(X ≥ 2) = 1 − P(X = 0) − P(X = 1).
  2. Substitute the two masses. P(X = 0) = (1−p)4 and P(X = 1) = C(4,1) p(1−p)3 = 4p(1−p)3.
  3. So the left side is 1 − (1−p)4 − 4p(1−p)3.
  4. The two-engine tail. P(Y ≥ 1) = 1 − P(Y = 0) = 1 − (1−p)2.

3. Solving the inequality

  1. Write it out. 1 − (1−p)4 − 4p(1−p)3 > 1 − (1−p)2.
  2. Cancel the ones. They appear on both sides, so subtract 1 from each: −(1−p)4 − 4p(1−p)3 > −(1−p)2.
  3. Multiply by minus one. That flips the inequality: (1−p)4 + 4p(1−p)3 < (1−p)2.
  4. Factor the left side. Both terms carry (1−p)3: it becomes (1−p)3(1 − p + 4p) < (1−p)2.
  5. Divide by (1−p)2. That quantity is positive for any p below 1, so the direction is safe: (1−p)(1 + 3p) < 1.
  6. Expand the product. 1 + 3p − p − 3p2 < 1, that is 1 + 2p − 3p2 < 1.
  7. Cancel the ones again. 2p − 3p2 < 0, so p(2 − 3p) < 0.
  8. Read the sign. p itself is positive, so the second factor must be negative: 2 − 3p < 0, giving 2 < 3p and p > 2/3.

The factoring at step 4 is what keeps this short. Expanding (1−p)4 gives a quartic and the algebra becomes a page of cancellation; taking the common factor out first leaves a quadratic that solves in two lines.

4. What the threshold means

The result is counter-intuitive until you count what the extra engines buy. Going from two engines to four doubles the redundancy but also doubles the number of things that can break, and raises the bar from one working engine to two. If each engine is unreliable, the extra requirement costs more than the extra spare gains, and the small plane is safer.

At p = 2/3 exactly, the two aircraft are equally likely to operate. Real jet engines sit far above that threshold, which is why the four-engine answer matches ordinary intuition in practice and only fails in the region the question is really probing.

Chapter 5 of Ross, A First Course in Probability sets this as an exercise on the binomial and expects exactly the tail comparison worked above.

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is it safe to divide both sides by the quantity one minus p squared?
A square of a real number is never negative, and p below one keeps it strictly positive, so dividing preserves the direction. The second option is false: the quantity varies with p. The third is a dangerous overstatement, since dividing by a negative flips an inequality, which is exactly what the earlier step had to handle. The fourth misdescribes the algebra: the term is a common factor, not a matching pair.
Each engine works with probability 0.6. Which plane should you board?
Two thirds is about 0.667, so 0.6 is below the threshold and the smaller plane is the safer one. The first option is the intuition the problem exists to break. The second treats a real gap as a tie; the two probabilities genuinely differ there. The third states a true fact about the four-engine plane that is outweighed by its higher requirement of two working engines.

Practice about 12 minutes, with a calculator

Practice 1working

This is Problem 6 of the same sheet, which the tutor set as homework and deliberately did not solve, asking the room to attempt it first.

A satellite system consists of 4 components and functions adequately if at least 2 of the 4 components are working. Each component works independently with probability 0.6.

Find the probability that the system functions adequately.

Hint

This is the left-hand side of the page's own inequality, with p set to 0.6. Complement the two failing cases rather than summing the three succeeding ones.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the variable. Let X be the number of working components, so X ~ Bin(4, 0.6), and the system works when X ≥ 2.
  2. Complement. P(X ≥ 2) = 1 − P(X = 0) − P(X = 1), two terms instead of three.
  3. P(X = 0). All four fail: (0.4)4 = 0.0256.
  4. P(X = 1). C(4,1)(0.6)(0.4)3 = 4 × 0.6 × 0.064 = 0.1536.
  5. Subtract. 1 − 0.0256 − 0.1536 = 0.8208.

Sanity check. The answer must beat the probability that a single component works, 0.6, because redundancy is buying something here, and 0.8208 does. It must also fall short of the two-out-of-two-style certainty of 1. And since 0.6 is below the page's threshold of 2/3, a two-component system needing one working part should do better: 1 − (0.4)2 = 0.84, and it does, by a small margin, exactly as the threshold predicts.

Carry this away

  • A comparison between two systems becomes an inequality between two binomial tails on a shared p.
  • Complement each tail first, then factor the common power rather than expanding it.
  • The answer to a "for what values of p" question is a range, and the boundary is where the systems tie.
  • Redundancy only pays above a reliability threshold, here exactly two thirds.

Your teacher. When an inequality in p has powers of both p and one minus p, look for the common factor before you expand anything. Here it turns a quartic into a linear factor and a bracket, and the whole solution fits in eight short lines. Expanding first is not wrong, only slow, and the slowness is where sign errors come from.
