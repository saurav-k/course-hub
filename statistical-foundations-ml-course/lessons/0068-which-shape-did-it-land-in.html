The one-minute version

  • Two shapes came out of this lecture. With replacement, or independent repeated trials, gives the binomial. Without replacement gives the hypergeometric. It is the same replacement switch that picks the column in Lecture 4's four boxes.
  • The classifier problem is a binomial, the quality check is a hypergeometric, and the mini-batch is also a binomial once you say what counts as a success. The class notes make that re-reading explicitly.
  • The direct route for "both classes represented", left open on Part 2, closes here: the four binomial terms add to 0.912, the same answer the complement gave.
  • The single most transferable idea in the lecture is not a formula. It is that a count is only half of a probability, and the other half is what each counted thing is worth.

3. The mini-batch was a binomial all along

The class notes come back to the first problem after the binomial has been written down, and re-read it. The dataset is 100 examples split 60 to class A and 40 to class B. Now draw 10 examples rather than five, call a class-A draw a success, and the notes write Pr(success) = 6/10, with n = 10 samples beside it. The question on the board is the probability of collecting 4 samples from class A.

Everything the binomial needs is present. The draws are with replacement, so they are independent and each has the same success probability, and the number of draws is fixed in advance.

This course's arithmetic, and one readingThe notes set this up and do not finish it, so the completion is this course's own. Reading "collecting 4 samples from class A" as exactly four, which is the reading the binomial line beside it selects, the answer is C(10, 4)(0.6)4(0.4)6 = 210 × 0.1296 × 0.004096 = 0.1115 to four places. If the intended reading were "at least four" the answer would be a sum of seven such terms instead; nothing on the page settles which was meant, and this page says so rather than choosing silently.

The re-reading matters for a reason beyond this one number. It shows that the same experiment can be described in two different sample spaces, and that the right one depends on the question. Asking about distinct examples needs the space of ordered index lists, all 1005 of them. Asking about class balance needs only the space of class patterns, and once the question is coarsened that far the binomial applies.

4. Closing the direct route from Part 2

Part 2 left a road half built. The direct route to "both classes represented" splits by how many of the five draws are class A, and it stalled because the count of patterns in each case had no tool yet. That tool is now on the table.

  1. Case k = 1. C(5, 1)(0.6)1(0.4)4 = 5 × 0.6 × 0.0256 = 0.0768.
  2. Case k = 2. C(5, 2)(0.6)2(0.4)3 = 10 × 0.36 × 0.064 = 0.2304.
  3. Case k = 3. C(5, 3)(0.6)3(0.4)2 = 10 × 0.216 × 0.16 = 0.3456.
  4. Case k = 4. C(5, 4)(0.6)4(0.4)1 = 5 × 0.1296 × 0.4 = 0.2592.
  5. Add the four. 0.0768 + 0.2304 + 0.3456 + 0.2592 = 0.912.

The complement route on Part 2 gave 1 − 0.07776 − 0.01024 = 0.912. The two routes agree exactly, which is what makes it safe to say the direct road really was the same road and merely the longer one.

6. Check yourself

A shipment of 500 parts holds 30 defective ones. Twelve are pulled and tested, none replaced. Which shape?
A fixed sample pulled without replacement from a population split into two kinds is the hypergeometric, so the probability of finding exactly r defective parts is C(30, r) times C(470, 12 minus r) over C(500, 12). The binomial would need each part to be tested with a fixed independent probability, which is not what pulling from a finite shipment does. The falling product prices all twelve being distinct, which they are by construction here, so that question is not open. And stars and bars counts multisets with repeats allowed, and this draw allows none.
Which fact makes the classification-error problem a binomial rather than a hypergeometric?
Ten independent trials each failing with the same fixed probability is exactly the binomial set-up, and no pool is being depleted anywhere in the problem. The test examples being distinct is true and irrelevant: what matters is that testing one does not change the error rate on the next. The size of 0.2 relative to a half decides nothing about which shape applies, though it does decide whether the configurations weigh equally. And both shapes count how many of something came out rather than where, so that is common to the two rather than distinguishing.
Your teacher. If one thing survives from this lecture, make it the question from Part 6: would I bet the same on any two of these outcomes? Every other habit here follows from it. It tells you whether to divide by the size of the space or to multiply by a shared weight, it tells you whether a coarser description of the experiment is safe to use, and it is the only step in the whole lecture that no notation reminds you to take. Print the formula sheet, then write that sentence at the top of it in your own hand.
