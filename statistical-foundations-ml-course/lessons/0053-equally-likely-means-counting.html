Lecture 4 · Part 8 of 12 · the equally-likely board

Once every outcome weighs the same, probability stops being analysis and becomes arithmetic

9 min the hinge One assumption collapses the whole probability measure into a ratio of two counts. The rest of Lecture 4 is about producing those two counts.

The one-minute version

  • Take a discrete sample space Ω in which every outcome is equally likely. The axioms then force each single outcome to carry probability 1 / |Ω|.
  • Any event A is a set of such outcomes, so its probability is simply how many it contains: P(A) = |A| / |Ω|.
  • That is why the rest of this lecture is counting rules. Under this assumption there is nothing left to compute except two integers.
  • The assumption is doing real work and it fails constantly. The most common way to get a counting problem wrong is to pick a sample space whose outcomes are not equally likely, and the two-dice example in section 4 is the classic instance.

1. Where the formula comes from

Nothing here is assumed. The formula falls out of the axioms that Lecture 2, Part 5 laid down, together with the one extra assumption named in its own line.

  1. The setting. Ω is finite, with |Ω| outcomes in it. The bars mean "how many", so |Ω| is a count, not a probability.
  2. The assumption. Every single outcome carries the same probability. Call it p, the same p for every outcome.
  3. Apply the second axiom. The whole space has probability one, and the single outcomes are disjoint and exhaust Ω, so their probabilities add to one: |Ω| × p = 1.
  4. Solve. p = 1 / |Ω|. Each individual outcome, written P({i}) with the braces because a single outcome has to be wrapped into a set before it is an event, carries one over the size of the space.
  5. Build up any event. An event A is a set of |A| outcomes, all disjoint, each worth 1/|Ω|, so P(A) = |A| × (1/|Ω|) = |A| / |Ω|.
P({i}) = 1 / |Ω|   and   P(A) = |A| / |Ω| Ω, omega, is the sample space: every outcome the experiment can produce. i is one particular outcome and {i} is the event consisting of just that outcome. |A| and |Ω| are cardinalities, meaning plain counts of how many elements each set has. The whole second formula is read: favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes, which is almost certainly the definition of probability you were taught at school, and this is where it comes from and what it costs.

2. What the assumption is doing

The phrase "equally likely" is easy to skip over. It is the load-bearing element of the whole section, and it cannot be checked from inside the mathematics: it is a claim about the experiment.

A fair die, a well-shuffled deck, a uniformly random subset, a hash function that spreads its inputs evenly - these are the situations where somebody has arranged for the assumption to hold. A person picked from a street, a request picked from a log, a file picked from a bucket: these are not, and treating them as if they were is not a small error.

The right-hand branch is not exotic. Lecture 2, Part 6 worked a discrete space where the outcomes carry different weights, and the medical test on Parts 1 to 4 of this very lecture is another: an outcome where the person is ill is a hundred times rarer than one where they are well, so counting outcomes there would have been meaningless.

3. Choosing the sample space is where the mistake lives

The subtle failure is not forgetting the assumption. It is choosing a description of the experiment under which the assumption quietly stops being true, while the description still looks perfectly reasonable.

Roll two fair dice and record the total. What is the probability the total is seven?

The tempting answer. The possible totals are 2, 3, 4, ..., 12. That is eleven outcomes. Seven is one of them. So the probability is 1/11.

Why it is wrong. The eleven totals are not equally likely. A total of two happens only one way, and a total of seven happens six ways. The formula P(A) = |A|/|Ω| was never licensed on this Ω, so applying it produces a number with no meaning.

The repair. Choose a sample space whose outcomes really are equally likely: the ordered pairs (first die, second die). There are 6 × 6 = 36 of them, each equally likely because both dice are fair and neither affects the other. Now count.

TA Session 1, Part 4 calls this the finite uniform model and works two problems inside it, and Homework 1, Graded 1 turns on exactly the same decision: get the sample space right and the arithmetic is short, get it wrong and no amount of care afterwards helps.

4. What this buys the rest of the lecture

Under the uniform assumption, every probability question becomes two counting questions: how many outcomes are there in total, and how many of them are favourable. The remaining pages of Lecture 4 answer the first of those systematically.

Where this formula stops workingEverything on this page is conditional on the uniform assumption, and Lecture 5, Part 6 works the first problem in this course where it fails: a classifier's error patterns, which are not equally likely unless the error rate is exactly one half. 5. Check yourself

The formula P(A) = |A| / |Ω| follows from the axioms plus which extra ingredient?
Equal likelihood is the whole extra assumption: it forces each outcome to 1 over the size of the space, and the rest is addition over disjoint outcomes. Independence is about events, not outcomes, and is not involved. Finiteness matters for the count to exist but is not sufficient on its own - a finite space with unequal weights breaks the formula. And the relative size of A against its complement is irrelevant; the formula works for any A.
Rolling two dice, why does using the eleven possible totals as the sample space fail?
A total of seven arises six ways and a total of twelve arises one way, so the outcomes are not equally likely and the counting formula was never licensed on that space. Divisibility is a symptom, not the reason - a space of eleven equally likely outcomes would be perfectly fine. The two dice are independent, which is exactly what makes the 36-cell space uniform. And the eleven totals are a legitimate sample space; it is simply one that needs weights rather than counts.
Your teacher. Whenever a counting problem will not come out, suspect the sample space before you suspect the arithmetic. Write down what one outcome is, in a full sentence, and then ask whether two different outcomes described that way really are equally likely. In practice that single question resolves most stuck counting problems, and it is the one step the answer key never shows you.
