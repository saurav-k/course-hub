Lecture 4 · Part 12 of 12 · the summary table

Two questions settle every sampling problem, and the lecture ends on their four answers

recap 8 min The table the whole counting section builds up to, all four cases on one basket, and what Lecture 4 leaves you holding.

The one-minute version

  • Two yes-or-no questions - does order matter and does the item go back - pick one of four boxes, and each box holds one formula.
  • Ordered, with replacement: mn. Ordered, without: m! / (m−n)!. Unordered, without: C(m, n). Unordered, with: C(n + m − 1, m − 1).
  • Answering the two questions is the whole difficulty. Once they are answered the arithmetic is mechanical, and once they are answered wrongly no amount of careful arithmetic recovers.
  • This is where Lecture 4 ends. What it leaves you holding is one worked base-rate example, a table-shaped test for independence, and the machinery to count a sample space.

2. All four, on one basket

The lecture uses a different example for each case, which is good teaching but makes the four counts hard to compare. Running one basket through all four boxes fixes that. Take m = 4 items and n = 2 draws throughout. Everything in this section is this course's own arithmetic on the lecture's four formulas.

  1. Case 1, ordered with replacement. mn = 42 = 16. Every ordered pair, repeats included.
  2. Case 2, ordered without replacement. m! / (m−n)! = 4! / 2! = 24 / 2 = 12. The sixteen minus the four repeats.
  3. Case 4, unordered with replacement. C(n + m − 1, m − 1) = C(5, 3) = 5! / (3! × 2!) = 120 / 12 = 10. The six mixed pairs plus the four repeated ones.
  4. Case 3, unordered without replacement. C(m, n) = C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! × 2!) = 24 / 4 = 6. The lecture's own four-candidate answer.

The four land in a strict order, 16 then 12 then 10 then 6, and each step has a reason. Dropping replacement removes the four repeated pairs. Dropping order halves what is left, but only the part of it that has no repeats, which is why case 4 sits at 10 rather than at 8.

3. How to pick the box, in words

Two questions, asked about the experiment rather than about the formula. Answer both aloud before writing anything.

  1. If I swap two of the draws around, is that a different result? Yes means ordered. No means unordered. Distinct job titles, positions in a sequence and password characters are ordered; a committee, a hand of cards and a popcorn order are not.
  2. Can the same item appear twice? Yes means with replacement. No means without. A password may repeat a character; a committee may not repeat a person.
  3. Then read the box. Substitute m and n, and check the condition: cases 2 and 3 need m ≥ n, and cases 1 and 4 do not.
  4. Sanity-check against a tiny case. Set m and n to two and three and count by hand. If the formula disagrees with the hand count, one of the two switches was answered wrongly.

Step 4 is the one people skip and the one that saves the most time. Every formula on this page can be verified by exhaustive listing when the numbers are small enough, and the discipline of doing that once, per problem shape, is worth more than memorising the grid.

5. Check yourself

A five-a-side team is picked from a squad of twelve. Which box is it, and what is the count?
Nobody plays twice, so there is no replacement, and swapping two names picks the same team, so order does not matter: that is case 3, and C(12, 5) = 792. Case 1 would count ordered lists with repeats. Case 2 would count ordered line-ups without repeats and gives 95,040, which overcounts every team by 5! = 120. And case 4 would allow the same player to be selected twice, which a team sheet does not.
Which pair of boxes gives the same count when the basket is huge relative to the draws?
With m far larger than n, drawing the same item twice is very unlikely, so replacement barely changes the count and m to the power n comes close to the falling product: cases 1 and 2 converge, and cases 3 and 4 converge for the same reason. Ordering never stops mattering as m grows; it is a definition of the outcome, not an approximation. Cases 2 and 4 sit in different rows and different columns, so they share no switch. And cases 3 and 4 converge rather than diverge in that limit.
Your teacher. You have now met the two ideas that account for most of the wrong probability in circulation. One is reading a conditional backwards, which is Parts 1 to 4. The other is multiplying probabilities that were never independent, which is Parts 5 to 7. If everything else from this lecture fades and those two stay, the lecture did its job. Print the formula sheet, work Homework 1's counting problem again with the four boxes in front of you, and then close the book: the counting is the part that comes back quickly, and the two habits are the part that does not.
