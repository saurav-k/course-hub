Fifteen marks, four problems, and the solving stays yours

start here 6 min Practice Set 1 is two sheets in one: four graded problems worth 15 marks, due Sunday 6 September, and thirty practice problems nobody collects. This chapter explains every graded problem and solves none of them.

The one-minute version

  • The sheet splits in two. The homework problems, 1 to 4, are submitted and graded: 15 marks, deadline Sunday 6 September. The practice problems, 1 to 30, are not submitted and exist for reps.
  • The graded marks divide as 4 + 5 + 4 + 2 across the four problems, and the split per part is printed on the sheet itself.
  • Each graded problem gets one page here: the question restated in full, every symbol named, the concept it tests with a link to the page that teaches it, the wording traps, and how to start. Then it stops.
  • Nothing on these pages is solved. No final answer, no count, no evaluated probability. The doing is the homework, and these pages refuse to do it for you.

How to plan the sitting

The graded half is one sitting of a couple of hours if the ideas land, and twice that if you fight the notation instead of the problems. Three pieces of advice, in the order they help.

Do the graded four in sheet order, but bank the warm-up first. Part 2(a) is a one-mark proof whose template the course has already published, so it is the fastest mark on the sheet and doing it first buys momentum. After that, work 1, then 2(b), then 3, and leave 4 for when you are fresh, because it rewards a fresh eye more than any other question here.

Write justification as you go, not at the end. The sheet's own instruction is to justify your answers and present all calculations and arguments clearly, and every part's mark figure assumes it. A bare number on this sheet is an incomplete answer even when the number is right. Say what the sample space is, name the theorem you are using, and show the line where you used it.

Leave the practice thirty for afterwards. They are not submitted, they carry no marks, and they are the reason the graded four will feel slow the first time and quick the second. The pages for this chapter cover the graded four only; the practice problems are a gym you visit on your own schedule, grouped by whatever concept the graded set just made you want to drill.

The one confusion that costs marksThe sheet's two halves look alike: numbered questions, some with parts, some with mark figures printed. They are not alike. Homework problems 1 to 4 are the only things on the sheet that get submitted and graded. The thirty practice problems are never submitted. Before you start writing anything, check the question number is from the first half. Every page in this chapter concerns itself with the graded four and says so at the top.

What each page gives you, and what it refuses to give you

Every graded problem gets one page, and every page runs the same six-part shape:

  1. The question restated in plain English, in full, with nothing dropped, so you never have to hold the sheet's compressed wording in your head while working.
  2. Every symbol named in words on the page where it appears: the matrix, the measure, the events, the conditioning bar.
  3. What the problem is really testing, with a link to the exact course page that teaches that concept, so reopening it costs one click.
  4. The traps: the places the wording can be misread, stated explicitly, because each of these problems was built around at least one.
  5. How to start: what to define, what the sample space is, what to condition on. Then the page stops.
  6. Marks and difficulty, honestly, so you can budget the sitting against reality rather than against the mark count alone.

The stopping rule is the point of the whole chapter, so it is worth stating as a rule rather than a tone. A page here stops before the first line of arithmetic. It will name what to define and which theorem applies; it will not evaluate an expression, complete a count, or hand over a probability. If you catch a page doing your work for you, it has failed, and so has the chapter. The homework only does what homework does when the answer comes out of your own pen.

Check yourself

Which of the sheet's two sections gets submitted and graded?
The sheet's own note says solutions to the homework problems are submitted and graded, with fifteen marks and a Sunday 6 September deadline. The practice problems carry an explicit note that solutions are not submitted, so the second option describes the half that costs nothing. Submitting everything would mean typing up thirty extra solutions for zero marks, and submitting nothing would mean forfeiting all fifteen.
A page in this chapter has just finished explaining how to start a problem. What does it do next?
Stopping before the arithmetic is the chapter's contract with you, stated on this page and kept on every page under it. Working the calculation or listing the answer would destroy the only thing the homework is for, which is why neither appears anywhere here. Naming the theorem happens earlier, in the concept section, not after the start.
Your teacher. Read the four graded questions on the printed sheet once, cold, before opening any page of this chapter. The pages will tell you what each question is really asking, and that lesson lands hardest when you have first felt the original wording resist you. Note where you stall; the stall points are the traps, named one page at a time.
