Bayes rule is the total-probability picture, read backwards

working 12 min Prior belief, then data, then updated belief. One formula moves the belief, and it is nothing but Parts 4 to 6 reassembled.

The one-minute version

  • You hold a prior over which slice of a partition the world is in: P(Bi), belief before the data.
  • Data arrives: event A is observed. Each slice explains the data with its likelihood P(A | Bi).
  • Bayes rule reweights the priors by the likelihoods and normalises, producing the posterior P(Bi | A): belief after the data.
  • Worked on the two coins: seeing heads moves the posterior from 1/2 and 1/2 to 1/3 and 2/3.

1. The process, named in words first

The notes frame Bayes rule as a three-step story before writing any algebra, and the framing is the part to keep. You hold a prior belief about which hypothesis is true: for the notes' setup, the hypothesis is which slice Bi of a partition the world is in, and the prior is P(Bi), its probability before anything happens. Then data arrives: some event A is observed. Then belief is updated: the new belief about the same hypotheses is the conditional P(Bi | A), now called the posterior.

Two more names, so you can read any book's version of the formula. The likelihood of the data under hypothesis Bi is P(A | Bi): how well that slice explains what you saw. The evidence is P(A) itself, the total probability of the data across all slices, which is exactly the denominator the law of total probability computed on the previous page.

2. The formula, assembled from three pages

No new probability is involved. Start from the definition of conditional probability, Part 4. Expand the numerator with the multiplication rule, Part 5. Expand the denominator with the law of total probability, Part 6. The notes box the result:

P(Bi | A) = P(Bi ∩ A) / P(A) = P(Bi) × P(A | Bi) / ∑j P(Bj) × P(A | Bj) B1, B2, ... are the slices of a partition, and A is the observed event. The first form is the conditional definition. In the second, the numerator is prior times likelihood for slice i, and the denominator runs the same product over every slice j and adds, which is P(A) by total probability. The denominator needs P(A) > 0: data that cannot happen cannot update anything.

Read the fraction as a contest. Each slice's prior is multiplied by how well it explains the data. The denominator changes nobody's relative standing; it only rescales so the updated beliefs sum to 1, which Part 4 promised conditional probabilities would do. So the posterior is the prior reweighted by explanation power, then normalised.

3. The coin posterior, worked to the notes' values

Back to the two coins, one fair and one that always lands heads, with A1 and A2 the events "coin 1 was chosen" and "coin 2 was chosen". The data is the complement of Part 4's event: Ec, the toss lands heads. The notes compute the update both ways, directly and through the formula, and both are worth seeing.

  1. Price the evidence. P(Ec) = 1 − P(E) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, using Part 1's complement rule on Part 4's answer. This is the denominator, and it can also be read off the outcome table directly: the heads outcomes carry 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4.
  2. Posterior for coin 1, directly. P(A1 | Ec) = P(A1 ∩ Ec) / P(Ec). The overlap is the single outcome "fair coin, heads", priced 1/4. So the posterior is (1/4) / (3/4) = 1/3.
  3. Posterior for coin 2, directly. The overlap is "trick coin, heads", priced 1/2, so P(A2 | Ec) = (1/2) / (3/4) = 2/3. The two posteriors sum to 1, as Part 4 promised.
  4. The same answers through the formula. The likelihoods: P(Ec | A1) = 1/2, the fair coin explains heads half the time, and P(Ec | A2) = 1, the trick coin explains it always. Bayes gives P(A2 | Ec) = (1/2 × 1) / (1/2 × 1/2 + 1/2 × 1) = (1/2) / (3/4) = 2/3. Same number, different route, which is the point: the formula is the direct computation, packaged.
The pattern to carry forwardEvery element of this page is already load-bearing in machine learning. Spam filters hold priors over message types and update on observed words. A/B analysis holds priors over which variant is better and updates on conversions. The names change, prior, likelihood, posterior, but the object is always the fraction on this page, and the denominator is always total probability doing its quiet work.
Where the notes are looseThe notes compute the posteriors directly from the conditional definition and box the general formula beside the worked example, without running the example through the formula; step 4 above is this course's filling-in, and it reproduces the notes' 2/3 exactly. The word "evidence" for the denominator P(A) is standard in the literature but the notes do not use it; they say "data" or "event". This page uses both names so the formula reads the same way whichever book you open next.

Check yourself

In Bayes rule, what does the denominator, the sum over j of P(Bj) P(A | Bj), actually represent?
The denominator is P(A) computed by the law of total probability: every slice's way of producing the data, added up. It is the evidence, and its job is only to normalise. The prior of the winning hypothesis is a single numerator term, not the whole sum. The likelihood under the best slice is likewise one term in the sum, not the sum. And a posterior before the data is a contradiction in words; nothing about the posterior exists until the denominator has been divided through.
After observing one heads toss, the posterior P(coin 2 was chosen) moves to which value?
Heads has likelihood 1 under the trick coin and 1/2 under the fair one, so the odds reweight from even to one-to-two in favour of coin 2, giving a posterior of 2/3. Heads was not equally likely under the two coins, that asymmetry is the entire engine of the update. One third is the posterior for coin 1, the correct number attached to the wrong hypothesis. And 3/4 is the evidence P(Ec), the normalising denominator, which is not about either coin on its own.
Your teacher. Run the same experiment again in your head: keep the posterior 1/3 and 2/3 as the new prior, observe a second heads, and update once more. The likelihoods are unchanged, so the new posterior is (1/3 × 1/2) and (2/3 × 1) normalised, which is 1/5 and 4/5. One more heads and it is 1/9 and 8/9. Belief compounds, and the loop in the process figure is why a stream of weak evidence eventually becomes a strong conclusion. Keep the pattern to hand the next time a detector on a serving path fires: P(bad prompt | detector fired) is a posterior, and with a rare bad population the base rate owns the denominator, which is why a 99 percent accurate filter can still return mostly false positives.
