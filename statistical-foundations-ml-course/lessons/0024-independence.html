Independence is a numerical coincidence, not a picture

12 min working One product equalling another number decides independence. No Venn diagram can, which is exactly why the two get confused.

This page runs ahead of the deckThe faculty notes for this session stop at Bayes rule. Independence belongs to a later session of the lecture course, but the tutorial for this week tests it heavily, and it would ask you to work problems whose concept you had not yet met. So this page teaches it now and says plainly: from here to the end of the lecture you are slightly ahead of the deck. The definitions and proofs here are the standard ones, and they follow Ross, section 2.5, and Hajek, section 2.10, both in the reading list.

The one-minute version

  • Events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B): the overlap is exactly what the two singles multiply to.
  • This is a statement about numbers. Two pairs of circles with identical geometry can differ in independence, so no Venn diagram can show it.
  • Disjoint is not independent: disjoint events with positive probability never pass the product test.
  • Pairwise independence does not add up to mutual independence, and independence survives taking complements. The tutorial tests both facts.

1. The definition, and the reading you already own

A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B) A and B are two events. P(A ∩ B) is the probability that both happen. The definition demands that the overlap's probability equal the product of the two single probabilities. It is an equation between numbers, and it either holds or fails; there is no picture of it.

Divide both sides by P(B), allowed whenever P(B) > 0, and the definition becomes P(A | B) = P(A). Read with Part 4's picture: learning that B happened shrinks the world to B, and A's share of the shrunken world equals its share of the original one. The information changed nothing, which is precisely what the word independent should mean. Part 4 ended by noticing that its ten-point picture had P(A | B) = 1/2 and P(A) = 1/2. That picture was, by these numbers, an independent pair, and the coincidence was planted on purpose.

3. Disjoint is not independent, and the difference is information

The classic exam trap, named plainly. Disjoint means the events cannot both happen: A ∩ B = ∅. Independence means neither event tells you anything about the other. Far from being the same, they are opposites in the information sense. Take two disjoint events with positive probability: P(A ∩ B) = 0, but P(A) × P(B) > 0, so the product test fails. They are maximally dependent: learning A happened tells you B certainly did not, which is the strongest information one event can carry about another.

This lecture already contains the example. The coin choices A1 and A2 from Parts 4 to 7 are disjoint, and P(A1 ∩ A2) = 0 while P(A1) × P(A2) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Not independent, because knowing which coin you picked settles the other hypothesis completely. Meanwhile the ten-point pair in Part 4 was independent despite overlapping. Overlap says nothing either way; the product is the only test.

4. Pairwise independence is weaker than mutual independence

For three events there are two different claims that both get called "independent" in casual speech, and the tutorial tests the difference. Pairwise independence says every pair passes the product test: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), P(A ∩ C) = P(A)P(C), P(B ∩ C) = P(B)P(C). Mutual independence says every intersection factors, including the triple: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A)P(B)P(C), and likewise for every sub-collection. Mutual implies pairwise. Pairwise does not imply mutual, and one constructed example shows it.

Constructed for this page, following the standard textbook example: toss two fair coins once each. A is "the first toss lands heads", B is "the second toss lands heads", and C is "the two tosses match", which happens on HH and TT. Each of the three has probability 1/2. Now check the pairs:

Why does the triple fail here? Because C is built from A and B: given the first two results, "the tosses match" is already decided. The pairwise tests never look at that three-way entanglement, which is exactly why passing them proves less than intuition expects. When a problem says "independent events" without qualification, it means mutual independence.

5. Independence survives taking complements

One more fact the tutorial asks for, and it is a two-line proof from Part 1. Suppose A and B are independent; then A is also independent of Bc, the event that B does not happen:

  1. Split A by B. The events A ∩ B and A ∩ Bc are disjoint, and together they are A: every outcome of A either also has B or does not. So P(A) = P(A ∩ B) + P(A ∩ Bc), which rearranges to P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B).
  2. Use the definition. A and B are independent, so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B), giving P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A) P(B).
  3. Factor and recognise. P(A) − P(A) P(B) = P(A) (1 − P(B)) = P(A) P(Bc), the last step by Part 1's complement rule. That is the product test for A and Bc, passed.

Running the same argument once more gives the fourth combination: if A is independent of B and of Bc, then Ac is independent of B, and of Bc as well. So independence is a property of the information between two events, not of the particular yes-or-no wording: flipping either event to its complement preserves it. The intuition matches the algebra. If heads tells you nothing about rain, then not-heads tells you nothing about rain either.

Check yourself

A and B are independent. You learn that B happened. What is P(A | B)?
Dividing the definition P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B) through by P(B) gives exactly P(A | B) = P(A): the conditional equals the unconditional, which is the information-free reading of independence. The product P(A) P(B) is the overlap's probability, not a conditional one. Excluding is disjointness, the opposite arrangement, and it fails the product test whenever both probabilities are positive. And B being certain in the shrunken world is true of every conditioning event; it says nothing about A's share of that world.
Events can be pairwise independent while failing mutual independence. What does that mean?
Pairwise independence is the three product tests on the pairs; mutual independence additionally demands the triple factorise, and the two-coin example shows a case where all pairs pass at 1/4 while the triple sits at 1/4 against a product of 1/8. The second option is the trap the example exists to kill: passing the pairs proves nothing about the triple. The third describes events that are dependent in pairs yet mutually independent, which cannot happen since mutual implies pairwise. The fourth conflates independence with disjointness, which this page exists to separate.
Your teacher. You have reached the end of Lecture 3, and the habit to carry out of it is the product test. Whenever a problem says "independent", write P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B) before doing anything else, and whenever it says "disjoint", write P(A ∩ B) = 0. Those two lines are different, they are rarely both true, and half the lost marks on tutorial problems of this kind come from writing one when the problem meant the other.
