Variance is the second moment about the mean, and one identity makes it computable

working 12 min The mean says where a distribution sits. Nothing so far says how wide it is. This page defines the width, then proves the identity that turns the definition into arithmetic you can actually do.

The one-minute version

  • The k-th moment of X is E[Xk]. The mean is the first moment, and nothing more exotic is going on.
  • Variance is Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])2]: the average squared distance from the mean. Squaring is what stops the distances from cancelling, and it is why the answer is never negative.
  • Its square root is the standard deviation, which is back in the units of X.
  • The working identity is Var(X) = E[X2] − E2(X): mean of the squares minus square of the mean. Both pieces are moments, so you never have to centre the variable first.
  • Spent three times: Ber(p) gives p(1 − p), Unif(a,b) gives (b − a)2/12, and the Gaussian gives σ2 after one integration by parts.

1. The picture: two distributions with the same mean

A mean is a single point, and two very different quantities can share one. A component that always fails at exactly 5000 hours and a component that fails uniformly somewhere between 0 and 10000 hours have the same mean life. You would not sign the same contract for both.

Variance is the number that separates them. It asks: on average, how far is a draw from its own mean? Distances above and below the mean cancel if you average them raw, which is exactly why the definition squares first and asks the question afterwards.

2. Moments, and which one variance uses

the k-th moment of X is E[Xk], for k a positive integer X is the random variable. k is a whole number 1, 2, 3 and so on. E[·] is the expectation from Parts 1 to 4: for a discrete variable it is the sum of each value times its probability, and for a continuous one the integral of the value times its density. So E[X1] is the ordinary mean, and E[X2] is the average of the squared values, which is a different and usually larger thing.
Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])2], and sd(X) = √Var(X) X − E[X] is the deviation: how far this particular draw landed from the mean. Squaring it makes the deviation positive whichever side it fell, and weights a far miss more heavily than a near one. E[·] of that squared deviation is its average over all draws, which is the variance. sd(X), the standard deviation, is its square root, which undoes the squaring and returns to the units of X: if X is in hours, the variance is in hours squared and the standard deviation is in hours.

Two consequences fall out immediately. Variance is never negative, since it is the average of squares and a square cannot be negative. And variance is zero exactly when X never moves off its mean, because the only way to average zero over non-negative quantities is for every one of them to be zero.

3. The identity, proved

The definition is honest but awkward: to use it directly you must know the mean first, then re-run the whole average over centred values. The identity below removes the second pass. The source proves it for a discrete X, and so does this page, with nothing compressed.

  1. Name the mean. Write μ = E[X], a fixed number, not a random one. That fact is the engine of the whole proof.
  2. Write the definition as a sum. Var(X) = ∑x pX(x) (x − μ)2, where pX(x) is the probability that X takes the value x, and the sum runs over every value X can take.
  3. Expand the square. (x − μ)2 = x2 + μ2 − 2μx. So Var(X) = ∑x pX(x)(x2 + μ2 − 2μx).
  4. Split the sum into three. A sum of three terms is the sum of three sums: ∑x pX(x) x2 + μ2 ∑x pX(x) − 2μ ∑x pX(x) x. Because μ is a constant it comes outside its sums; a random quantity could not.
  5. Read each sum. The first is the second moment E[X2] by definition. The second is ∑x pX(x) = 1, since a pmf sums to one over its support. The third is ∑x pX(x) x = E[X] = μ, the definition of the mean.
  6. Substitute. The three pieces become E[X2] + μ2 · 1 − 2μ · μ = E[X2] + μ2 − 2μ2.
  7. Collect. μ2 − 2μ2 = −μ2, so Var(X) = E[X2] − μ2 = E[X2] − E2(X). Mean of the squares, minus square of the mean.

The same argument runs for a continuous X with an integral in place of the sum and a density in place of the pmf; every step above uses only that the total mass is one and that μ is constant. Both canon texts carry the identity in their chapters on expectation and variance: Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, Introduction to Probability and Hajek, Probability with Engineering Applications.

4. The Bernoulli: an inverted parabola

Let X ~ Ber(p), taking the value 1 with probability p and 0 with probability 1 − p.

  1. First moment. E[X] = 1 · p + 0 · (1 − p) = p.
  2. Second moment. Squaring changes nothing, because 12 = 1 and 02 = 0. So E[X2] = p as well, which is the small surprise of this example.
  3. Apply the identity. Var(X) = p − p2 = p(1 − p).

Read that as a function of p and it is a downward parabola through zero at both ends. A coin that always lands heads, or never does, carries no uncertainty at all, and the widest uncertainty is exactly at the fair coin.

This course's arithmeticThe source stops at the closed form. Evaluating it: a fair coin, p = 0.5, gives variance 0.25 and standard deviation 0.5. A one-in-ten event, p = 0.1, gives variance 0.09 and standard deviation 0.3. So a rare event is not merely rarer, it is also less variable per trial, which is why rare-event estimates need more trials rather than fewer.

5. The uniform: same mean, different width

Let X ~ Unif(a,b), with density 1/(b − a) on the interval and zero outside it.

  1. First moment. From Lecture 8, Part 1, E[X] = (a + b)/2, the midpoint.
  2. Second moment. E[X2] = ∫ab x2/(b − a) dx = [x3/3]ab / (b − a) = (b3 − a3)/(3(b − a)).
  3. Cancel the factor. b3 − a3 = (b − a)(b2 + ab + a2), so the (b − a) divides out and E[X2] = (b2 + ab + a2)/3.
  4. Apply the identity. Var(X) = (b2 + ab + a2)/3 − (a + b)2/4.
  5. Put over 12. 4(b2 + ab + a2)/12 − 3(a2 + 2ab + b2)/12 = (b2 − 2ab + a2)/12.
  6. Recognise the square. That numerator is (b − a)2, so Var(X) = (b − a)2/12. Only the width enters; the position does not.
This course's arithmeticReading the formula on the two intervals drawn above: the wide one has variance 102/12 = 8.333 and standard deviation 2.887; the narrow one has 22/12 = 0.333 and standard deviation 0.577. The variance ratio is 25, exactly the square of the width ratio, because width enters squared.

6. The Gaussian variance, worked here

The source writes X ~ N(μ, σ2) → Var(X) = σ2 and marks it Prove! The derivation below is this course's, not the lecture's, and it uses the mean E[X] = μ that Lecture 8, Part 2 established.

  1. Centre the variable. Put Y = X − μ. Since E[X] = μ, the definition reads Var(X) = E[Y2] directly, with no need for the identity here.
  2. Write the integral. Substituting y = x − μ in the Gaussian density gives Var(X) = (1/√(2πσ2)) ∫−∞∞ y2 e−y2/2σ2 dy. The substitution shifts the whole line, so the limits do not move.
  3. Split the integrand for parts. Write y2 e−y2/2σ2 as y · (y e−y2/2σ2). Take u = y and dv = y e−y2/2σ2 dy. That second factor was chosen because it is exactly what an exponential of −y2 differentiates into, so it can be integrated in closed form.
  4. Find v. Differentiating e−y2/2σ2 gives (−y/σ2) e−y2/2σ2, so v = −σ2 e−y2/2σ2, and du = dy.
  5. Apply integration by parts. ∫ u dv = [uv] − ∫ v du = [−σ2 y e−y2/2σ2]−∞∞ + σ2 ∫−∞∞ e−y2/2σ2 dy.
  6. Kill the boundary term. As y → ±∞ the exponential falls faster than y grows, so y e−y2/2σ2 → 0 at both ends and the bracket contributes nothing. This is the step the whole derivation turns on.
  7. Name the surviving integral. ∫−∞∞ e−y2/2σ2 dy = √(2πσ2). That is not new: it is precisely the statement that the Gaussian density integrates to one, rearranged.
  8. Assemble. Var(X) = (1/√(2πσ2)) · σ2 · √(2πσ2) = σ2. The normalising constant cancels against the integral it created, and the parameter named σ2 turns out to deserve the name.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the proof of the identity, why can μ be pulled outside the sums?
A constant factor comes out of a sum; a quantity that varied with x could not. The first option is true of the raw deviations and is the motivation for squaring, but it is not what licenses the factoring. The second is used later, at step 5, to turn one sum into 1, and is a different step. The fourth explains why the definition squares at all and plays no part in the algebra of pulling μ out.
Two uniform variables share a mean, but one interval is three times wider. How do their variances compare?
Variance is (b − a)2/12, so tripling the width multiplies the variance by three squared, which is nine. The second option is the answer for the standard deviation, which scales linearly, not for the variance. The third confuses position with spread, which is exactly what this example is built to separate. The fourth is wrong because the formula depends on the endpoints only through their difference.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A fair die shows 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6.

Find its variance and standard deviation using the identity, then say how the answer would change if every face were relabelled by adding 10.

Hint

You need two moments, not one. The sum of the first six squares is 91. For the relabelling, do not recompute anything: ask what the definition measures.

Show the worked solution
  1. First moment. E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5.
  2. Second moment. E[X2] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6, which is about 15.167.
  3. Apply the identity. Var(X) = 91/6 − 3.52 = 15.1667 − 12.25 = 35/12, about 2.917.
  4. Take the root. sd(X) = √2.9167, about 1.708, in the units of the face value.
  5. Relabel. Adding 10 to every face adds 10 to the mean, so every deviation x − μ is unchanged. The variance is therefore still 35/12: shifting a distribution does not stretch it.

Sanity check. The die's values span 5 units, so a standard deviation of 1.708 sits comfortably inside that span, as it must. Compare a uniform variable on the continuous interval from 1 to 6: (6 − 1)2/12 = 25/12, about 2.083. The die is slightly more spread out, because its mass sits at the six endpoints rather than filling the interval, and that is the right direction.

Carry this away

  • The k-th moment is E[Xk]; variance is the average squared distance from the mean, and its root is the standard deviation.
  • The identity Var(X) = E[X2] − E2(X) is proved by expanding the square and reading the three sums; only the constancy of μ is needed.
  • Bernoulli gives p(1 − p), largest at the fair coin; uniform gives (b − a)2/12, which sees width and not position.
  • The Gaussian's σ2 is worked here by parts, because the source marks it "Prove!" and leaves it.

They must agree to the last decimal, and if they do not, the arithmetic slip is almost always a forgotten square on the mean.
