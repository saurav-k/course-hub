Lecture 2 · Part 6 of 6 · Class notes, the user-session board

One small space, carried end to end with real numbers

working 10 min The deck's user-session example: four outcomes, four point masses, one axiom check, and two events weighed - the whole machinery of Lecture 2 turning once, visibly.

The one-minute version

  • The experiment: one user session, recording device (mobile or desktop) and whether they purchased.
  • The space: Ω = {MP, MN, DP, DN}, four outcomes, with point masses 0.18, 0.42, 0.12 and 0.28.
  • The check: the four masses sum to 1, which is the total-mass axiom, verified before anything else is computed.
  • The payoffs: P(mobile) = 0.6 and P(purchase) = 0.3, each a sum of point masses over a subset.

1. The space, and its four weights

One user arrives. Two things about the session are recorded: the device they joined from, and whether they made a purchase. Each is binary, so by the product rule the sample space has four outcomes, which the deck abbreviates MP, MN, DP and DN. A probability measure is defined by giving each single-outcome event its weight, called a point mass, and the deck's four are:

OutcomeMeaningPoint mass
MPjoined from mobile, made a purchase0.18
MNjoined from mobile, no purchase0.42
DPjoined from desktop, made a purchase0.12
DNjoined from desktop, no purchase0.28

2. The axiom check, before any other question

A set of four numbers is a legal probability measure only if it passes the axioms of Part 5. All four are non-negative and at most one, so the range clause holds. The total-mass axiom is the one that could actually fail, so it gets checked, in the open, first:

  1. Add the four point masses. Running total: 0.18, then 0.60, then 0.72, then 1.00.
  2. Read the sum against axiom 2. The four outcomes are disjoint single-outcome events whose union is Ω, so countable additivity says P(Ω) is their sum, and it is 1, exactly what the axiom demands.
  3. Only now is P legal, and any event built from these outcomes can be weighed.

This is the step worth copying into your own work. A table of masses that sums to 0.97 or to 1.03 is not a slightly wrong model; it is not a probability measure at all, and every number computed from it inherits the defect.

3. Weighing two events, exactly as the deck does

The user joined from mobile. That English sentence names the subset {MP, MN}: whichever purchase outcome happened, the device was mobile. The two outcomes are disjoint, so axiom 3 adds their masses:

  1. Name the subset. "Mobile" = {MP, MN}, two of the four cells of the grid.
  2. Add the point masses. P({MP, MN}) = P({MP}) + P({MN}) = 0.18 + 0.42 = 0.6.

The user made a purchase. That names {MP, DP}: whichever device, a purchase happened. Again the two outcomes are disjoint, so:

  1. Name the subset. "Purchase" = {MP, DP}, one row of the grid.
  2. Add the point masses. P({MP, DP}) = P({MP}) + P({DP}) = 0.18 + 0.12 = 0.3.

4. Where the two events intersect

The deck closes the board by intersecting the two events, and the answer is a single cell. A = {MP, MN} is mobile and B = {MP, DP} is purchase; the only outcome in both is MP, so A ∩ B = {MP}, weighing 0.18. Notice what the set algebra guarantees: A ∩ B ⊆ A and A ∩ B ⊆ B, so the weight of the intersection, 0.18, cannot exceed either 0.6 or 0.3. It does not, and it could not.

Where this goes nextTwo questions this space makes natural are deliberately left unasked: given the user came from mobile, what is the chance they purchase? And what is P(A ∪ B) when the discs overlap? Both belong to Lecture 3, conditional probability, which starts from exactly the picture above.

Check yourself

A colleague proposes masses 0.2, 0.4, 0.2 and 0.3 for MP, MN, DP and DN. What is wrong?
The four proposed masses add to 1.1, so P of the whole space would exceed 1 and axiom 2 fails; no legal measure has these values. The masses are free to be unequal, as the deck's own 0.12 against 0.42 shows. And masses between zero and one are exactly what the range clause allows.
Why must the total-mass check come before computing P(mobile) and P(purchase)?
Until the masses sum to one, the function P is not a probability measure, so its outputs would be numbers without meaning; the check is the licence, not a ritual. Difficulty has nothing to do with ordering. The masses are fixed from the start and do not drift. And the check sums all four masses, which is not either event's weight.
On this space, which outcome sits in the intersection of "mobile" and "purchase"?
The intersection needs both properties at once, and only MP records both a mobile join and a purchase; it weighs 0.18. MN fails the purchase test, DP fails the mobile test, and DN fails both, sitting outside the union entirely.
Your teacher. Run the same three moves on any funnel you own: define the outcomes, check the masses sum to one, then read off the event weights by adding cells. If your analytics tool reports a "probability of purchase" that was computed from masses never checked against one, you are reading a number from an illegal measure, and Lecture 3's conditional probability will inherit that error loudly.
