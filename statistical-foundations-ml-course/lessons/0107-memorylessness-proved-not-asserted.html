The exponential forgets how long you have waited, and the four lines that show it

working 12 min Lecture 6 wrote "prove it" beside this identity and moved on. This session worked it live, in four lines, and the notes carry them.

After this page

  • You can prove the exponential is memoryless from its tail, without skipping the step where the intersection collapses.
  • You can say what a memoryless model asserts about ageing, and name a quantity it therefore fits badly.
  • You can tell the property apart from the uniqueness claim, and say which one this course has proved.

Before you start: The exponential and the loan decision, for the tail identity this whole proof runs on.

The one-minute version

  • Memorylessness is the identity P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s), for s and t both non-negative.
  • In words: knowing you have already waited t tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait.
  • The proof needs one input, the tail P(X > x) = e−λx, and one observation: the intersection of "beyond s + t" and "beyond t" is just "beyond s + t".
  • After that it is division: e−λ(s+t) / e−λt = e−λs, which is the tail at s.
  • What it costs: a memoryless model has no ageing in it at all, which suits a service time and misrepresents mechanical wear.

1. What the identity claims

The lecturer had already met this shape once, for the geometric distribution in discrete time. Here it returns in continuous time, and he stated it before proving it: the fact that X has already exceeded t does not change your belief about how much further X will go.

P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s), for s, t ≥ 0 X is the random quantity, here a waiting time or a lifetime. t is how much has already elapsed, and s is how much more you are asking about. The vertical bar reads "given that", so the left side is the probability of surviving past s + t in a world where surviving past t is already known. The right side is the same question asked of a brand new item. The two being equal is the whole property.

2. The proof, line by line

The lecturer said it was easy enough to show on the spot and did so. Page 6 of the notes carries the same four lines. Nothing here is compressed.

  1. Start from the CDF. For an exponential, FX(x) = P(X ≤ x) = 1 − e−λx for x ≥ 0.
  2. Turn it into a tail. Every outcome is either at most x or beyond it, so P(X > x) = 1 − FX(x) = e−λx. This is the only input the rest of the proof needs.
  3. Write the conditional probability by its definition. P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s + t ∩ X > t) / P(X > t), where the intersection sign means both events happen together.
  4. Collapse the intersection. If X is beyond s + t it is automatically beyond t, since s is not negative. So "beyond s + t and beyond t" is the same event as "beyond s + t": the larger threshold is the binding one and the smaller adds no restriction. The numerator becomes P(X > s + t).
  5. Substitute the tail twice. The numerator is e−λ(s+t) and the denominator is e−λt.
  6. Divide. Dividing powers of the same base subtracts the exponents: e−λ(s+t) / e−λt = e−λs − λt + λt = e−λs.
  7. Name what is left. By step 2, e−λs is exactly P(X > s). So P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s), which is the identity. The t has cancelled, and that cancellation is the whole content of the theorem.

Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis run the same argument as Example 3.10 of Introduction to Probability, on a reader waiting for a bus, and Hajek gives it in Section 3.4 of Probability with Engineering Applications. Both reach the cancellation by the same route.

3. What it means: a model with no ageing

Read the result on a component. It has run for t hours without failing. The identity says its chance of running another s hours is the chance a brand new one had of running s hours. The hours already served bought nothing and cost nothing. The item is neither worn nor proven, and in Hajek's phrasing it is as good as new.

4. What the assumption costs

Memorylessness is why the exponential is the default model for a queue. A till serving a customer has no natural notion of wearing out mid-transaction, so "however long this has taken, the remaining time looks the same" is close to what actually happens, and the lecturer named that as the reason the shape is so popular in queueing.

The same property is a poor fit wherever ageing is real. Hajek shows in Section 3.9 that memorylessness is equivalent to a constant failure rate: the instantaneous chance of failing in the next moment, given survival so far, is λ and never changes. A bearing, a battery or a mechanical seal does not behave that way.

Its failure rate climbs with use, so an exponential model understates late-life failures and overstates early ones. No choice of λ repairs that, because λ sets the level of the failure rate and not its shape.

5. Two different claims, and only one is proved

Lecture 6 said "prove it" and this proves the easier halfLecture 6, Part 12 carried the lecturer's pre-class claim that the exponential is the only continuous memoryless random variable, wrote "prove it" in brackets beside the identity, and moved on. Lecture 7 delivers a proof, and it is a proof of the property and not of the uniqueness: it starts from the exponential and shows the identity holds. The uniqueness claim runs the other way and would have to show that no other continuous law can satisfy it, which needs an argument about all distributions rather than one calculation about this one. Nothing in this course supplies that argument, and the canon states it without proof too: Hajek's own summary sheet asserts that any non-negative random variable with the continuous-time memoryless property is exponentially distributed, and leaves it there. Treat the uniqueness as a signpost you have been given, not as a result you have seen.

The discrete twin is worth holding beside it. Lecture 6, Part 9 carried the geometric's memorylessness, P(L > k + n | L > n) = P(L > k), proved the same way from the tail (1 − p)k. Two shapes, one in discrete time and one in continuous time, each singled out by the same property, and both uniqueness claims still standing on assertion.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the proof, why does the event "X > s + t and X > t" reduce to just "X > s + t"?
Since s is not negative, s + t is at least t, so anything beyond s + t is beyond t too and the second condition adds nothing. The first option is false and would break the proof: the two events overlap completely, which is the opposite of independent. The third names a condition that is used but does not by itself say why one event swallows the other. The fourth is a true fact about continuous variables that plays no part in this step.
Which claim has this course actually proved about memorylessness in continuous time?
The four lines start from the exponential tail and land on the identity, so the property is what was proved. The first option is the uniqueness claim, which this course asserts and never establishes. The second reverses the truth: memorylessness forces a constant failure rate, not a rising one. The third is invented, and the exponential itself has a perfectly good mean of one over lambda.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A support queue's handling time is modelled as Exp(0.25) in minutes. A ticket has already been open for 8 minutes.

Find the chance it stays open at least 4 minutes longer, then repeat for a ticket open 30 minutes, and say what the comparison shows.

Hint

Write the question as a conditional tail and apply the identity before you reach for a calculator. You need e to the minus one, about 0.368.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the first question. It asks for P(X > 12 given X > 8), with s = 4 and t = 8.
  2. Apply the identity. The condition drops out, leaving P(X > 4) = e to the minus 0.25 times 4.
  3. Evaluate. The exponent is 1, so the answer is e to the minus 1 = 0.368.
  4. Write the second question. P(X > 34 given X > 30), with s = 4 and t = 30.
  5. Apply the identity again. t drops out exactly as before, so the answer is again 0.368.
  6. Say what it shows. The 30-minute ticket is no nearer resolution than the 8-minute one. If your own support data disagrees, the data is telling you the handling time is not exponential.

Sanity check. Both answers must be equal, since the identity says the elapsed time cancels, and both must sit between 0 and 1. A mean handling time of 1/0.25 = 4 minutes also makes 0.368 the right order: surviving one further mean is always e to the minus one for this shape.

Carry this away

  • The proof is one input and one observation: the tail is e to the minus lambda x, and the larger threshold swallows the smaller.
  • The elapsed time t cancels in the division, and that cancellation is the theorem.
  • A memoryless model contains no ageing, which fits a service time and misrepresents a part that wears out.
  • The property is proved here; the claim that no other continuous law has it is still only asserted.
