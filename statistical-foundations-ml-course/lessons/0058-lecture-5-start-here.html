Lecture 5: the road from counting to the binomial

start here 8 min Saturday 29 August 2026. Three machine-learning problems worked end to end, the one place the counting formula stops working, and the shape all three problems turn out to share. Ten sittings.

The one-minute version

  • Lecture 4 built the four sampling boxes. Lecture 5 shows which box a real problem lands in, and why, on three problems that all come out of machine-learning practice.
  • A mini-batch is drawn with replacement, so it is case 1 and the answers are products. A dataset quality check inspects distinct images, so it is case 3 and the answers are ratios of binomial coefficients.
  • A classifier evaluated on ten test examples is neither: it is a run of independent trials, and counting the runs with exactly three errors is what produces the binomial, C(n, k) pk (1−p)n−k. That is where this lecture is heading from the first line.
  • The sharpest idea in the session gets its own page: you cannot write P(3 errors) = |A| / 210. Lecture 4's |A| / |Ω| needs every outcome to weigh the same, and error patterns do not, except at p = 0.5.
  • Three places these pages are honest about a gap rather than smoothing it over, and two threads the lecturer left open for the next session. All five are named below before you meet them.

What is new here, and what is not

Lecture 4 ended by handing you a grid: two yes-or-no questions about an experiment, and four counting formulas underneath them. It is a good grid and none of it is re-taught here. What it does not tell you is which square any particular problem belongs in, and that decision is the one that actually goes wrong in practice.

So Lecture 5 is an applied session. Three problems, each stated in machine-learning language, each carried from the first sentence to a final expression. Equally likely means counting, the basic counting rule, ordered sampling and unordered sampling are all assumed and all linked from the pages that use them.

What is genuinely new is this: a working procedure for choosing the box, the complement argument used three separate times on three different events, the exact boundary at which the equally-likely formula stops being legal, and the binomial distribution built from counting rather than borrowed. Lecture 1, Part 3 already used a binomial to price a run of extreme days, on trust. This is the session that pays that debt.

How this lecture is arranged

The class worked the three problems in order, and these pages keep that order. The third problem is much the longest, because it is the one that grows a general result out of a particular answer: four of the ten pages sit under it.

The ten parts

  1. The mini-batch that can repeat. A batch of five drawn with replacement from a hundred examples, and the falling product that prices all five coming out different.
  2. Both classes in the batch. The complement argument in its cleanest form: two disjoint bad cases, each a single power, subtracted from one.
  3. The quality check that finds nothing. Twenty distinct images from a thousand, fifty of them mislabelled, and the ratio of two binomial coefficients that prices a clean result.
  4. Exactly r mislabelled. Split the choice in two, one factor per pool, and the general formula falls out. The shape has a name the course has not formally met yet.
  5. The binomial shape. Ten test examples, an error rate of 0.2, and the answer that is a count multiplied by a product. Then the general form, named on the board.
  6. When counting outcomes stops working. The sharpest idea in the lecture. Dividing by the size of the sample space is illegal here, and the reason is visible in two lines of Cs and Es.
  7. Count the configurations, then multiply. The repair, checked by hand on four images and two errors, small enough to list every case.
  8. The gaps argument. Given three errors, how likely is it that two of them are neighbours? Place the correct results first and count the gaps between them.
  9. Conditioning on one image. A question asked in the room and left at the set-up: how likely are three errors, given that the second image is one of them?
  10. Which shape did it land in. The three problems side by side, the two new shapes named, and what Lecture 5 leaves you holding.

The honesty notes, named in advance

This course quotes what the lecture states and marks anything it works out itself. Three places in Lecture 5 need that distinction spelled out on the page, and two more places are threads the lecturer said he would return to.

Almost nothing in this lecture is evaluated to a numberThe handout and the class notes both stop at an expression. 1 − (0.6)5 − (0.4)5 is written and never multiplied out; so is the ratio of binomial coefficients in the quality check, and so is the classifier's C(10, 3) (0.8)7 (0.2)3. That is normal for a lecture, where the expression is the teaching and the arithmetic is bookkeeping. Every page below shows the expression exactly as the source leaves it, and where a decimal is useful it is worked in a separate callout marked as this course's own arithmetic. Nothing derived carries a key-number mark, because that mark means the lecture said it. The policy is recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md.
Two places the handwriting cannot be read with confidenceThe class notes are handwritten and two marks on them will not resolve. One is a stray binomial coefficient beside the quality-check line, whose role is genuinely unclear; Part 3 names both readings and builds on neither. The other is the first symbol of the last pattern in the four-image list, which is overwritten; Part 7 says so and derives the complete list from the count instead of guessing at the ink.
Two threads the lecturer left open for the next sessionBoth were raised in the room, attempted, and deliberately parked. The first is a pictorial view of the complement for the at-least-one question in the quality check, which Part 3 carries as open. The second is the direct route for "both classes represented", adding up the mixed batches instead of subtracting the pure ones, which was started and not finished; Part 2 shows why it does not close with the tools available at that point in the lecture, and Part 10 closes it once the binomial has arrived.

Your teacher. Carry one question through all ten pages: what is a single outcome here, and do two of them weigh the same? That one question sorts every problem in this lecture. A mini-batch outcome is an ordered list of five examples and they do all weigh the same. A quality-check outcome is a set of twenty images and they also all weigh the same. A classifier outcome is a string of ten Cs and Es and they emphatically do not, which is why the third problem needs a different tool and gets one. Ask the question first, every time, and the choice of formula stops being a guess.
