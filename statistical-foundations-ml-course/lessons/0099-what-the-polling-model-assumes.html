Four assumptions hold this model up, and the room challenged every one

working 10 min The live session's four objections, each conceded by the lecturer, with what each one costs.

After this page

  • You can list what a binomial model of a poll assumes, and say which of those assumptions an election actually satisfies.
  • You can tell an assumption that is merely simplifying from one that stops the calculation being usable at all.

Before you start: how many people must you poll, for the three numbers this page puts conditions on.

The one-minute version

  • Four assumptions sit under every number in Parts 1 and 2, and all four were raised by students in the room. The lecturer conceded each.
  • Independent answers. Assumed outright. Real sampling is without replacement, and people who live together do not decide independently.
  • A known p. Nobody knows p. That is the assumption that stops the calculation being usable as it stands, and the lecture defers it to estimation and confidence intervals.
  • Exactly two candidates. With more, the count is no longer binomial; the lecturer names the multinomial and moves on.
  • A rule for ties. Whether "no call" counts as an error is a choice, not a fact, and at n = 20 it moves the answer from 0.1275 to 0.2447.

1. The four, and what the lecturer said to each

2. Independence, assumed and conceded

A student asked directly whether independence holds here. The lecturer's answer was that the model assumes each selected individual decides independently of the others. That is the assumption Hajek's definition of the binomial, section 2.4.4 on page 38, requires: n independent Bernoulli trials, and without it the sum of indicators is still a count but is no longer binomial.

Two things break it in a real poll. Sampling is done without replacement, so once a supporter of A has been drawn there is one fewer left, and the next draw is slightly less likely to be one. The exact distribution then is the hypergeometric, worked in Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, chapter 1, problem 51. And people are not drawn from a well-shuffled bag: households, workplaces and regions correlate opinions, so two answers are related even when the sampling is clean.

When the simplification is safeThe without-replacement effect shrinks as the population grows relative to the sample. Polling 1,000 people out of 500,000 removes one five-hundredth of one per cent of the pool, so the binomial is an excellent approximation to the hypergeometric. The practice problem below does it the other way round, on a population of ten, where the two answers differ by a fifth.

3. The unknown p, which is the real problem

A student pressed the point hardest here: if the calculation uses 0.6, does the polling company not already know the answer? The lecturer agreed. Nobody knows the population split before the election, and the whole point of running a poll is that it is unknown. He went further and named the consequence himself: if you were sizing a sample from this formula, you could not actually evaluate it.

His answer was that a polling company estimates p from the sample, and then reports a confidence interval around that estimate. Hajek, section 2.9 on pages 51 and 52, develops exactly that, with an opinion poll as the worked example: the sample proportion is an estimator, the interval around it is random rather than the parameter, and the confidence level says how often such an interval covers the true value.

The assumption that stops the calculation being usableParts 1 and 2 read as arithmetic with a definite answer. They are not, because p is not known and cannot be. The lecturer conceded this in the session: asked how a polling company could ever evaluate the formula, he answered "you have a fair point", then "I don't know, yeah, whether we'll get to such things", and finally that the company would estimate p from a sample and form confidence intervals, which the course would reach at some point. So treat every figure in Part 2 as the error probability given a p, and never as the error probability of a real poll. The sweep above is this course's own arithmetic, added because the lecture states the sensitivity in words and never draws it.

4. Two candidates, and what a tie means

Two students raised the same structural objection: real elections have more than two candidates. The lecturer agreed and named the generalisation. With M categories instead of two, the counts follow the multinomial distribution, which Wolfram MathWorld defines as the extension of the binomial to n mutually exclusive outcomes. He also noted that the winner rule matters: predicting the largest of M counts is a different question from predicting whether one candidate clears 50%. The lecture names the multinomial and does not develop it, and neither does this page.

The tie was raised three separate times. With n even the sample can split exactly, and a student put it plainly: a tie is not the right result either. What follows is not a mathematical fact but a definition the modeller chooses. At n = 20, if a tie counts as a failure the error event is X ≤ 10; if the company is allowed to say "no call" and that is not held against it, the error event is X ≤ 9.

The lecturer's own resolution was to sidestep the question by keeping n odd, which is why Part 2 works at 21 and 101 rather than 20 and 100. That is a legitimate move, and it is worth seeing it for what it is: a choice of sample size made to avoid a definitional problem, not a consequence of the mathematics.

5. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which assumption stops Part 2's numbers being usable by a real polling company?
The formula needs a value of p, and p is what the poll exists to discover, so the calculation cannot be evaluated before the fact. Independence is an approximation that is good when the population is much larger than the sample, so it degrades the answer rather than blocking it. More candidates changes which distribution applies, which is a different model rather than an unusable one. And the tie rule only bites for even n, which the lecture avoids by sampling an odd number.
Why does sampling without replacement break the binomial model?
Removing a supporter from the pool lowers the chance that the next draw is one, so the draws are dependent and the exact law is hypergeometric rather than binomial. Without replacement is precisely what stops a person being drawn twice, so the first option describes sampling with replacement. The count stays a whole number under either scheme. And the population's true split does not move at all; what moves is what remains available to draw.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A village has exactly 10 voters, 6 of whom support A. A pollster samples 5 of them without replacement, and predicts the sample's majority.

Find the exact probability that the poll names the wrong winner, and compare it with the binomial answer of 0.3174 from Part 1.

Hint

Count subsets rather than multiplying probabilities. There are 10 choose 5 equally likely samples; count how many contain 2 or fewer supporters of A.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count all samples. 10C5 = 252 equally likely subsets of five voters.
  2. Count the samples with 0 supporters of A. That needs all five drawn from the 4 supporters of B, and there are only four of them, so this is impossible: 0 samples.
  3. Count the samples with exactly 1. Choose 1 of the 6 supporters of A and all 4 supporters of B: 6C1 × 4C4 = 6 × 1 = 6 samples.
  4. Count the samples with exactly 2. Choose 2 of the 6 and 3 of the 4: 6C2 × 4C3 = 15 × 4 = 60 samples.
  5. Divide. (0 + 6 + 60) ÷ 252 = 66 ÷ 252 = 0.2619.
  6. Compare. The binomial gives 0.3174 and the exact answer is 0.2619, so treating these five draws as independent overstates the error by about a fifth.

Sanity check. The without-replacement answer should be the smaller of the two, because each supporter of A already drawn makes the next draw less likely to be one and each supporter of B drawn makes it more likely, which pulls the sample towards the population's own 60:40 split and away from the extremes that produce an error. If your answer came out above 0.3174, a count of subsets was taken from the wrong group.

Carry this away

  • A model is a set of conditions, and the four here are independent answers, a known p, two candidates, and a stated rule for ties.
  • Independence is an approximation that improves as the population dwarfs the sample; the unknown p is not an approximation at all, and it is what estimation and confidence intervals exist to repair.
  • What counts as an error is the modeller's definition, and at n = 20 that one choice is worth as much as doubling the sample.

Your teacher. Notice what the room did here, because it is the skill this page is really about. Nobody in the session disputed an arithmetic step. Every objection was about the correspondence between the model and the world: is that independent, do you know that number, are there only two of those, what happens on a draw. Those four questions are portable, and they are the ones to ask of any model you are handed, including your own. Write them on the same page as the answer, because a probability with no conditions attached is a number pretending to be a fact.
