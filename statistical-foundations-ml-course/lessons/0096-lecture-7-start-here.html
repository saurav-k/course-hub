Lecture 7 turns four distributions into decisions you can defend

foundation 9 min Six pages of class notes and the full transcript, Saturday 5 September 2026. The lecture that stops naming distributions and starts using them: how many people to poll, whether a recommendation list is working, and which shape fits a quantity you have never modelled. Thirteen sittings.

The one-minute version

  • Lecture 6 named the shapes. This lecture spends them, and every question it asks turns out to be a CDF evaluated at one point.
  • A poll is a binomial in disguise, so the chance it calls the wrong winner is a binomial CDF. Poll 5 people and you are wrong about a third of the time; poll 101 and about one time in forty-eight.
  • A recommendation list is a PMF with no famous name at all, and it is still a complete model. Two product questions read straight off its CDF at two different points.
  • Then the crossing. A random variable is continuous when its CDF is an integral, and that one clause forces the rest: no jumps, a density that is not a probability, every exact value weighing zero.
  • The uniform, the exponential and the Gaussian arrive with the applications attached - a bus timetable, a loan decision, and the noise term in linear regression - and memorylessness gets a proof this time rather than an assertion.

What is new here, and what is not

This lecture covers ground Lecture 6 has already been over, and it is cheaper to say so here than to let a reader discover it on part six. Lecture 6 came from pre-class notes that defined the density, drew the uniform and the exponential, and reached the Gaussian. This is the same material delivered live, and the figure below names the four things that adds.

How this lecture is arranged

The session runs in a straight line and these pages keep that order, drawn below.

The thirteen parts

  1. A poll is a binomial in disguise. An election, two candidates, and a company that samples a handful of voters. Each person polled is one Bernoulli trial, so the count is binomial and the chance of calling it wrong is a CDF value.
  2. How many people must you poll. The same calculation at three sample sizes, worked in full, and the page that recomputes the quoted figures and finds where they came from.
  3. What the polling model assumes. Four assumptions the room challenged, one after another: independence, a known p that nobody knows, exactly two candidates, and what a tie counts as.
  4. A recommendation list is a PMF. Five results and a "show more" link, six possible click positions, and a distribution that belongs to no named family whatsoever.
  5. Two CDF questions a product team asks. Relevance in the top three, and users left with nothing. Both read off the same CDF, at two different points, and they are not complements of each other.
  6. When the staircase becomes a curve. A random variable is continuous when some non-negative function integrates to its CDF. That one clause is why the jumps disappear.
  7. A density is not a probability. The height can exceed 1 and routinely does. Only the area under it is a probability, and the total area is exactly 1.
  8. Every single point has probability zero. The shrinking-band argument, and the consequence that makes life easier rather than harder: strict and non-strict inequalities now agree.
  9. The uniform, and the bus you just missed. A flat density, a straight-line CDF, and two places it turns up: a waiting time between scheduled buses, and the initial weights of a neural network.
  10. The exponential, and the loan decision. Small values likelier than large ones, a tail that is one exponential term, and the Lecture 1 loan question answered with the distribution it always assumed.
  11. Memorylessness, proved not asserted. Lecture 6 wrote "prove it" in the margin. This session works it on the board in four lines, and the property turns out to be a modelling cost as much as a convenience.
  12. The Gaussian as a noise model. The bell, the CDF that cannot be written down, and why the shape is everywhere: it is what noise is assumed to look like.
  13. Choosing a distribution for a quantity. Check the support, then check the shape. The lecturer's own example rules the exponential out for profit and loss before any curve is drawn.

The honesty notes, named in advance

Five things need saying first: two arithmetic, two gaps left open, and one objection the lecturer conceded.

Two of the three quoted error probabilities belong to the even sample sizeThe board says the poll is wrong about .25 of the time at n = 21 and about .03 at n = 101. Recomputed, the binomial gives 0.1744 and 0.0209; the quoted figures belong to n = 20 and n = 100, where they are 0.2447 and 0.0271. The third figure is sound: ≈ .3 at n = 5 recomputes to 0.3174. The recording explains the drift. The lecture opened at n = 20, a student pointed out that ten against ten is a tie, and 21 was adopted on the spot with 101 following the same repair - while the prepared numbers were still the ones for 20 and 100. A second student then computed 0.17 and challenged the 0.25; the lecturer answered that he had probably computed it for 20, was not sure of the figure, and was sure of the formula - right on all three counts. Part 2 works the recomputation.
One identity was written wrong on the board and corrected liveAsking what fraction of users find nothing useful, the lecture first wrote P(X > 4) = 1 − FX(3). A student caught it and it became 1 − FX(4), which is what the notes carry. Part 5 names the slip rather than printing the right version quietly, because that off-by-one is the mistake a reader is about to make alone.
Three results are asserted, one of them only sketchedThat a continuous CDF is differentiable almost everywhere, and that the Gaussian CDF has no closed form, are stated without proof. That every exact value carries probability zero is argued from a shrinking band. All three are taught as stated and each page says so.
Memorylessness is proved in one direction onlyLecture 6 asserted the exponential is the only memoryless continuous distribution and wrote "prove it" in the margin. This session proves the exponential is memoryless, which is the easy direction. Uniqueness is still unproved anywhere in the course; it is proved in Sheldon Ross, A First Course in Probability, section 5.5, which is where Part 11 now sends a reader who wants it, and that page keeps the two claims apart.
The Gaussian is fitted to a quantity it does not fitThe lecture offers heights as a Gaussian, and a student objected that a height is never negative while a Gaussian puts probability across the whole real line. The lecturer agreed, said practitioners use it anyway when the negative tail carries almost nothing, and offered the repair: model the deviation from the sample mean, which genuinely takes both signs. Part 12 carries both.

What the lecture deliberately does not say

mu is the centre of the Gaussian, sigma its effective width, and lambda the rate that sets how fast the exponential decays. Asked what they really mean, the lecturer said mu is the expected value, sigma squared the variance and the exponential's mean 1 over lambda, then said the course has not reached those ideas and moved on. These pages do the same: a definition borrowed early is one the reader cannot yet use.

What to carry out of it

How to read the pages. Every page leads with a picture, names the mechanism, then works the numbers with no step skipped. A missing step is a defect in the page, not in you. The glossary defines every term in a sentence, and the Lecture 7 formula sheet holds every expression on one printable page.
Your teacher. Carry one question through all thirteen pages: which single point is this question asking about? Nearly every quantity here is a CDF evaluated somewhere, and the difficulty is not the algebra but deciding where. The poll's error is the CDF at half the sample size, the recommendation system's quality is the CDF at position three, and the loan decision is one minus the CDF at 2000 rupees. Getting fluent at turning an English sentence into one evaluation point makes the rest of this course much easier.

Where this came from

These pages rest on two sources and no others: six pages of class notes taken during Lecture 7 of Statistical Foundations of Machine Learning, IIT Bombay, Saturday 5 September 2026, and the full transcript of the same session. Every page cites both. Every student objection is here because the transcript records it; the notes record none of the discussion. Two copies of the notes exist, plain and watermarked, and they are the same six pages.

The canon is Sheldon Ross, A First Course in Probability, tenth edition, and the sections read for these pages are 4.6 and 4.6.2, the binomial and how to compute its distribution function; 4.10, properties of the CDF; 5.1, the definition of a continuous random variable; 5.3, the uniform; 5.4, normal random variables; and 5.5, exponential random variables, which proves the uniqueness this lecture leaves open. Bruce Hajek, Probability with Engineering Applications, is the freely readable companion. Both are in RESOURCES.md.
