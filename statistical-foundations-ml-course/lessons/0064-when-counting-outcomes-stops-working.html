Lecture 5 · Part 6 of 10 · the sample-space sketch

Dividing by the size of the sample space is illegal here, and the illegality costs a factor of two

11 min frontier The one place where a tool from the previous lecture stops working, drawn rather than asserted.

The one-minute version

  • There are 210 = 1024 patterns of ten C's and E's, and C(10, 3) = 120 of them hold exactly three errors. It is very tempting to answer 120 / 1024.
  • That is wrong, and the class said so explicitly. The correct answer from Part 5 is about 0.2013; the tempting one is about 0.1172, low by nearly a factor of two.
  • Lecture 4, Part 8 licensed P(A) = |A| / |Ω| only when every outcome weighs the same. Error patterns do not: the class notes price a three-error pattern and a one-error pattern side by side and they differ by a factor of sixteen.
  • What replaces it is the definition itself: P(A) is the sum of the probabilities of the outcomes inside A. The board draws this with four outcomes worth 1/2, 1/8, 1/8 and 1/4, crosses out the count-and-divide answer, and writes the sum instead.
  • The patterns are equally likely in exactly one case: p = 0.5. That is why the fair-coin instance on Part 5 comes out at 120/1024 and this problem does not.

1. The tempting wrong answer

Everything needed for it is already on the table. An outcome of the evaluation is a string of ten letters, each C or E. Each position has two possibilities and the positions are free, so the basic counting rule gives 210 = 1024 strings in all. Part 5 counted the strings holding exactly three errors: C(10, 3) = 120 of them.

Favourable over total. 120 over 1024. It has the right shape, both numbers are correct counts, and it is the wrong answer.

2. Why it is wrong: the patterns do not weigh the same

The class notes settle this by writing two patterns one above the other and pricing each. Both are legitimate outcomes of the same evaluation. They are not worth the same.

The comparison generalises. Each error swapped for a correct result multiplies a pattern's weight by 0.8 / 0.2 = 4, so a pattern with no errors at all is 43 = 64 times as likely as one with three. The 1024 patterns come in eleven weight classes, one per error count, and the heaviest is over a million times the lightest.

3. What the board draws instead

The class notes make the point in miniature, on a sample space with four outcomes rather than 1024, small enough to see all at once. The four outcomes carry probabilities 1/2, 1/8, 1/8 and 1/4, which add to one as they must. An event A holds two of them.

The board writes P(A) = 2/4, marks it wrong, and writes P(A) = 1/2 + 1/8 instead.

P(A) = ∑ω ∈ A P({ω}) ω, omega in lower case, is one single outcome, and {ω} is the event that holds only that outcome. The sum runs over every outcome inside A. This is not a new rule: it is the third axiom applied to a finite collection of disjoint single outcomes. When all the terms in the sum happen to be equal, say each worth w, the sum collapses to |A| × w, and when in addition w = 1 / |Ω| it collapses further to |A| / |Ω|. Lecture 4's formula is the end of that chain, not the start of it.

4. The two answers side by side

It is worth seeing what the illegal division actually costs on this problem, because the error is not a rounding error.

This course's arithmeticBoth decimals in that figure are this course's own. 120 / 1024 = 0.1172, and 120 × 0.0016777216 = 0.2013. The lecture states that the count-and-divide route is wrong; it does not evaluate the gap. Neither figure carries a key-number mark.

5. The one case where the count is right

Set the error probability to 0.5 and everything changes. Every C is worth 0.5 and every E is worth 0.5, so every pattern, whatever its error count, is worth (0.5)10 = 1/1024. All 1024 outcomes weigh the same and Lecture 4's formula becomes legal again.

That is exactly the fair-coin instance the class notes write immediately after the general binomial: C(10, 3)(1/2)3(1/2)7 = C(10, 3) · 1/210, which is 120/1024. The tempting wrong answer to this problem is the right answer to a different problem, and the only difference between the two problems is the value of p.

The rule, stated once. Configurations are equally likely when, and only when, every configuration is built from the same multiset of factors. For a run of independent trials that means p = 1 − p, which means p = 0.5 and nothing else. Away from that single value, counting configurations tells you how many there are, and nothing whatever about how likely any of them is.

6. A question from the room: does the same trap sit in Lecture 4's fourth box?

Someone raised exactly the right objection during the session, aimed backwards at the previous lecture. Case 4 counts multisets, sampling with replacement and without ordering, and the count is C(n + m − 1, m − 1). Does having that count license dividing over it?

No. The objection is correct, and the counterexample is already on the site, in the two-song table on that very page.

  1. The set-up. Two songs, S1 and S2, drawn twice with replacement, with the order thrown away. The case 4 count is C(2 + 2 − 1, 2 − 1) = C(3, 1) = 3 multisets, and that count is right.
  2. The three multisets. {S1, S1}, {S1, S2} and {S2, S2}.
  3. Their actual probabilities. The underlying draws are uniform and independent, so the four ordered outcomes each carry 1/4. Grouping them gives {S1, S1} at 1/4, {S1, S2} at 2/4 because two ordered sequences produce it, and {S2, S2} at 1/4.
  4. The verdict. The three multisets carry 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4, not 1/3 each. Counting them is correct; dividing over them is not.

This is the same failure as the error patterns, arriving through a different door. A multiset with a repeat in it corresponds to fewer ordered sequences than one without, which is precisely the unequal grouping that Lecture 4, Part 11 uses to explain why case 4 cannot be reached by dividing case 1 by n!. The unequal group sizes that break the division are the same unequal group sizes that break the probability.

What Lecture 4 actually claimsRead back, Lecture 4 is careful about this and claims nothing wrong. Part 8 states the equal-weight assumption as load-bearing and unverifiable from inside the mathematics, and Part 10, Part 11 and Part 12 present the four boxes as counts and never as probability tables. What was missing was a page saying out loud that three of the four boxes commonly sit under uniform experiments while the fourth usually does not. This is that page.

7. Check yourself

Why is 120 over 1024 the wrong value for the probability of exactly three errors?
Dividing a count by a count is licensed only when every outcome weighs the same, and here a one-error pattern is sixteen times as likely as a three-error one, so the division has no meaning. The 1024 patterns are all distinct and disjoint, so nothing is double counted; that is exactly why they can be added up. The count 120 is right, and 720 would be the count if the order of the three errors were recorded, which a pattern does not do. And independence is assumed by the problem and is what lets the ten factors multiply in the first place.
Under what change would 120 over 1024 become the correct answer?
At p = 0.5 a C and an E are worth the same, so every one of the 1024 patterns costs one over 1024 and the counting formula is legal again; that is the fair-coin instance the class notes write down. Replacement is not part of this problem at all, since the ten trials are independent rather than drawn from a pool. Counting errors rather than locating them is what X already does, and it changes the description without changing any weight. And a smaller error probability makes the weights more unequal, not less, so it moves further from the case where the count works.
Your teacher. This is the page to come back to. The habit it installs is a single question asked before any division: if I list two different outcomes of this experiment, would I bet the same money on each? For ten coin flips, yes. For ten classifier verdicts at eighty percent accuracy, obviously not, and you knew that before you learned any probability. The formula from Lecture 4 is a shortcut with a licence attached, and the licence is exactly that bet. Nothing about the notation warns you when it has lapsed, so the warning has to come from you.
