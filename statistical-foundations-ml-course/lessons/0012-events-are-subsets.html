An event is a subset of the sample space, and the subsets are collected

foundation 9 min The middle letter of the triple: what turns "something that could happen" into a set you can weigh, and why the deck gathers events into a collection instead of leaving them loose.

The one-minute version

  • An event is a subset of the sample space: any bag of outcomes the Part 1 language can describe.
  • Saying "the event occurred" means exactly "the outcome ω the experiment produced landed in that subset".
  • The deck's example: three tosses give eight outcomes, "first toss tail" picks four of them, "no head" picks one.
  • The events are gathered into a collection called F, because the deck wants complements and unions of events to still be events.

1. The definition, and what "occurred" means

E ⊆ Ω is an event, and E occurred if ω ∈ E Read as: an event E is a subset of the sample space Ω, and "E occurred" means the outcome ω that the experiment produced is a member of E. Nothing deeper is happening: "the event happened" and "the outcome is in the subset" are the same sentence.

2. The deck's worked example: three tosses of a coin

Toss a coin three times and record the side each time. By the Part 1 product rule the sample space is {H, T}3, the eight ordered triples from HHH to TTT. The deck then reads two English questions as subsets.

  1. "No head at all." Every toss must come up tails, so only one triple qualifies: E = {(T, T, T)}. A one-element subset.
  2. "The first toss is a tail." The first position is fixed at T and the other two are free, so E = {(T,H,H), (T,H,T), (T,T,H), (T,T,T)}. A four-element subset, half the space.

3. Why the events are gathered into a collection

The deck's next move is easy to skim past, and it is the whole point of the middle letter. Having defined events as subsets, it does not leave them as loose individuals: it gathers them into a collection named F and demands that F have properties. The deck's own list of wanted properties is three lines long:

  1. If A is an event, so is Ac. If you can ask "did A happen", you must be able to ask "did A not happen".
  2. If A and B are events, so is A ∪ B. "At least one of these two" must be a legal question.
  3. Ω itself is an event. "Something happened" is the most boring question there is, and it must still be legal.

On the countably infinite space from Part 2, the deck's own events show why the collection matters. "I need at least five tosses" is E1 = {5, 6, 7, ...} and "the count is odd" is E2 = {1, 3, 5, 7, ...}. Neither is a short list. Both are unions of infinitely many one-outcome events, and the collection has to admit them or those questions have no answers.

Why not just take every subset?For a finite space, the deck does exactly that: F is the power set 2Ω, every subset welcome, and nothing is lost. The trouble arrives with uncountable spaces like the speed example, R+. There, demanding a probability for every subset runs into genuine contradictions, and the fix is to admit only a well-chosen collection of subsets and leave the pathological ones out. The deck does not dwell on this, and this course follows it: the details belong to measure theory, which is out of scope here. The honest summary is the one Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis give in their Section 1.2 footnote: for uncountable spaces, certain unusual subsets cannot meaningfully carry a probability, and the issue can be safely ignored in practice.

Check yourself

Two fair-ish dice are rolled and their sum is recorded. Which is the event "the sum is even"?
An event is the bag of outcomes that satisfy the question, so it is the subset of pairs with an even sum. The pair (3, 3) is one outcome of that event, not the event. The whole space is the event "some sum came up", which is always true. Different values is the event "the dice disagree", which says nothing about parity.
The deck wants the collection of events closed under complement and union. What goes wrong on an infinite space if closure is skipped?
Without closure, E₂ = {1, 3, 5, ...} is a union of infinitely many single-outcome events that the collection need not contain, so the question cannot even be asked, let alone weighed. The zero-to-one range is a property of the measure in Part 5, not of closure. The space was written down in Part 2 and closure does not touch it. And the meaning of union never changes; what changes is whether the union is guaranteed to be a member.
Your teacher. The move to watch for is "a question is a set". Take any alert you own, say "latency above 300 milliseconds", and notice that it is a subset of the latency sample space, that its complement is "latency at or below 300", and that two alerts or-ed together are a union. If those three sentences feel obvious, Part 4 will feel inevitable, because that is exactly the closure the sigma-field formalises.
