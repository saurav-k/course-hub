Lecture 1 · Part 7 of 8 · Slides 16 to 18

Two candidate predictors, and one number that tells them apart

13 min a table worked cell by cell One behaviour genuinely tracks spend and one does not. Pearson's correlation coefficient separates them without anyone having to guess.

The one-minute version

  • The business question is which user behaviour is worth watching as an early warning of future spend.
  • Two candidates: in-app session time (X), which sounds plausible, and screen brightness (Z), which sounds absurd. The target is daily spend (Y).
  • Pearson's correlation coefficient centres each column, multiplies the centred values pairwise, adds the results, and divides by the sizes so the answer always lands between −1 and +1.
  • The deck reports rXY ≈ 0.71 and rZY ≈ −0.03. Session time is a leading indicator. Screen brightness is noise.

1. The question, and why it needs a number

Slide 16 poses a question every product team has argued about: which user behaviour is actually informative about future spend? The trouble is that the argument is usually settled by whoever is most confident, and confidence is not evidence. Session time sounds like it should matter. Screen brightness sounds like it should not. But "sounds like" is exactly the instinct that a leading-indicator hunt is supposed to replace, so the lecture deliberately includes one candidate nobody believes in, as a control.

The tool is Pearson's correlation coefficient, written r:

r = ∑(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / √( ∑(xᵢ − x̄)² ∑(yᵢ − ȳ)² ) xᵢ − x̄ is one user's session time minus the average session time: how far above or below the crowd that user sits. Statisticians call this centring. The top multiplies the two centred values together and adds those products over all users. It measures co-movement. The bottom divides that out by how spread each column is on its own, which is what forces the answer into the range −1 to +1 and makes two different pairs of columns comparable.

2. Centring the columns, worked in full

Slide 17 does the arithmetic for five users. Before reading its table, work out the three averages, because every later column depends on them.

  1. Average session time. 15 + 21 + 25 + 31 + 38 = 130, and 130 ÷ 5 = 26 minutes.
  2. Average daily spend. 600 + 800 + 1,100 + 1,250 + 2,000 = 5,750, and 5,750 ÷ 5 = Rs 1,150.
  3. Average screen brightness. 50 + 85 + 10 + 45 + 60 = 250, and 250 ÷ 5 = 50.
  4. Check the centring. Once you subtract the average from a column, the new column must add to zero. For session time: −11 − 5 − 1 + 5 + 12 = 0. That is a free correctness check on every centred column, and it is worth doing every time.

Read the two product columns as stories rather than as arithmetic, because the contrast is the whole lesson.

In the Prod(XY) column every single entry is positive. User A is below average on both session time and spend, so two negatives multiply to a positive. User E is above average on both, so two positives multiply to a positive. Nobody disagrees, so nothing cancels, and the sum piles up to 18,550.

In the Prod(ZY) column the entries fight each other. User B is well above average on brightness but below average on spend, contributing −12,250. User E is above average on both, contributing +8,500. User A's brightness is exactly average, so their deviation is zero and they contribute nothing at all, whatever they spent. The positives and negatives very nearly cancel, and what limps out the other end is −2,250, a small number relative to the sizes involved.

3. Normalising the sums, worked in full

The two sums, 18,550 and −2,250, cannot be compared directly, because they are in different units and built from columns of different spread. Step four of the recipe fixes that by dividing each sum by the sizes of the columns that produced it. Slide 18 does exactly that and reports two results.

rXY = 18,550 / √(300 × 1,125,000) ≈ 0.71 Slide 18 as printed. The interpretation the slide gives: a strong positive association, so higher engagement goes with higher spend.
rZY = −2,250 / √(3,250 × 1,125,000) ≈ −0.03 Slide 18 as printed. The interpretation the slide gives: no meaningful relationship, consistent with noise.

The conclusion slide 18 draws is unambiguous and correct: session time is a useful leading indicator, and screen brightness is not. Anything above about 0.7 in size is a strong association; anything within a few hundredths of zero is indistinguishable from randomness.

The constants on slide 18 do not reproduce from slide 17

The two cross-product sums on slide 17, 18,550 and −2,250, both reproduce exactly if you redo the table. The normalising constants on slide 18 do not. Adding up the squares of the centred columns gives 316 for session time and 1,160,000 for spend, where slide 18 divides by 300 and 1,125,000.

Two consequences, and they are worth separating. First, the fraction as printed does not evaluate to the answer as printed: 18,550 ÷ √(300 × 1,125,000) works out to about 1.01, which is outside the range a correlation coefficient can occupy at all, while using the table's own sums gives 18,550 ÷ √(316 × 1,160,000) ≈ 0.97. Second, and more importantly, none of this disturbs the conclusion. On every version of the arithmetic session time is strongly and positively associated with spend, and screen brightness is not: the brightness figure comes out at −0.037 as printed, −0.038 from the table, and −0.03 as stated, and all three say the same thing.

This page quotes the deck's stated results because those are the numbers on the slide you are studying. It also shows what the table gives, because a reader who does the multiplication deserves to know why their answer differs. The full reasoning is recorded in learning-records/0001.

4. Reading a correlation coefficient

The reason step four is worth the trouble is that it puts every possible answer on one fixed scale, so a correlation between minutes and rupees is directly comparable with a correlation between a brightness setting and rupees.

Two things r never tells youFirst, r measures straight-line association only. A perfect but curved relationship, such as spend rising and then falling with session length, can produce an r near zero even though the two variables are tightly linked. Second, r says nothing about cause. Session time and spend might move together because engaged users buy more, or because users who intend to buy browse longer first, or because both rise on paydays. Correlation ranks candidates for you; it never explains them. Anscombe's four datasets, in Anscombe (1973), share identical summary statistics and look nothing alike when plotted, which is the classic demonstration of why the scatter plot above is not optional decoration.

5. Check yourself

User B has a brightness 35 above the average but spends 350 below the average. What does user B contribute to the Prod(ZY) column?
One deviation is positive and the other negative, so the product 35 × (−350) = −12,250 is negative, and it is the largest single entry in that column by size, so it is anything but small. The second option gets the sign backwards, which would need both deviations on the same side of average. The third describes user A, whose brightness sits exactly on the average and whose deviation of zero therefore wipes out their contribution no matter what they spent.
Session time and spend correlate at about 0.71. Which of these does that number not establish?
Correlation is a statement about co-movement in the data you happen to hold, and it is silent about mechanism: engaged users might buy more, or intending buyers might browse longer first, or both might simply rise on paydays. The other three all follow directly from the arithmetic on this page, since a positive r is exactly a positive cross-product sum after rescaling, and comparing the sizes of the two coefficients is precisely what the shared scale was built for.
Five users is five usersEverything on this page rests on a table with five rows. That is enough to demonstrate the mechanism and far too few to trust the result. With five points a correlation of 0.71 is not statistically remarkable at all: small samples produce large correlations by accident routinely, which is the same lesson Part 6 taught about conversion rates. The lecture is teaching you the tool here, not reporting a finding. Before acting on a leading indicator, ask for the sample size and the interval around the coefficient, exactly as you would for any other estimate.
Your teacher. Next time somebody shows you a correlation, ask for three things before you discuss the number: the scatter plot, the sample size, and what else could have moved both columns at once. Nearly every bad decision made from a correlation was made by somebody who had the number and none of the other three.
