TA Session 2 · Part 1 of 7 · Question 3

Three events on eight outcomes, and only one of the three pairs fails the product test

working 12 min Question 3, three parts, one mark each. The whole sample space fits on one line, which is exactly why this question is set.

The one-minute version

  • Eight equally likely outcomes. A is all three flips the same, B is at least one head and at least one tail, C is at most one head.
  • P(A) = 2/8 = 1/4, P(B) = 6/8 = 3/4, P(C) = 4/8 = 1/2.
  • (a) A and B are not independent. They cannot both happen, so P(A ∩ B) = 0, while P(A)P(B) = 3/16.
  • (b) B and C are independent. P(B ∩ C) = 3/8 and P(B)P(C) = 3/8.
  • (c) A and C are independent. P(A ∩ C) = 1/8 and P(A)P(C) = 1/8.

Question 3, restated

You flip a fair coin three times and note the outcome, which is an ordered triple in {H, T}3. Define three events:

  • A is the event that all tosses have the same result, so all heads or all tails.
  • B is the event that you see at least one head and at least one tail.
  • C is the event that you see at most one head.

Then three parts, each worth [1 mark]: (a) are A and B independent? (b) are B and C independent? (c) are A and C independent?

Which theorem is in play, and why the listing comes first

The governing definition is the product test from Lecture 3, Part 8: two events X and Y are independent exactly when the probability of both happening equals the product of the two separate probabilities.

X and Y are independent  ⇔  P(X ∩ Y) = P(X) P(Y) X ∩ Y, read "X and Y", is the event that both happen at once. The test is an equality between two numbers, so it is settled by computing both sides and comparing, never by intuition about whether the events "feel related". Note what the test is not: it says nothing about whether the events overlap, and an overlap of zero is a perfectly ordinary number to compare against a product.

Three flips of a fair coin give 23 = 8 outcomes, and because the coin is fair and the flips do not affect one another, all eight carry the same weight. That makes the counting formula legal, so every probability on this page is a count over eight. With only eight outcomes the honest move is to list them and read the events straight off the list.

Ω = {HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT} Ω, omega, is the sample space: every result the three flips can produce, written in order, so HHT and HTH are different outcomes. There are eight of them and each carries probability 1/8.

Reading the three events off the list

  1. Event A, all tosses the same. Only two outcomes qualify, all heads and all tails, so A = {HHH, TTT} and P(A) = 2/8 = 1/4.
  2. Event B, at least one head and at least one tail. Every outcome except the two pure ones, so B = {HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH} and P(B) = 6/8 = 3/4.
  3. Event C, at most one head. The outcomes with no head at all, plus the three with exactly one, so C = {TTT, HTT, THT, TTH} and P(C) = 4/8 = 1/2.

(a) Are A and B independent?

A says all three flips agree. B says they do not all agree. Both cannot be true of the same outcome, so the two events have nothing in common.

  1. The intersection is empty. A ∩ B = ∅, so P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  2. The product is not. P(A)P(B) = (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16, which is not zero.
  3. Compare. 0 is not 3/16, so the product test fails.
Answer, part (a)No, A and B are not independent. This is the source's own stated answer.
This course's observationThere is a shorter reason, and the notes do not give it. B is exactly the complement of A. "At least one head and at least one tail" is precisely "not all the same". So the two events partition the eight outcomes between them, which forces the intersection to be empty and forces the probabilities to add to one: 1/4 plus 3/4. An event and its complement can only be independent when one of them has probability zero or one, and neither does here. Spotting the complement answers part (a) in a line and also checks the two probabilities against each other.

(b) Are B and C independent?

  1. Intersect the two lists. C holds TTT, HTT, THT and TTH. Of those, TTT is not in B, because it has no head. The other three are. So B ∩ C = {HTT, THT, TTH}.
  2. Count it. Three outcomes out of eight, so P(B ∩ C) = 3/8.
  3. Form the product. P(B)P(C) = (3/4)(1/2) = 3/8.
  4. Compare. The two sides agree exactly.
Answer, part (b)Yes, B and C are independent. This is the source's own stated answer.

(c) Are A and C independent?

  1. Intersect. A holds HHH and TTT. HHH has three heads, so it is not in C. TTT has none, so it is. A ∩ C = {TTT}.
  2. Count it. P(A ∩ C) = 1/8.
  3. Form the product. P(A)P(C) = (1/4)(1/2) = 1/8.
  4. Compare. Equal again.
Answer, part (c)Yes, A and C are independent. This is the source's own stated answer.
Where this goes wrongThe trap is part (a), and it catches almost everybody once. Disjoint is not independent. It is the opposite of independent. If two events cannot both happen, then learning that one occurred tells you the other definitely did not, which is the strongest possible dependence. The confusion comes from the word "exclusive" sounding like "unrelated" in ordinary English. The arithmetic fix is to notice that disjointness gives P(X ∩ Y) = 0, and a product of two positive probabilities is never 0, so two disjoint events with positive probability are never independent. A second, smaller trap is reading C as "exactly one head", which drops TTT, gives P(C) = 3/8, and breaks both (b) and (c).

A check you can run without redoing the work

Three independent checks on the three answers, none of which repeats the calculation.

  1. The probabilities must be consistent. A and B are complements, so P(A) + P(B) must be exactly 1. It is: 1/4 plus 3/4.
  2. The intersection counts must not exceed either event. B ∩ C has three outcomes, which is at most C's four and at most B's six. A ∩ C has one, at most A's two.
  3. Independence should survive complementing. If B and C are independent then so are B and C's complement. Cc is "at least two heads" = {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH}, so P(Cc) = 4/8 = 1/2, and B ∩ Cc = {HHT, HTH, THH}, giving 3/8 against P(B)P(Cc) = 3/8. It holds, as it must. This third check is this course's own addition; the notes stop at the three parts asked.

Check yourself

Why does an empty intersection make A and B dependent rather than independent?
The test asks whether P(A and B) equals P(A)P(B), and here the left side is 0 while the right side is 3/16, so the equality fails and the events are dependent. Disjointness is the opposite of independence rather than a form of it: if one happens the other certainly did not. Having different probabilities is irrelevant, since B and C also differ and are independent. And independence is defined for any two events whatever their sizes.
B turns out to be exactly the complement of A. What follows from that?
Complements are disjoint and their probabilities add to one, so with P(A) = 1/4 the overlap is 0 while the product is 3/16, and independence is impossible unless one of them has probability zero or one. Complements are never automatically independent; that is the false rule this part is built to break. Their probabilities are 1/4 and 3/4, which are not equal. And complements cover the whole space between them by definition, which is what makes them complements.
Your teacher. When a question gives you a sample space small enough to write down, write it down. Eight outcomes take twenty seconds and they turn every part of this question into reading. The temptation is to reason about the events in words, which is where "at most one head" quietly becomes "exactly one head" and the whole question falls over. The listing is not a beginner's crutch; on a space this size it is simply the fastest correct method.
