Homework 1 · Graded problem 1 of 4 · sheet questions 1(a) and 1(b) · submitted

Two ratios of counts, and each part hides one decision

working 12 min Four marks: two for a 5×5 grid of independent entries pinned by three conditions at once, two for a random subset of ten numbers whose product must come out odd.

The one-minute version

  • Part (a) asks for the probability that three conditions hold at once in a 5×5 grid of independent zero-or-one entries. Two of the three conditions physically overlap, and the third condition names the cell where they do.
  • Part (b) asks for the chance that a uniformly random subset of {1, 2, ..., 10} has an odd product. That is a question about which numbers join the subset, not about how many do.
  • Both parts are ratios in a space where everything is equally likely: favourable outcomes over all outcomes.
  • This page names the sample spaces, the overlap, and the traps. It does not count either pile - the counting is the homework.

Part (a), restated in full

Let A be a 5×5 matrix. Every one of its 25 entries is a 0 or a 1, chosen equally likely and independently across entries. Find the probability that all three of the following conditions are satisfied simultaneously. [2 marks]

  1. The sum of the entries in the 2nd row is 4.
  2. The sum of the entries in the 2nd column is 3.
  3. The entry A2,2 - the cell in the 2nd row of the 2nd column - equals 1.

Every symbol, named: the matrix A is the whole grid of 25 entries. An entry is one cell, holding a 0 or a 1. Equally likely means every complete filling of the grid has the same probability. Independently across entries means what any one cell shows tells you nothing about what any other cell shows. The 2nd row is the row of five cells in the second position from the top, and the 2nd column is the column of five cells in the second position from the left. A2,2 is read row first, then column: second row, second column.

What it is really testing

This is the finite uniform model at work: a sample space in which every outcome is equally likely, so a probability is a ratio of two counts. TA Session 1, Geometry and counting states that model and counts favourable outcomes systematically; this problem is the same move in a grid instead of a list. The phrase independently across entries is the other load-bearing idea: Lecture 3, Independence defines it, and it is what lets the joint event be built up from per-entry facts rather than priced as one opaque lump. The overall shape - a sample space, an event collection, a measure defined by a counting rule - is the one carried end to end in Lecture 2, A discrete space worked.

The overlap is the whole problem

Look at the geometry before the arithmetic. The 2nd row is a strip of five cells running across the grid. The 2nd column is a strip of five cells running down it. Two strips of five, crossing in a grid of five by five, intersect in exactly one cell: A2,2. The first condition makes a demand that involves that cell, because the cell sits in the row. The second condition makes a demand that also involves it, because the cell sits in the column. And the third condition pins the cell outright: it must hold a 1.

The traps

  • Rows and columns count from 1 here, not from 0. The 2nd row is the second strip from the top. The engineering habit of zero-indexing puts every condition in the wrong place before a single line of work is done.
  • "Sum to 4" is a statement about a total, not about individual entries. Read it precisely, and work out for yourself what total 4 across five zero-or-one entries forces. The same care applies to the column's total of 3.
  • The three conditions are simultaneous. The question asks for one probability of a conjunction. Answering three separate probabilities, or answering them one at a time and stopping, answers a question the sheet did not ask.
  • "Independently across entries" is load-bearing. It is not scene-setting prose. It is the fact that lets a joint statement about 25 cells be assembled from per-cell facts. A solution that never uses it has almost certainly done something else instead.
  • The third bullet is a condition, not a definition. A2,2 = 1 is one of the three demands, on the same footing as the two sums, even though it looks more like a piece of notation.

How to start

Define the sample space first: one outcome is a complete filling of all 25 entries with zeros and ones. Note that the measure is uniform over those fillings, so the answer will be a ratio: the number of fillings satisfying all three conditions, over the number of fillings in total. Then stop. Which fillings satisfy the conditions, and how the shared cell lets you count them in pieces, is the homework.

Check yourself

Which single cell do the row condition and the column condition both count?
A row is a horizontal strip of five cells and a column is a vertical strip of five cells, so the 2nd row and the 2nd column cross in exactly one cell, A(2,2), which the third condition then pins. The corners A(1,1) and A(5,5) sit outside at least one of the two strips, and geometry gives the last option the lie: two crossing strips of a grid always share a cell.
The sheet says the entries are chosen independently across entries. What does that fact buy the solution?
Independence across entries means one cell's outcome carries no information about another's, which is precisely the licence for building a statement about many cells out of statements about single cells. Equal row totals, identical entries, and a surplus of zeros are all consequences of nothing in particular - independence says nothing about what the values actually are, only about how little they tell each other.

Part (b), restated in full

A subset is chosen uniformly at random from all possible subsets of {1, 2, ..., 10}. Find the probability that the product of the elements in the subset is odd. [2 marks]

Every symbol, named: a subset here is any selection of some of the ten numbers, in no order, with each number either in or out. Uniformly at random means every subset - every one of them, with no exceptions - is equally likely to be the one drawn. The product of the elements means multiply together everything the subset contains. Odd is the parity of that product.

What it is really testing

The sample space is not the ten numbers; it is the family of all subsets of them, each equally likely. The picture that makes this concrete: walk the numbers one at a time, and for each one decide in or out. Every complete run of decisions produces exactly one subset, and because the draw is uniform, every run is as good as every other. Geometry and counting shows the resulting ratio-of-counts method, and the multiplication rule is the tool that structures a decision made ten times in a row.

The event side needs one reminder, and it is the reframe the two marks are for. A product of whole numbers is odd exactly when every one of its factors is odd. One even factor anywhere in the multiplication is enough to make the whole product even. So "the product is odd" is a condition on which numbers the subset may contain - not on how many elements it has.

The empty subset is a real case, and it is yours to decide

The family of all subsets includes the empty subset: the draw where every number is out. It is one legitimate outcome of the experiment, exactly as likely as any other subset, so the event either catches it or does not, and your answer has to say which. The catch is that "the product of the elements" is a phrase written for non-empty subsets, and the empty subset has no elements to multiply. Mathematics resolves this by convention - there is a standard value assigned to the empty product - and the convention decides the empty subset's side of the event. Recall the convention, decide the case deliberately, and state the decision in your solution. A marker reading this problem looks for exactly that sentence, because it is the difference between having noticed the boundary case and having walked past it.

The traps

  • "Product of the elements" must not quietly assume two or more elements. A one-element subset's product is just that element, and the empty subset's product is the convention case above. The phrase has to be interpreted, not assumed.
  • Odd is about ingredients, not quantity. A subset with a single element can qualify or fail, and so can a large one. Nothing about the size of the subset decides the event on its own.
  • Uniform over subsets is one specific experiment. It is not "pick a random size first, then a random subset of that size". Those are different experiments with different answers, and the sheet's words pick the first. Work the experiment the sheet wrote.
  • The ten numbers are ingredients, not outcomes. The outcomes are the subsets. Reading the sample space as "the numbers 1 to 10" sends the whole solution down the wrong corridor before it starts.

How to start

Define the sample space: the family of all subsets of {1, 2, ..., 10}, each equally likely. Restate the event using the parity reminder: a condition on which numbers the subset may contain. Settle the empty subset's side of that condition, explicitly. Then stop. Turning the condition into a count, and the count into the ratio, is the homework.

Check yourself

A subset's product is odd exactly when which statement holds?
One even factor makes an entire product even, so a product is odd exactly when no even factor appears, which is the first option. The second is the classic impostor: parity of the count has nothing to do with parity of the product, and a two-element subset of odd numbers is a counterexample. The third fails the same way, since one odd element alongside an even one still leaves an even product. The fourth describes a condition about the number ten that no part of the problem suggests.
The draw comes up empty: the subset contains no numbers at all. What should the written solution do?
The empty subset is a genuine outcome of a uniform draw over all subsets, so it belongs either inside the event or outside it, and the solution must say which and why - which is exactly what deciding and stating demands. Dropping it loses a real case, asserting its parity without reasons is exactly the unsupported claim the sheet's instructions penalise, and the experiment makes it possible whether or not products feel well-defined without factors.
Your teacher. Both parts reward the same habit, which is why they share a page: fix the sample space in words before touching a number. In part (a) the outcome is a whole grid; in part (b) it is a whole subset. If you find yourself computing with a single entry or a single number as though it were an outcome, stop - you have left the sample space the sheet defined.
