TA Session 1 · Part 2 of 8 · Problem 3

A measure is only as good as its worst axiom

working 13 min Tutorial problem 3. Two famous laws hiding inside an audit exercise, and one impostor that passes two axioms out of three.

The one-minute version

  • Candidate (i) is the Poisson distribution and candidate (ii) the geometric distribution, though the sheet does not name them. Both are genuine probability measures.
  • The audit rests on one reusable observation: any non-negative weights summing to one define a measure by adding them over events.
  • Candidate (iii) - zero on finite sets, one on infinite ones - fails countable additivity, witnessed by the singletons {0}, {1}, {2}, ... .
  • Naming a familiar law beats verifying it from scratch: recognition tells you the answer before the algebra starts.

Problem 3, restated

The sample space is Ω = {0, 1, 2, ...}, the non-negative integers, and F = P(Ω) = 2Ω is its power set: the collection of all subsets of Ω, so every conceivable event is admissible. For each of the following set functions P, decide whether (Ω, F, P) is a probability space, justified by checking the probability-measure axioms.

(i)   P(A) = e−λ ∑x ∈ A λx/x! Read as: for an event A, sum over every member x of A the quantity lambda-to-the-x divided by x-factorial (x! means 1 × 2 × ... × x, with 0! defined as 1), all multiplied by e to the minus lambda. Here λ (lambda) is a fixed positive number and e is Euler's constant.
(ii)   P(A) = ∑x ∈ A p(1−p)x Read as: sum over the members x of A the weight p times one-minus-p to the power x, where p is a fixed number strictly between zero and one.
(iii)   P(A) = 0 if A has finitely many elements, and 1 if A has infinitely many elements Read as: small-looking sets get nothing, endless-looking sets get everything. There are no free parameters here at all.

A note on names: the question sheet prints the parameter in (i) as ω, the tutorial notes write λ; they are the same fixed positive constant. This page follows the notes.

Which theorem is in play, and why

A probability measure must satisfy three axioms: non-negativity (P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A), normalisation (P(Ω) = 1, the whole space carries all the mass), and countable additivity (for pairwise disjoint events A1, A2, ..., sharing no outcomes, the probability of their union equals the sum of their probabilities). All three candidates assign numbers to every event, so the question is purely whether the three axioms hold.

The working tool is a general observation from the tutorial notes. Suppose qx ≥ 0 for every x in Ω and ∑x qx = 1. Then setting P(A) equal to the sum of qx over members of A always produces a probability measure:

Candidate (i): a Poisson law

  1. Recognise it first. The weight e−λλx/x! evaluated at single points and summed over events is exactly the Poisson distribution with parameter λ - the standard model for counts of rare independent arrivals. Naming it answers the question in advance: textbooks list it as a probability distribution, so the axioms must hold. Now confirm it honestly anyway.
  2. Non-negativity. λ > 0, powers of a positive number stay positive, factorials are positive, e−λ > 0. Every term is non-negative, so every sum is. Pass.
  3. Normalisation, using the exponential series. The identity below turns the infinite sum into something already known.
∑x=0∞ λx/x! = eλ Read as: the exponential series - the defining expansion of e-to-the-lambda as an infinite sum of lambda-to-the-x over x-factorial. It converges for every value of lambda.
  1. Finish normalisation. P(Ω) = e−λ × eλ = e0 = 1. Pass.
  2. Additivity. The weights pass the general observation's two conditions, so disjoint unions split their sums cleanly. Pass.
  3. Conclude. (Ω, F, P) is a probability space: the Poisson measure with parameter λ.

Candidate (ii): a geometric law

  1. Recognise it. The weights p(1−p)x for x = 0, 1, 2, ... form the geometric distribution on {0, 1, 2, ...}: the classic model for "how many failures before the first success", where each trial succeeds with probability p.
  2. Non-negativity. 0 < p < 1 makes p positive and puts 1−p strictly between zero and one, so every power stays positive. Pass.
  3. Normalisation, using the geometric series.
∑x=0∞ (1−p)x = 1/(1−(1−p)) = 1/p Read as: the geometric-series formula - a starting term 1 followed by powers of the ratio one-minus-p, whose infinite total is one over one minus that ratio. Since the ratio here equals one-minus-p, the total is one over p.
  1. Finish normalisation. P(Ω) = p × (1/p) = 1. Pass.
  2. Additivity. Same appeal to the general observation as in candidate (i). Pass.
  3. Conclude. (Ω, F, P) is a probability space: the geometric measure with parameter p.

Both laws paint the same kind of picture - a unit of probability mass smeared across the non-negative integers - with different shapes:

Candidate (iii): the impostor

  1. Non-negativity. Zero and one are both non-negative, so P(A) ≥ 0 everywhere. Pass.
  2. Normalisation. Ω itself has infinitely many elements, so P(Ω) = 1. Pass.
  3. Suspicion. Every individual outcome got probability zero, yet the whole space carries probability one. Infinitely many zeros somehow summed to one. Countable additivity is where that tension must break, so test it there.
  4. Build the witness sequence. Let An = {n} for n = 0, 1, 2, .... Each singleton is finite, so P(An) = 0 for every n. The singletons are pairwise disjoint, and their union is ∪n An = Ω.
  5. Compare the two sides of the axiom. Left side: P(Ω) = 1. Right side: ∑n P(An) = 0 + 0 + ... = 0. And 1 ≠ 0. Fail.
  6. Conclude. Countable additivity fails, so (Ω, F, P) is not a probability space.
Where this goes wrongThe tempting wrong turn is declaring candidate (iii) absurd and skipping the check - or, worse, accepting it because the first two axioms pass. A third slip is testing additivity on the wrong sets, such as nested finite sets; the witness must be pairwise disjoint with a union you can compute, and singletons are the cleanest possible choice. Notice also what made candidates (i) and (ii) easy: reducing everything to the weight sequence once, instead of re-arguing each axiom per event.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does defining P(A) as the sum of weights qx over members of A automatically satisfy countable additivity?
When events are disjoint, no outcome is counted twice, so summing over the union equals chaining the per-event sums - that is the whole argument. Events here are frequently infinite, since the space itself is. Weights below one do not matter; the geometric weights shrink yet still misbehave if the total misses one. And positive series converge to many values, not only one.
For the zero-on-finite, one-on-infinite candidate, which axiom fails, and where?
Infinity is precisely why normalisation passes: P(Omega) = 1. Singletons receive zero, which is perfectly non-negative. The broken axiom is additivity: P({0} ∪ {1} ∪ ...) = 1 while the summed parts give 0. Accepting it as valid ignores that a measure may not create mass by collecting empty pieces.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1 - a third family of weightsworking

On Ω = {0, 1, 2, ...} with F its power set, define P(A) = ∑x ∈ A 2−(x+1). Is (Ω, F, P) a probability space?

Hint

Write out the first few weights and see which familiar series they form.

Show the worked solution
  1. List the weights. At x = 0: 2−1 = 1/2. At x = 1: 1/4. At x = 2: 1/8. Halving forever.
  2. Total them. ∑x≥0 2−(x+1) = 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... = 1 by the geometric series with ratio 1/2.
  3. Apply the general observation. The weights are positive and sum to one, so P is a probability measure.
  4. Conclude. Yes - this is a genuine probability space, sometimes met again as the measure behind binary expansions of a uniform random number.

Sanity check. A halving series must approach 2, and half of that is 1, matching the required total mass.

Practice 2 - the impostor, flipped onto a finite spacefoundation

Keep candidate (iii)'s rule - zero on finite sets, one on infinite ones - but change the sample space to the finite set Ω = {1, 2, ..., 10}. Which axiom fails now, and does the earlier counterexample still apply?

Hint

Ask first what the rule even assigns to Ω when Ω is finite.

Show the worked solution
  1. Apply the rule on a finite space. Every subset of a ten-point space is finite, including Ω itself. So P(A) = 0 for every A.
  2. Test normalisation. P(Ω) = 0 ≠ 1. Normalisation fails outright.
  3. The old counterexample is gone, not fixed: there are no infinite sets left, so the old failure mode cannot even be stated. The rule dies earlier, at a different axiom.
  4. Conclude. Not a probability space - and the lesson is that the same formula can fail different axioms on different spaces, so the audit always restarts from scratch.

Sanity check. A function assigning zero everywhere cannot hand the full space its required unit of mass, whatever else is true about it.

Your teacher. If the exponential-series step feels like a trick pulled from nowhere, ask for the first five terms of λx/x! with λ = 1 and watch them reproduce 1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/24, closing on e. If recognising distributions interests you, ask which real-world counters - bus arrivals in an hour, typos per page - each law models.
