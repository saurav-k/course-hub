When the exact answer is out of reach, bracket it

12 min working Inclusion-exclusion with 20 events needs over a million terms. Cutting the sum short still tells you the truth, up to a bracket you can compute.

The one-minute version

  • Stop the inclusion-exclusion sum after level 1 and you get the union bound: a union is never more likely than the sum of its parts.
  • Stop after level 2 and the sum dips below the truth: a lower bound. Stop after level 3 and you are back above it. Odd truncations are ceilings, even truncations are floors. These are the Bonferroni inequalities.
  • Worked in full: 100 balls into 20 bins, the probability that some bin stays empty. The bracket lands at 0.1133 to 0.118, and the true value is 0.1134.
  • A bracket you can compute beats an exact answer you cannot.

1. Why the exact sum is hopeless exactly when it matters

Inclusion-exclusion for n events has a level for every intersection size, so the total number of terms is n + C(n, 2) + C(n, 3) + ... + 1, which grows like 2n. For 20 events, the pairs level alone holds C(20, 2) = 190 terms, and the triples level holds C(20, 3) = 1140. For a question like "does some server out of 20 fail this hour", every one of those terms is a real probability you would have to compute. The exact formula is correct and unusable at the same time, which is a familiar combination in engineering.

The escape comes from the region tally of Part 2. Each level of the sum corrects the overcount left by the levels before it, overshooting and undershooting in turn as it converges on the truth. Stop the sum anywhere and you land on one side of the truth, and which side depends only on the parity of the levels you kept.

2. The three inequalities the notes state

Union bound: P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An) ≤ ∑i P(Ai) A1 through An are n events, not necessarily disjoint. The sum adds the n single probabilities. The inequality says the union, "at least one of them happens", is at most that sum, because throws counted by several events get counted several times in the sum.
Second Bonferroni: P(∪ Ai) ≥ ∑i P(Ai) − ∑i<j P(Ai ∩ Aj) Keep the first two levels of inclusion-exclusion and the sum drops to the other side of the truth. The second sum runs over every pair i < j, and subtracts each pairwise overlap once.
Third Bonferroni: P(∪ Ai) ≤ ∑i P(Ai) − ∑i<j P(Ai ∩ Aj) + ∑i<j<k P(Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak) Keep three levels and the sum climbs back above the truth. Each inequality in the family is the exact formula with a tail cut off, and the cut end alternates between a ceiling and a floor.

The tally behind the alternation is the one from Part 2. A region lying in exactly k events contributes C(k, 1) to the level-1 sum, C(k, 1) − C(k, 2) to the level-1-plus-2 sum, C(k, 1) − C(k, 2) + C(k, 3) to the three-level sum, and so on. Each partial tally is never below 0 and never above 1, and it approaches 1 from above at odd depths and from below at even ones. Summed over all regions, that is exactly the bracketing behaviour the inequalities claim.

3. The worked example: 100 balls, 20 bins

The notes run one example all the way through. Throw 100 balls independently into 20 bins, each ball choosing a bin uniformly at random. Let Ai be the event that bin i ends up empty. The question is the probability that at least one bin is empty:

P(some bin is empty) = P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ ... ∪ A20) Ai is the event that bin i receives no balls. The union is the event that at least one bin is empty, which is the question "some bin is empty" asked in set language.
  1. Price one empty bin. Bin 1 stays empty exactly when all 100 balls avoid it. Each ball avoids bin 1 with probability 19/20, and the balls choose independently, so P(A1) = (19/20)100. The same price holds for every bin.
  2. Apply the union bound. P(∪ Ai) ≤ 20 × (19/20)100 ≈ 0.118, the figure the notes state. One line of arithmetic, and the answer is already pinned above.
  3. Price an empty pair. Bins 1 and 2 are both empty exactly when every ball lands in the other 18 bins, so P(A1 ∩ A2) = (18/20)100, and again the same price holds for every pair.
  4. Apply the second Bonferroni inequality. There are C(20, 2) = 190 pairs, so P(∪ Ai) ≥ 0.118 − 190 × (18/20)100 ≈ 0.1133, the notes' figure.
  5. Read the bracket. 0.1133 ≤ P(some bin is empty) ≤ 0.118. The notes also state the true value, which they give as ≈ 0.1134: the floor is already within about a tenth of a percentage point of the truth.
Why this matters more than it looksThe pattern here is not a probability curiosity; it is how hard reliability questions are actually answered. "At least one of these 20 dependencies fails this quarter", "at least one of these 100 hash buckets overflows", "at least one of these checks fires falsely": each is a union of many awkwardly-overlapping events. The union bound prices the ceiling in one line, the second Bonferroni inequality prices the floor in two, and a decision rarely needs more than the two of them.
Where the notes are looseThe notes state the three inequalities and work the example, but they do not prove why truncation brackets the truth; the region-tally argument in section 2 is this page's filling-in of that step. The true value ≈ 0.1134 is quoted from the notes as a stated figure rather than computed here, since computing it exactly is precisely the job the bracket exists to avoid.

Check yourself

You need the probability that at least one of 40 checks fires, and the intersections are uncomputable. The union bound gives you what?
The union bound is the level-1 truncation of an alternating sum, and level 1 is a ceiling: P(union) ≤ the sum of the single probabilities. Calling it a floor points the inequality the wrong way, which is the second Bonferroni inequality's job, not the union bound's. Forty events hold about 2 to the 40 inclusion-exclusion terms, so exact is out of reach, which is the entire reason the bound exists. And the maximum of the forty single probabilities is a different, much cruder ceiling that ignores how many events there are; the sum is what uses all the information you have.
In the balls-and-bins example, why does P(A1) come out as (19/20)100?
Bin 1 is empty when all 100 balls independently avoid it, each with probability 19/20, and multiplying 19/20 by itself 100 times is exactly (19/20) to the 100. The nineteen is the number of other bins, not a count of balls, so the second option misreads the fraction. The third describes a nearly-full bin, which is the opposite event. The fourth is the event that bin 1 receives every ball, whose probability would be (1/20) to the 100.
Your teacher. Rerun the bracket with different dials and watch which side moves: 100 balls in 10 bins, then 1000 balls in 20 bins. In the first, the union bound is wildly loose because many bins are empty at once and the overlaps are huge. In the second, the answer collapses towards zero and the bracket follows. Knowing when the cheap bracket is tight is the actual skill, and you now have a sandbox to build it in.
