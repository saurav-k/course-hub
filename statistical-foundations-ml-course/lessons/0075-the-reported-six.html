TA Session 2 · Part 6 of 7 · Question 30

A witness who is right three times in four still leaves a reported six more likely false than true

working 12 min Question 30. The second Bayes question, and the first where the prior is lopsided enough to overturn the intuition entirely.

The one-minute version

  • A man tells the truth 3 times out of 4. He throws a fair die and reports a six. How likely is it actually a six?
  • The prior is P(S) = 1/6, and the die is fair, so P(Sc) = 5/6.
  • Truthfulness gives P(R | S) = 3/4, and the notes take the lying case as P(R | Sc) = 1/4.
  • Bayes: P(S | R) = (3/4)(1/6) / [(3/4)(1/6) + (1/4)(5/6)] = (1/8) / (1/8 + 5/24) = 3/8.
  • So 37.5%. Most people guess something near 75 percent, because that is the number in the question, and the gap is the whole lesson.

Question 30, restated

A man is known to speak the truth 3 out of 4 times. He throws a die and reports that it is a six. Find the probability that it is actually a six.

Which theorem is in play, and the two populations it needs

Name the two events. Both matter, and the question is easy to misread because the words "six" appear in both.

  • S is the event that the die actually shows a six. This is about the world.
  • R is the event that the man reports a six. This is about the testimony.

The question gives facts about the testimony conditioned on the world, and asks for the world conditioned on the testimony, which is the reversal Part 3 built Bayes for. The die is a fair die, which the question does not say in so many words but which the notes assume when they write the prior.

  1. The prior, from the die. One face in six is a six, so P(S) = 1/6 and P(Sc) = 5/6.
  2. The truthful case. If it really is a six, he reports a six exactly when he tells the truth, so P(R | S) = 3/4.
  3. The lying case. If it is not a six, then reporting a six requires him to lie, which happens with probability 1 − 3/4 = 1/4. The notes therefore set P(R | Sc) = 1/4.
Where the question is loose, and this course says soStep 3 hides an assumption the notes do not state. "He lies" and "he says six" are treated as the same event, but if the die shows a three and he decides to lie, he has five wrong answers available and only one of them is "six". Under the stricter reading his chance of saying six after a non-six would be (1/4) × (1/5) = 1/20, and the answer would come out at 3/4 rather than 3/8. This is a known ambiguity in this classic problem, not a defect in the arithmetic. The notes take the first reading and this course quotes their answer, 3/8, as the answer to the question as set. The alternative is named here because a reader who spots it should know they have spotted something real rather than made a mistake. Neither the value 1/20 nor the value 3/4 is on the board; both are this course's own working.

Step 1: price the four roads

  1. Six, and he says so. (1/6)(3/4) = 1/8, which over a denominator of 24 is 3/24.
  2. Six, and he denies it. (1/6)(1/4) = 1/24.
  3. Not six, and he says six. (5/6)(1/4) = 5/24.
  4. Not six, and he says so. (5/6)(3/4) = 15/24.
  5. Check. 3 + 1 + 5 + 15 = 24, so the four roads account for everything exactly once.

Step 2: keep only the roads where he says six

The report is the evidence, so the world shrinks to the roads that produce it. Two of the four do: the true one worth 3/24 and the false one worth 5/24. Together they are 8/24, and the answer is the true one's share of that.

Step 3: apply Bayes

P(S | R) = P(R | S)P(S) / [ P(R | S)P(S) + P(R | Sc)P(Sc) ] The numerator is the one road that is both a real six and a report of six. The denominator is every road that ends in a report of six, which is the law of total probability doing the same job it did on Part 5. Sc, read "S complement", is the event that the die shows anything but a six.
  1. Substitute. P(S | R) = (3/4)(1/6) / [(3/4)(1/6) + (1/4)(5/6)].
  2. Evaluate the two products. (3/4)(1/6) = 1/8 and (1/4)(5/6) = 5/24.
  3. Put them over a common denominator. 1/8 is 3/24, so the denominator is 3/24 + 5/24 = 8/24.
  4. Divide. (3/24) / (8/24) = 3/8.
AnswerP(actually a six | reported a six) = 3/8 = 37.5%. The notes state it in exactly that form.
Where this goes wrongThe dominant error is answering 3/4, by treating the man's reliability as the answer. That confuses P(R | S), how often he says six when it is one, with P(S | R), how often it is one when he says six, and it ignores the die entirely. The second error is forgetting the prior: reasoning that he is right three times in four so the report is right three times in four, which would be correct only if sixes and non-sixes were equally common. The third is using 1/6 as the answer, by ignoring the testimony instead. The right answer sits between the prior of 1/6 and the reliability of 3/4, closer to the prior, because the prior is lopsided and the evidence is only moderately strong.

A check you can run without redoing the work

  1. The complement must work out. P(not a six | reported six) = 5/24 over 8/24 = 5/8, and 3/8 + 5/8 = 1.
  2. The answer must lie between the prior and the reliability. The prior is 1/6 ≈ 0.167 and the reliability is 0.75. The answer, 0.375, sits between them, which any genuine piece of evidence must produce: it moves belief without replacing it.
  3. Push the prior and watch. If the die had only two faces so that P(S) = 1/2, the same testimony would give (3/4)(1/2) over [(3/4)(1/2) + (1/4)(1/2)] = 3/4, which is the reliability exactly. That is the sanity check on the whole method: with an even prior, the posterior equals the witness's accuracy, and every departure from 3/4 in the real question is the prior doing work. This variation is this course's own.

Check yourself

Where does the figure P(reports six given not a six) = 1/4 come from?
He lies one time in four, and the notes additionally treat a lie about a non-six as always being the word "six", which is what turns the lying rate into this conditional. The die fixes the prior of 1/6 rather than any reporting rate. Bayes consumes this number rather than producing it, so it must be established first. And the last option is half right and half wrong: 1/4 is indeed the complement of 3/4, but the complement of "tells the truth" is "lies", and getting from there to "says six" is the assumption the page flags.
Why is the answer well below one half, even though the man is right three times in four?
There are five non-sixes for every six, so even a one-in-four lying rate applied to that large pool produces 5/24 of false reports against only 3/24 true ones. Three in four is high rather than low, and it is precisely what lifts the answer from the prior of 1/6 up to 3/8. The die is fair, which is what fixes the prior at 1/6. And Bayes has no such rule: with an even prior the same testimony would give 3/4, comfortably above a half.
Your teacher. This question is the medical test from Lecture 4, Part 3 wearing a different costume, and it is worth putting them side by side. There, a test that catches 99 percent of a disease left a positive patient only about 17 percent likely to be ill. Here, a witness right 75 percent of the time leaves a reported six 37.5 percent likely to be true. In both, a reliable-sounding signal is swamped because the thing it is signalling is rare. If you can state that sentence without looking at either page, both questions are yours, and so is the next one, which is the same story with the rarity turned up hard.
