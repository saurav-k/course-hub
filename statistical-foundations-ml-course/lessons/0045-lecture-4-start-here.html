Lecture 4: the applied pass, where the rules meet real numbers

start here 7 min Aug 25, 2026. Three items on the board: one worked conditional-probability example, independence, and counting. Twelve sittings, because the arithmetic is where this lecture actually teaches.

The one-minute version

  • Lecture 3 gave you conditional probability, total probability, Bayes rule and independence as definitions. This lecture uses them, and the using is the point.
  • Part 1 to Part 4 work one medical test from end to end. A test that catches 99 percent of a disease, applied to a population where 1 percent have it, leaves a positive patient about 0.17 likely to be ill. Five times in six the alarm is false.
  • Part 5 to Part 7 turn independence from a definition into a check you can run on a table: a thousand users, four cells, and a product that either matches or does not. Then the n-event condition, and conditional independence.
  • Part 8 to Part 12 build the counting machinery. When every outcome is equally likely, a probability is a ratio of two counts, so the whole job is counting, and there are exactly four ways to count a sample.
  • Two places on these pages are honest about a gap rather than smooth over it, and both are named below before you meet them.

What is new here, and what is not

This lecture overlaps with material already on the site, and it is worth being exact about which parts, because re-reading something you already own is the fastest way to lose an evening.

Conditional probability, the law of total probability and Bayes rule were all built in Lecture 3, and independence was defined there too. TA Session 1, part 7 already separates pairwise independence from mutual independence. None of that is re-taught here. Every page below links back to the page that owns the definition and then does something with it.

What is genuinely new is this: a single worked example carried far enough that the answer surprises you, a table-shaped test for independence, the definition of conditional independence, and the counting rules that Lecture 3 assumed but never wrote down.

How this lecture is arranged

The lecturer put three items on the board at the top of the session, in this order: an example of conditional probability, independence, and counting and sampling. The twelve pages below keep that order, because the example is what makes the rest feel necessary.

Read them in sequence the first time. After that, the Lecture 4 formula sheet is the faster way back in, and it carries the four-box sampling table on its own, which is the one thing from this lecture worth printing.

The twelve parts

  1. The three numbers a test carries. Prevalence, sensitivity and specificity, each named in words, and the one observation that makes the rest of the example work: every number a test ships with points the wrong way round.
  2. Two roads to a positive result. The law of total probability applied to the alarm itself. There are two ways to see a plus sign, and the wrong one is five times as common as the right one.
  3. The base rate answer. Bayes rule, one fraction, and the lecture's stated figure of 0.17. Then what has to change for the answer to change.
  4. The negative result, left unfinished. The notes set up the reverse decomposition and stop without evaluating it. This page says so, then works it out under this course's own name.
  5. Independence, checked on a table. A thousand users, a two-by-two table, and independence as a product that either matches or does not. The equivalent conditional form falls out of the same margins.
  6. Independence for n events. The definition quantifies over every subset, which is many more equations than anybody expects. Three events need four, not one.
  7. Conditional independence. The definition as the notes give it, then a worked example built for this course, because the notes give none.
  8. Equally likely means counting. When every outcome carries the same weight, a probability is favourable outcomes over total outcomes, and the rest of the lecture is about the two counts.
  9. The basic counting rule. Independent choices multiply. Two examples from the notes, one of them a machine-learning hyperparameter grid.
  10. Ordered sampling: the two cases. Draw n from m when order matters, once with the ball going back into the basket and once without.
  11. Unordered sampling: the two cases. The same two draws when order is thrown away: the binomial coefficient, and the stars-and-bars count for multisets.
  12. The four boxes, side by side. The summary table the lecture builds up to, all four formulas on one grid, and what Lecture 4 leaves you holding.

The two honesty notes, named in advance

This course quotes what the lecture states and marks anything it works out itself. Twice in Lecture 4 that distinction matters enough to carry a callout on the page.

The lecture does not finish P(D given a negative test)After the positive-test answer, the notes write the reverse decomposition P(D) = P(+) P(D | +) + P(−) P(D | −) and stop there. No number is evaluated. Part 4 shows the identity exactly as the notes leave it, then finishes the arithmetic in a separate callout marked as this course's own derivation. The decision follows the policy recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md, which itself follows the shape of learning-records/0001-quoting-a-deck-that-does-not-recompute.md.
Conditional independence has a definition and no exampleThe notes define conditional independence in one line and move on. Part 7 therefore builds an example of its own, using two API replicas behind one shared database, and labels it plainly as this course's construction rather than the lecture's. Nothing invented is presented as though the lecturer said it.

The glossary holds one-sentence definitions for every term, and the Lecture 4 formula sheet holds every formula in this lecture on a single printable page, including the four-box sampling table.
Your teacher. Carry one question through the first four pages: which population am I standing in? Sensitivity stands inside the ill; specificity stands inside the well; the patient with the positive result stands inside neither and has to be told which is more likely. Almost every mistake anybody makes with this example is a step taken while standing in the wrong population, and once you can name the population out loud at each step, the answer stops being surprising and starts being obvious.
