The opposite of finding at least one bad label is finding none, not finding only bad ones

working 10 min The second problem, and the one where the sampling switches flip: distinct images, no order, so the answers are ratios of counts.

The one-minute version

  • A dataset holds 1000 labelled images, of which 50 are mislabelled. A data scientist selects 20 distinct images at random and inspects them. X is how many mislabelled images the check finds.
  • Distinct images and no order means case 3. There are C(1000, 20) equally likely ways to choose the twenty, and the class notes expand that as 1000! / (20! · 980!).
  • A check that finds nothing is a check whose twenty images all came from the 950 correctly labelled ones, so Pr(X = 0) = C(950, 20) / C(1000, 20).
  • Then Pr(X ≥ 1) = 1 − C(950, 20) / C(1000, 20). The event subtracted is "every inspected image is correct". Subtracting "every inspected image is wrong" instead is the classic error, and the class stopped on it.

1. The problem, and the two switches

The problem. A machine-learning dataset contains 1000 labelled images. It is known that 50 of these images have incorrect labels. As part of a dataset quality check, a data scientist randomly selects 20 distinct images from the dataset for manual inspection. Let X denote the number of incorrectly labelled images found among the twenty inspected. What is the probability that the check finds none, and what is the probability that it detects at least one?

Two words settle the sample space, and both differ from Part 1.

  • Distinct. No image is inspected twice, so this is sampling without replacement. The pool shrinks with every image taken out.
  • Selects for inspection. Nothing in the question depends on the sequence in which the twenty images were pulled. A set of twenty images inspected in one order is the same quality check as the same twenty in another. So the sampling is without ordering.

Because the selection is random and every set of twenty is as likely as any other, the equally-likely formula is licensed and a probability here really is one count over another. The handout says this in as many words: there are C(1000, 20) equally likely ways of selecting the twenty images.

|Ω| = C(1000, 20) = 1000! / (20! · 980!) C(1000, 20), read "1000 choose 20", counts the sets of twenty images that can be drawn from a thousand, ignoring order and forbidding repeats. The factorial form on the right is the case 3 formula with m = 1000 and n = 20: 1000! arranges the whole dataset, 980! cancels the arrangements of the images left behind, and 20! cancels the arrangements of the twenty chosen, which is the ordering being thrown away. The class notes write this expansion out beside the answer.

2. A check that finds nothing

Name the event. X = 0 is the event that not one of the twenty inspected images is mislabelled, which is exactly the event that all twenty were drawn from the correctly labelled part of the dataset.

How large is that part? A thousand images with fifty bad ones leaves 950 good ones. Choosing twenty images all from those 950 is the same counting problem as before with a smaller pool.

  1. Count the favourable sets. Twenty images, all from the 950 correctly labelled ones, unordered and distinct: C(950, 20) of them.
  2. Count all the sets. Twenty images from all thousand: C(1000, 20).
  3. Divide, because the outcomes are equally likely. Pr(X = 0) = C(950, 20) / C(1000, 20).

Both counts are counts of the same kind of object, a set of twenty images, which is what makes the division meaningful. The numerator is not a smaller version of a different question; it is the same question asked over a restricted pool.

3. The same answer as a falling product, and what it comes to

The ratio of two binomial coefficients hides a great deal of cancellation. Writing both out and cancelling turns it into a product of twenty fractions, which is both easier to compute and easier to compare with Part 1. This rewriting is this course's own; the sources leave the ratio as it stands.

  1. Write both out. C(950, 20) = 950! / (20! · 930!) and C(1000, 20) = 1000! / (20! · 980!).
  2. Divide, and the 20! cancels. The ratio becomes (950! · 980!) / (930! · 1000!).
  3. Cancel the factorials in pairs. 950!/930! is the falling product 950 × 949 × ... × 931, and 980!/1000! is one over 1000 × 999 × ... × 981.
  4. Read the result. Pr(X = 0) = (950 × 949 × ... × 931) / (1000 × 999 × ... × 981), which is twenty fractions multiplied together.

Each fraction has a plain reading: given that every image inspected so far was correctly labelled, the next one is correctly labelled with probability "good images still available over images still in the pool". That is the multiplication rule again, exactly as in Part 1, with one difference that matters.

This course's arithmeticNeither source evaluates the ratio. Multiplying out the twenty fractions gives Pr(X = 0) = 0.3549 to four places, so Pr(X ≥ 1) = 0.6451. In plain words: a twenty-image spot check of a dataset that is five percent mislabelled misses the problem entirely about 35 percent of the time, and catches it about 65 percent of the time. Neither figure is on the board and neither carries a key-number mark. Swap the dataset for a canary deployment and the sentence still holds: a 20-request spot check of a change whose error rate rose to 5 percent overlooks the regression about a third of the time, which is the minimum-sample-size argument behind any serious canary analysis.

4. At least one, and the complement that people get backwards

The second question asks for Pr(X ≥ 1), the probability that the check detects at least one mislabelled image. Head on this is a sum of twenty terms, one for each possible value of X from 1 to 20. By the complement it is one subtraction.

Pr(X ≥ 1) = 1 − Pr(X = 0) = 1 − C(950, 20) / C(1000, 20) X ≥ 1 means the check found one or more mislabelled images. Its complement is X = 0, the check found none. There is no third possibility, so the two probabilities add to one and each is one minus the other. Note what is not here: no count of mislabelled images appears in this formula at all, because the event being subtracted never touches them.

Here is the mistake, and it is common enough that the class stopped on it. The phrase "at least one incorrect" makes people reach for "all incorrect" as its opposite, because the two sentences look like mirror images. They are not. The opposite of some is none, never all.

  1. The event. At least one of the twenty inspected images is mislabelled.
  2. Its complement, in words. None of the twenty inspected images is mislabelled, which is to say every one of them is correctly labelled.
  3. Its complement, as a set. All twenty were drawn from the 950 good images, which is the event already priced in section 2.
  4. The wrong complement, for contrast. "All twenty are mislabelled" is a real event, with probability C(50, 20) / C(1000, 20), but it is not the opposite of anything asked here. It leaves out every check that found one, or two, or nineteen bad images, and those are the bulk of the space.

It is genuinely useful and it is not on these pages, because no such figure exists in either source and this course does not invent one and attribute it to the lecture. The bar above is this course's own arithmetic drawn to scale, which is a different thing from the set picture that was promised.
One mark on the class notes cannot be readBeside the line for Pr(X = 0) the notes carry a second binomial coefficient, C(50, 20), with an arrow pointing at it and no surrounding words. Two readings are plausible and nothing on the page settles which: it may be the count for the opposite extreme, all twenty inspected images coming from the fifty bad ones, jotted as a contrast; or it may be a slip of the pen for the C(950, 20) directly above it. This course names both readings and builds on neither. The solution as the handout states it uses C(950, 20) and nothing else.

5. Check yourself

For "the check finds at least one mislabelled image", which event is the complement?
The opposite of finding one or more is finding none, which means every inspected image turned out to be correctly labelled, and that event has probability C(950, 20) over C(1000, 20). Option two is the opposite extreme rather than the opposite: it is a genuine event, but subtracting it would leave out every check that found between one and nineteen bad images. Option three is almost certain and is the complement of "all twenty are mislabelled", a different question. And option four is a restatement of the original event, not its complement.
Which of the four boxes counts the ways to choose the twenty inspected images?
The problem says distinct, so there is no replacement, and nothing about the answer depends on the order the images were pulled, so the outcome is a set: that is case 3 and the count is C(1000, 20). Case 1 would let the same image be inspected twice, which a manual check does not do. Case 2 is not wrong so much as wasteful: keeping the order multiplies the favourable count and the total count by the same 20!, so it cancels and gives the same answer through more work. And case 4 counts multisets, which needs repeats to be possible.
Your teacher. Write the complement out as a full English sentence before you write any symbol for it. Not "not at least one", which is a phrase nobody can picture, but "every single one of the twenty images I looked at turned out to be fine". Said that way the count writes itself, because the sentence names the pool the twenty came from. The failure mode this page is about is entirely a language failure, and it is defeated by refusing to work in symbols until the English is right.
