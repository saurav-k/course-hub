Sampling more people buys accuracy fast, and three numbers say how fast

working 10 min The lecture's parts (a), (b) and (c), worked to four places, with the board's own figures checked.

After this page

  • You can compute a poll's error probability at any sample size, and say what the extra people bought.
  • You can spot a quoted probability that belongs to a different sample size than the one it is attached to.

Before you start: a poll is a binomial in disguise, which sets up the model this page evaluates three times.

The one-minute version

  • The lecture asks its own question three times, at n = 5, n = 21 and n = 101, keeping p = 0.6 throughout.
  • The error probability is 0.3174, then 0.1744, then 0.0209. Twenty times the sample cuts the error by a factor of fifteen.
  • The gain is front-loaded. The first twenty extra people buy far more than the last eighty do.
  • The board quotes ≈ 0.25 and ≈ 0.03 for the last two. Those are the values for n = 20 and n = 100, not 21 and 101, and a student said so live.
  • This is how a sampling budget is set: pick the error you can live with, then read the sample size off the curve.

1. The error event moves with n

The poll names the sample's majority, so the poll is wrong when supporters of A are not the majority. That threshold shifts with the sample size.

P(poll is wrong) = P(X ≤ ⌊n/2⌋) = FX(⌊n/2⌋) X is the number of sampled people who support A. n is the sample size. ⌊n/2⌋, read "floor of n over two", is n halved and rounded down: it is the largest count that still leaves A short of a majority. FX is the CDF of X, so FX(⌊n/2⌋) is the total probability of every count from 0 up to that threshold. Both the distribution and the threshold change when n changes, which is why the three cases below cannot be read off one another.

At n = 5 the threshold is 2. At n = 21 it is 10. At n = 101 it is 50. The lecturer picks odd sample sizes deliberately, and says why on the recording: with n odd there can be no tie, so the poll always has a majority to name.

2. The three cases, worked

Every term below is nCk × 0.6k × 0.4n − k, the binomial mass at count k. The only thing that changes between the cases is how many of those terms get added.

  1. Case (a), n = 5, threshold 2. Three terms. 1 × 0.45 = 0.01024, then 5 × 0.6 × 0.44 = 0.0768, then 10 × 0.36 × 0.43 = 0.2304. Adding: 0.01024 + 0.0768 + 0.2304 = 0.31744.
  2. Case (b), n = 21, threshold 10. Eleven terms, and they are wildly unequal. The k = 10 term alone is 352,716 × 0.610 × 0.411 = 0.089454, the k = 9 term is 0.049696, the k = 8 term is 0.022937, and everything from k = 7 down contributes 0.012291 between them. Adding all eleven: 0.174378.
  3. Case (c), n = 101, threshold 50. Fifty-one terms, and now the concentration is extreme: the k = 50 term is 0.008189, the ten terms from k = 41 to k = 50 come to 0.020869, and all forty-one terms below k = 41 add up to 0.0000278. The total is 0.020897.
  4. Read the three together. 0.3174, then 0.1744, then 0.0209. Going from 5 to 21 people roughly halves the error; going from 21 to 101 divides it by another eight.

The two long sums were evaluated term by term with exact binomial coefficients rather than approximated, and the individual terms quoted above are there so a reader can check any one of them by hand. The pattern they show is worth naming: the sum is dominated by the terms nearest the threshold, because counts far below it require an implausible run of B answers from a population that is 60% A.

Every quoted figure on the board is the even-n valueThe board reads ≈ 0.25 beside n = 21 and ≈ 0.03 beside n = 101. Recomputed here: P(X ≤ 10) = 0.1744 for n = 21 and P(X ≤ 50) = 0.0209 for n = 101, while 0.2447 is n = 20 and 0.0271 is n = 100. So both board figures are the even-sample values, one step below the sizes they are written against. A student computed 0.17 during the session and challenged the 0.25; the lecturer answered that he had "probably computed it for 20", that he was not sure of the number, and that what he was sure of was the formula. He had the cause exactly right. Take the formula from the lecture and these four decimals from this page.

3. What a sample costs

Accuracy is bought, not found. The curve above says what a sample size gets you; this says what it costs, and the two together are the whole sampling decision.

That is the shape of the answer to "how many people must you poll". You do not pick n by intuition and you do not pick it by budget alone: you name the error you can tolerate, read the sample size off the curve, and then check that you can afford it. The lecturer's own summary is that this gives you a handle on selecting the sampling size, and that this is one of the key questions in polling.

4. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Going from 21 people to 101 people, what happens to the error probability?
The two computed values are 0.1744 and 0.0209, and 0.1744 divided by 0.0209 is about 8.3. The first option uses the board's 0.03, which is the value for a sample of 100 rather than 101. The second describes a proportional fall, which would give about 0.036, and the error falls faster than the sample grows. The fourth halves it, which is what the earlier step from 5 to 21 did, not this one.
Why did the lecture choose odd sample sizes of 21 and 101?
With n odd the two counts can never be equal, so the poll always has a winner to name and the error event needs no special case. The sum is not shorter: n = 21 needs eleven terms where n = 20 needs eleven as well. The rate of decrease is governed by the sample size, not by its parity, and 20 and 21 sit next to each other on the same curve. And the threshold for odd n is (n - 1)/2, which is just below half rather than exactly half.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A polling company will accept a 5% chance of naming the wrong winner, in the same 60:40 election.

Estimate the smallest odd sample size that meets that bar, using only the curve in section 2, and then say what you would need in order to check your estimate exactly.

Hint

Find where the curve crosses the 0.05 grid line, and read n off the horizontal axis. The plotted points either side of the crossing are n = 51 at 0.0735 and n = 71 at 0.0438.

Show the worked solution
  1. Bracket the answer. The curve is above 0.05 at n = 51, where the error is 0.0735, and below it at n = 71, where the error is 0.0438. So the crossing sits between 51 and 71.
  2. Interpolate. The drop across those twenty people is 0.0735 − 0.0438 = 0.0297, and you need to lose 0.0735 − 0.05 = 0.0235 of it. That is 0.0235 ÷ 0.0297 = 0.79 of the way, so about 51 + 0.79 × 20 = 67 people.
  3. Round to an odd size. Call it 67, and treat it as an estimate rather than an answer.
  4. Say what an exact check needs. Evaluate P(X ≤ 33) for a Bin(67, 0.6), which is 0.0485, and then the size below it: P(X ≤ 32) for a Bin(65, 0.6) is 0.0510, which fails. So 67 is the smallest odd sample that clears the bar, and the estimate happened to land on it.

Sanity check. The answer must sit between 51 and 71, because those two points bracket the 0.05 line. It must also be at or below whatever the straight-line reading gives, because the curve is convex and a straight chord therefore sits above it, over-stating the error everywhere between the two plotted points. An estimate outside the bracket means a point was read from the wrong side.

Carry this away

  • The poll's error is F(⌊n/2⌋) and both the distribution and the threshold move with n, so each sample size is a fresh computation rather than a scaling of the last one.
  • 0.3174 at five people, 0.1744 at twenty-one, 0.0209 at a hundred and one: the gain is front-loaded, and cost is not, which is what makes a few hundred the usual answer.
  • A quoted probability is only as good as the n written beside it, and the two on this board belong to the even sample size one step down.

Your teacher. The most useful habit this page can leave you with is checking the n beside a probability before you trust the probability. It cost the lecture nothing to write 0.25 next to 21, the formula above it was correct, and the slip survived until a student evaluated the sum during class. If you take one number from a slide into a decision, evaluate it once yourself at the stated parameters. It takes a minute with any binomial routine, and it is the only thing that catches a right calculation attached to the wrong case.
