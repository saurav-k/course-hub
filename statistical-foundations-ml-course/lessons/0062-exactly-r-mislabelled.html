Lecture 5 · Part 4 of 10 · the general quality-check answer

Split the choice across the two pools and the general formula writes itself

10 min a named shape, unnamed on the board One question with a particular answer, then the same question with a letter in it, and a distribution the course has not formally met.

The one-minute version

  • For exactly two mislabelled images to be found, 2 must come from the 50 bad ones and the other 18 from the 950 good ones. Those are two independent choices, so their counts multiply.
  • Pr(X = 2) = C(50, 2) · C(950, 18) / C(1000, 20), exactly as the board writes it.
  • Replacing the 2 with a letter changes nothing in the argument: Pr(X = r) = C(50, r) · C(950, 20 − r) / C(1000, 20).
  • The class notes add a further reading: Pr(X ≥ 2) = 1 − (Pr(X = 0) + Pr(X = 1)), which is the same complement move as Part 3 with one more term taken off.
  • This shape has a standard name, the hypergeometric. Neither source uses the word, and this course has not met it formally: that comes with random variables.

1. Two pools, one choice made twice

The dataset is not one pool but two, and the question is what forces the split into the open. There are 50 mislabelled images and 950 correctly labelled ones, and an inspection set of twenty draws some from each.

Fix the answer we want: exactly two of the twenty are mislabelled. That single sentence fixes both halves at once. Two of the twenty come from the bad pool, and because the set has twenty images in all, the other eighteen must come from the good pool. There is no freedom left to choose.

  1. Choose which bad images. Two images out of the fifty mislabelled ones, unordered and distinct: C(50, 2) ways.
  2. Choose which good images. Eighteen images out of the 950 correctly labelled ones: C(950, 18) ways.
  3. Multiply the two stages. The basic counting rule applies because the number of ways to complete stage two does not depend on which images stage one picked: whichever two bad images were chosen, there are still 950 good ones to choose eighteen from. So the favourable count is C(50, 2) · C(950, 18).
  4. Divide by the whole space. Pr(X = 2) = C(50, 2) · C(950, 18) / C(1000, 20).

2. The general answer

Nothing in that argument used the number two. Replace it with a letter and the same four steps run unchanged, which is why the lecture states the general result immediately after the particular one.

Pr(X = r) = C(50, r) · C(950, 20 − r) / C(1000, 20) X is the number of mislabelled images the check finds, and r is the particular value being priced. C(50, r) counts which bad images are in the set, C(950, 20 − r) counts which good ones fill the remaining slots, and C(1000, 20) counts every inspection set the scientist could have drawn. The formula is only meaningful for r between 0 and 20, since the set holds twenty images and there are far more than twenty of each kind available.

Two sanity checks are worth running on any general formula before trusting it, and both pass here.

  1. Set r = 0. C(50, 0) is 1, because there is exactly one way to choose nothing, and C(950, 20 − 0) is C(950, 20). The formula collapses to Part 3's answer, as it must.
  2. Add every value up. Every inspection set contains some definite number of mislabelled images, from zero to twenty, and no set is counted under two different values of r. So the twenty-one probabilities partition the space and add to exactly one. This course has checked that sum and it comes to 1.

3. At least two, which the room asked for and the handout does not

The class notes carry a question the typeset handout does not: what is the probability that the check finds two or more mislabelled images? The answer is written as a complement, and it is worth seeing why this one needs two terms taken off rather than one.

Pr(X ≥ 2) = 1 − ( Pr(X = 0) + Pr(X = 1) ) X ≥ 2 means the check found two or more. The only ways for that to fail are finding exactly none and finding exactly one, and those two cases are disjoint, so their probabilities add. Subtracting the pair from one leaves everything else. The general formula supplies both terms: Pr(X = 0) = C(950, 20) / C(1000, 20) and Pr(X = 1) = C(50, 1) · C(950, 19) / C(1000, 20).

The pattern generalises: the complement of "at least k" is the sum of the k terms below it. On Part 3 that was one term, here it is two, and the cost of the complement route grows one term at a time while the cost of the direct route shrinks. Somewhere past the middle it stops being worth it.

This course's arithmeticNeither source evaluates any of these. Working the general formula for each r gives Pr(X = 0) = 0.3549, Pr(X = 1) = 0.3812, Pr(X = 2) = 0.1904, Pr(X = 3) = 0.0588 and Pr(X = 4) = 0.0126, with everything from r = 5 upwards adding to about 0.0022 in total. So the notes' own question comes to Pr(X ≥ 2) = 0.2640. Reading those figures in plain words: a twenty-image check of a five percent mislabelled dataset most often finds exactly one bad image, finds none about a third of the time, and finds three or more only about seven times in a hundred. None of these numbers is on the board and none carries a key-number mark.

4. The shape has a name, and the course has not met it yet

Look at the general formula again with the particular numbers stripped out. A population splits into two kinds. A fixed-size sample is drawn without replacement. The question is how many of one kind came out.

A name this course is supplying, not the lectureNeither the handout nor the class notes uses the word hypergeometric anywhere. This course names it here for one reason only: so that a reader who wants to look the shape up has the word to look it up with. Nothing else about the distribution is claimed on this page, because this course has not built the machinery to state it. Named distributions belong with random variables, which the source series' own plan puts a few sessions ahead of this one and which the course map carries as a roadmap entry. Until then the formula above is what you have, and it is complete: it answers the question for every r without needing a name at all.

It is worth being precise about what the name buys and what it does not. It buys recognition, so that the next time a problem describes a fixed draw from a two-kind population you write the ratio down immediately instead of rederiving it. It does not buy any new fact: everything on this page was obtained from the counting rule and the equally-likely formula, both of which are already on the site.

5. Check yourself

In Pr(X = 2), what does the factor C(950, 18) count?
The inspection set holds twenty images and two of them are fixed as mislabelled, so the remaining eighteen slots must be filled from the 950 correctly labelled images, and C(950, 18) counts exactly those choices. Leaving images out is not a separate choice: choosing eighteen to include is the same act as leaving 932 out, so counting it twice would be wrong. Order was discarded when the outcome was defined as a set, which is why a binomial coefficient appears rather than a falling product. And drawing eighteen from all thousand would allow more mislabelled images into the set and break the exactly-two condition.
The class notes write Pr(X at least 2) as one minus something. One minus what?
Two or more fails in exactly two disjoint ways, finding none and finding exactly one, so both terms come off and the answer is 1 minus 0.3549 minus 0.3812, which is 0.2640. Subtracting only the zero term answers a different question, at least one, which is Part 3 and comes to 0.6451. Doubling the exactly-two term corresponds to nothing at all, and the value being subtracted must be a probability of a complement, not a multiple of a term inside the event. And the twenty-bad-images case is inside the event being asked about, not outside it, so subtracting it would remove part of the answer.
Your teacher. The move worth stealing from this page is the sentence "fixing r leaves no choice about the rest". Whenever a problem splits a population into kinds and asks for a specific mix, write down what each stage is choosing and then check that the last stage has no freedom left. If it does have freedom left, you have missed a constraint and the counts will not multiply cleanly. That check takes five seconds and it catches the commonest error in this whole family of problems, which is multiplying two counts that were never independent stages.
