Counting problems are won or lost on what counts as one outcome

working 11 min Four practice problems from the unsubmitted half of Practice Set 1, each counted through to a number or a set.

After this page

  • You can fix what one outcome is before counting anything, and say when two descriptions name the same outcome.
  • You can price a uniform finite space three ways: directly, inside a conditioned world, and through a complement.

Before you start: the sample space, for what an outcome must resolve, and geometry and counting, for the uniform counting used throughout.

The one-minute version

  • All four turn on the same discipline: decide exactly what one outcome is before you count any of them.
  • Problem 2 is three counts over the 56 committees of three from eight: 3/8, then 2/7 inside Bob's world, then 9/14 for the union.
  • Problem 5 asks for a sample space and no probability. An outcome is the winner of every one of the 2k - 1 matches, not just the champion.
  • Problem 7 collapses rotations and gets 1/120; problem 22 goes through the complement and gets about 0.0082.

What decides each of the four

Problem 2 - Alice, Bob and the eight students

A delegation is a committee, not a lineup: the three picked students carry no order, so the space is the 56 unordered triples from eight. Part (b) is a fresh count inside a smaller world - the committees already containing Bob - and reusing part (a)'s number there is the classic slip.

Problem 5 - the knockout tournament's sample space

This problem reads like a probability question and is not one: the sheet asks for a sample space and nothing else, and its closing line that the elements need not be equally likely is permission you never have to use. An outcome must resolve every match, because the loser of round 1 is as random as the champion.

Problem 7 - six numbers on a circle

A circle has no first position, so "one outcome" is undefined until two conventions are fixed: which way round counts as reading order, and whether a rotated copy is a new placement. Fix both the standard way - one direction, rotations identified - and the count is the ordinary circular-arrangement count.

Honest gapThe course's counting pages have not yet worked arrangements around a circle. The rotation step below - dividing the 720 labelled-seat placements by the 6 rotations that say the same thing - is a symmetry move the lectures have not performed, so the solution shows it twice over and checks the two routes against each other.

Problem 22 - three birthdays and the word "some"

The sheet glosses "some" as "either two or three", which is not "exactly two": the three-way match belongs to the event. That makes the event "at least one collision", whose complement is the single clean pattern "all three days differ" - the standard route, and the one the counting below takes.

Sheet problem 2 - Alice, Bob, eight studentsfoundation

Alice and Bob are among a group of eight college students interested in debating. Three students are picked at random to represent the college at a fest. Let A be the event that Alice is picked and B the event that Bob is picked. Find (a) P(A), (b) P(A | B), and (c) P(A ∪ B).

Hint

Count committees, never lineups. For part (b), throw away every committee without Bob and recount from scratch.

Show the worked solution
  1. Size the space. An outcome is an unordered three-person committee from eight students, so there are C(8, 3) = (8 × 7 × 6) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 56 of them, all equally likely.
  2. (a) Committees with Alice. Fix Alice in one seat; the other two come from the remaining seven, so C(7, 2) = 21. Hence P(A) = 21 / 56 = 3/8.
  3. (b) Shrink to B. Committees containing Bob: fix Bob, choose two from the other seven, C(7, 2) = 21. Of those, the ones also containing Alice fix both and choose one from the remaining six: C(6, 1) = 6.
  4. (b) Divide. P(A | B) = 6 / 21 = 2/7. That is below 3/8, because knowing Bob is in uses up one of the three seats Alice was competing for.
  5. (c) Add and subtract the overlap. P(B) = 3/8 by the same count as (a). P(A ∩ B) = 6 / 56 = 3/28. The addition rule gives P(A ∪ B) = 21/56 + 21/56 - 6/56 = 36/56 = 9/14.

Sanity check. Count the complement of (c) directly: committees with neither Alice nor Bob are C(6, 3) = 20, so P(A ∪ B) = 1 - 20/56 = 36/56 = 9/14. The two routes agree. A second check on (a): each student occupies 3 of the 8 seats' worth of chances, so P(A) = 3/8 by symmetry alone, with no counting at all.

Sheet problem 5 - the tournament's sample spaceworking

Consider a knockout tournament with k rounds of matches, the final included; in each round half the remaining players are knocked out. Grand slam tennis tournaments start with 128 players and have seven rounds. Define a suitable sample space for the random outcome of the tournament. Each element of this sample space need not be equally likely.

Hint

Count the matches first. Then ask what a single random run must decide about each one of them.

Show the worked solution
  1. Size the field. Each round halves the field and the final leaves one player, so the tournament starts with 2k players. For k = 7 that is 128, which matches the sheet's example.
  2. Count the matches. Round i has 2k-i matches, for i = 1 to k. The total is 2k-1 + 2k-2 + ... + 1 = 2k - 1. Equivalently: every match eliminates exactly one player and all but the champion are eliminated.
  3. Fix the bracket. The draw pins which slots meet in each match, so the only thing chance still decides is who wins each match.
  4. Define one outcome. Number the matches 1 to 2k - 1 in bracket order and let ω = (w1, ..., wm) with m = 2k - 1, where wj = 1 if the upper slot of match j wins and 0 if the lower slot does.
  5. State the space. Ω = {0, 1}m with m = 2k - 1, so |Ω| = 2(2k - 1). Each ω determines every winner, every loser and the champion, and no two different tournaments give the same ω. Probabilities are not needed, and the elements are not assumed equally likely.

Sanity check. Put k = 1: two players, one match, Ω = {0, 1}, two outcomes - right. Put k = 2: four players, three matches, 23 = 8 outcomes, and listing them by hand gives eight distinct tournaments. For the sheet's k = 7 the space has 2127 elements, which is why it is described rather than listed. A candidate space whose elements name only the champion fails this check: it has 2k elements and cannot tell two different roads to the same title apart.

Sheet problem 7 - six numbers on a circleworking

The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 are randomly placed on a circle. What is the probability that they are placed in increasing order?

Hint

Two placements that differ only by turning the ring say the same thing. Divide them out, then ask how many of what is left read 1 to 6.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix the conventions. Read clockwise, and treat two placements as the same when one is a rotation of the other - a ring carries no first position, so this is what "a placement" means.
  2. Size the space. With rotations identified, six distinct numbers around a circle admit (6 - 1)! = 5! = 120 placements, all equally likely.
  3. Count the favourable ones. "Increasing clockwise" means the number following each one clockwise is the next integer, and after 6 comes 1. That fixes every neighbour, so exactly 1 of the 120 placements qualifies.
  4. Divide. P(increasing order) = 1 / 120 ≈ 0.0083.

Sanity check. Redo it without collapsing rotations: put the six numbers into six labelled seats, giving 6! = 720 equally likely placements. Of those, 6 read 1 to 6 clockwise - one for each choice of which seat holds the 1. So 6/720 = 1/120, the same answer, which shows the rotation convention cancels out rather than changing the verdict. Small case: three numbers on a circle give 2! = 2 placements and 1 increasing one, so 1/2 - and by hand, 1-2-3 clockwise and 1-3-2 clockwise are indeed the only two rings.

Sheet problem 22 - three shared birthdaysfoundation

Three friends are hanging out. Assume their birthdays are uniformly and independently distributed across the 365-day year. What is the probability that some - either two or three - of the people in the group have a birthday in common?

Hint

The event is "at least one collision". Its complement is one clean pattern, and one clean pattern is far easier to count.

Show the worked solution
  1. Size the space. Each friend's birthday is one of 365 days, independently, so an outcome is an ordered triple of days and there are 3653 = 48,627,125 of them, equally likely.
  2. Read the event. "Some (two or three) share" means at least one collision, so its complement is the single pattern "all three birthdays differ".
  3. Count the complement. The first friend may have any of 365 days, the second any of the remaining 364, the third any of the remaining 363: 365 × 364 × 363 = 48,228,180.
  4. Price the complement. P(all different) = (365 × 364 × 363) / 3653 = (364 × 363) / 3652 = 132,132 / 133,225 ≈ 0.99180.
  5. Subtract. P(some share) = 1 - 132,132 / 133,225 = 1,093 / 133,225 ≈ 0.0082, a little under one chance in 122.

Sanity check. Add the two collision patterns directly: exactly two share is 3 × 365 × 364 / 3653 = 1,092 / 133,225, and all three share is 365 / 3653 = 1 / 133,225. Those sum to 1,093 / 133,225, matching the complement route exactly. Note that answering "exactly two" instead would drop that final 1 - a real, if tiny, difference, and the reason the sheet glossed its own word.

Carry this away

  • Settle what one outcome is before counting: committee not lineup, whole tournament not champion, ring not seating. Every wrong answer on this page starts there.
  • Conditioning is recounting in a smaller world, which is why 3/8 becomes 2/7 rather than staying put.
  • "At least one" is a union of overlapping pieces, and its complement is usually a single clean pattern - one count instead of three.
