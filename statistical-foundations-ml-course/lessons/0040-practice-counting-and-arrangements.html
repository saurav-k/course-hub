The four problems, one at a time

Sheet problem 2 - Alice, Bob and the eight students

Restated in full. Alice and Bob are among a group of eight college students interested in debating. Three students are picked at random from the group to represent the college at an inter-collegiate fest. Event A is the event that Alice is among those picked, and event B is the event that Bob is among those picked. Find (a) P(A), (b) P(A | B), and (c) P(A ∪ B).

The symbols, named. The group has eight students and three seats. "Picked at random" means every three-person committee is equally likely - the uniform measure of the probability measure page, so every probability is a count of favourable committees over a count of all committees. A is the set of committees containing Alice; B is the set of committees containing Bob. P(A | B) is A's share of the shrunken world B, in the sense of the conditional probability page.

What it is really testing. Three things in one sitting: counting a uniform finite space (geometry and counting), conditioning as shrinking the world and recounting (conditional probability), and the addition rule for the union in part (c).

The traps. A delegation is a committee: the three picked students have no order, so counting ordered lineups counts every committee many times over. Part (b) is a fresh count in a shrunken world, not a reuse of part (a)'s number. And part (c)'s union means "at least one of the two is picked", which the addition rule prices only once the overlap - committees containing both - has its own count.

How to start. Write the sample space: all three-person committees from eight students, each equally likely. Count it with the counting machinery of the geometry-and-counting page. Then part (a) is committees containing Alice over all committees; part (b) shrinks the space to committees containing Bob and recounts Alice's share there; part (c) applies the addition rule, which needs the committees containing both Alice and Bob counted on their own. Every count is yours to do.

Sheet marks and difficulty. 1 mark per part, 3 in all. Routine - the classic slip is counting lineups instead of committees, and the second classic slip is answering (b) with (a)'s number.

Sheet problem 5 - the knockout tournament's sample space

Restated in full. Consider a knockout tournament with k rounds of matches, counting the final as one of the rounds. In each round, half of the remaining players are knocked out. The sheet recalls the example: grand slam tennis tournaments start with 128 players and have seven rounds of matches. The task: define a suitable sample space for the random outcome of the tournament. The sheet adds, kindly: each element of this sample space need not be equally likely.

The symbols, named. k is the number of rounds, final included. "Knocked out" means eliminated the moment a match is lost - there are no second chances. "The random outcome of the tournament" means everything that chance decides about how the whole event actually goes, not merely who wins.

What it is really testing. Whether the sample-space page's central instruction has landed: an outcome is a complete resolution of everything random. This is the one problem on the sheet that asks for the stage before probabilities, which is exactly why it is worth doing slowly.

Read the question twiceThis problem reads like a probability question - tournaments come wrapped in odds talk - but the sheet asks for a sample space and for nothing else. No probability is requested, no probability should be computed, and "need not be equally likely" is permission you never have to use. Answering the question that was not asked is the trap here.

How to start. Interrogate any candidate set with three questions. Does each element describe one complete way the randomness could resolve - pairings, every match's result, round after round? Are two tournaments that played out differently guaranteed two different elements? Does every element correspond to something that could actually happen under the knockout structure? A candidate that fails any of the three - for instance one whose elements name only the champion - is not yet suitable, and the failure itself tells you what the elements were missing.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Conceptually the most unusual ask on the sheet, but short once you see that the deliverable is a set, not a number.

Sheet problem 7 - six numbers on a circle

Restated in full. The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 are randomly placed on a circle. What is the probability that they are placed in increasing order?

The symbols, named. Six distinct numbers, six positions around one circle. "Randomly placed" carries its natural reading: every placement is equally likely. "In increasing order" means that reading the numbers around the circle in one fixed direction of travel yields 1, then 2, then 3, through to 6.

What it is really testing. The same discipline as problem 5, one page earlier in the sheet: the sample space must be settled before any counting. The geometry-and-counting page supplies the uniform counting machinery once the space is fixed.

The traps. A circle has no first position. "Increasing order" is not yet a fully defined event until two decisions are made: which direction counts as reading order, and - the bigger one - when two placements are the same placement. Rotate every number one position around the circle: is that a new outcome, or the same outcome seen from a different starting point? Defensible readings differ, and they lead to different counts of the space. This is not an annoyance; it is the problem. Second, smaller trap: "increasing" is strict, one number of each value, so do not let "non-decreasing" drift in.

Honest gapThe course's counting pages have not yet worked arrangements around a circle - the step where rotated copies of the same ring collapse into a single outcome is a symmetry move the lectures have not performed. The principle behind it is not new: it is the sample-space discipline of the sample-space page, with the uniform counting of the geometry-and-counting page on top. If the rotation step stalls you, the gap is in the course's coverage so far, not in your reading.

How to start. Settle the space first: fix a reading direction, decide whether rotated copies coincide, and count the placements on that decision. Then count the placements that read 1 through 6 in the chosen direction. Both counts, and the final division, are yours.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Short, but with a genuine conceptual trap; the counting itself is the easy part once the space is settled.

Sheet problem 22 - three birthdays and the word "some"

Restated in full. Three friends are hanging out. To keep things simple, assume their birthdays are uniformly and independently distributed across the 365-day year. What is the probability that some - the sheet glosses this immediately as "either two or three" - of the people in the group have a birthday in common?

The symbols, named. Three people; each person's birthday is uniform over 365 equally likely days, and the three birthdays are independent, which is what lets the multiplication rule page build the joint space from the three singles. The event of interest is the sheet's own phrase, "some (i.e. either two or three) have a birthday in common".

What it is really testing. Reading an event correctly and then counting ordered triples in a uniform space - the geometry-and-counting page again - with the complement rule from the axioms page as the standard shortcut for "at least one" questions.

The traps. The sheet's own gloss is the trap and the gift at once: "some (two or three)" is not the event "exactly two share". Exactly-two excludes the three-way match; the sheet's event includes it. Second, "at least one shared birthday" is a union of collision patterns, and unions whose pieces overlap are awkward to count head-on; the complement is one clean pattern, and "one minus the complement" is the standard move for exactly this shape. Third, "uniformly and independently" is doing real work - it is the licence to count ordered triples of days and nothing subtler.

How to start. Translate the event into plain English: at least two of the three birthdays land on the same day - equivalently, it is not the case that all three birthdays are different. Then count, with the multiplication rule: the whole space of ordered triples of days, and the complement in which all three days differ. The final assembly is one complement rule away, and it is yours to do.

Sheet marks and difficulty. No marks printed - practice section. Routine once the event is read correctly; the reading is the problem, the arithmetic is not.
