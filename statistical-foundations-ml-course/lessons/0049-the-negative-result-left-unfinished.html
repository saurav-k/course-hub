Lecture 4 · Part 4 of 12 · the reverse decomposition

The same disease can be cut by test result instead of by test result by disease

10 min an open line The notes write one more identity, aimed at the negative result, and then stop before evaluating it. This page shows both halves of that sentence.

The one-minute version

  • Total probability can be applied with the cut running the other way. Instead of slicing the alarm by whether the person is ill, slice the disease by what the test said.
  • The notes write P(D) = P(D ∩ +) + P(D ∩ −) = P(+) P(D | +) + P(−) P(D | −) and stop there. No number is evaluated for P(D | −).
  • This course finishes it, in a callout marked as its own derivation: P(D | −) = 1/9,406, about 0.000106.
  • A negative result divides the risk by 94.06, from one in a hundred to about one in nine thousand four hundred. The negative result on this test is far more trustworthy than the positive one, and for the same reason: the population is mostly well.

1. Cutting the same cake the other way

Part 2 partitioned by health status and asked how much of each slice tested positive. The partition was {D, Dc} and the event being priced was +.

Nothing forces that choice. The pair {+, −} is also a partition: every person gets exactly one test result, and never both. So the same law can price the event D instead.

P(D) = P(D ∩ +) + P(D ∩ −) = P(+) × P(D | +) + P(−) × P(D | −) The first equality splits the ill people into those the test flagged and those it did not, which is exhaustive and non-overlapping. The second rewrites each piece with the multiplication rule, conditioning on the test result: the size of the positive group times the share of it that is ill, plus the size of the negative group times the share of it that is ill.

This is the identity the notes write. Read as a sentence it says: all the disease in the population is either sitting inside the alarms or sitting inside the all-clears, and there is nowhere else for it to be.

2. Where the disease actually sits

Before touching the unknown, look at the two pieces the identity names. Both are already known from Part 2.

  1. Disease inside the alarms. P(D ∩ +) = P(D) × P(+ | D) = 0.01 × 0.99 = 0.0099. In ten thousand people, 99 of them.
  2. Disease inside the all-clears. P(D ∩ −) = P(D) × P(− | D) = 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001. In ten thousand people, 1 person.
  3. Check the identity. 0.0099 + 0.0001 = 0.01 = P(D). The two pieces do reassemble into the prevalence, which is what the notes' first equality asserts.

3. Where the lecture stops

The lecture writes the identity and does not evaluate itThe notes reach P(D) = P(+) P(D | +) + P(−) P(D | −) and end there. There is no number for P(D | −) anywhere on the board, and no key figure for it exists to quote. Anything numerical about the negative result on this page is therefore this course's derivation, not the lecture's, and it is kept in its own callout below so the two can never be confused. The reasoning behind handling it this way rather than either hiding the gap or silently finishing the lecturer's arithmetic is recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md, which follows the pattern set by learning-records/0001-quoting-a-deck-that-does-not-recompute.md.

It is worth saying why an unfinished line is normal rather than a mistake. The identity is the teaching point: it shows that total probability is symmetric in its partitions, and that the same population supports two different cuts. Evaluating it is bookkeeping the lecturer can leave to the reader, and a lecture that stopped to do every division would cover a third of the material. The defect would be a course that quietly did the division and printed the answer as though the lecturer had said it.

4. Finishing it, under this course's own name

This course's derivationEverything in this section is worked from the lecture's stated figures using the lecture's own identity. None of it is a quoted result. Two independent routes are shown, and they agree, which is the only check available when there is no stated answer to compare against.

Route one: read it off the table. Part 2 built a table of ten thousand people. The negative column holds 1 + 9,405 = 9,406 people, of whom exactly 1 is ill. So the share of the all-clear group that is ill is 1 in 9,406.

Route two: solve the notes' identity. Every other term in it is known, so the unknown can be isolated.

  1. The size of the negative group. The events + and − are complements, so P(−) = 1 − P(+) = 1 − 0.0594 = 0.9406.
  2. Substitute into the identity. 0.01 = 0.0594 × (1/6) + 0.9406 × P(D | −).
  3. Evaluate the known product. 0.0594 × (1/6) = 0.0099, which is the disease sitting inside the alarms, as expected.
  4. Isolate the unknown. 0.9406 × P(D | −) = 0.01 − 0.0099 = 0.0001.
  5. Divide. P(D | −) = 0.0001 ÷ 0.9406. Multiplying top and bottom by 10,000 gives 1 / 9,406, which is route one exactly.
  6. As a decimal. 1 ÷ 9,406 ≈ 0.000106, or roughly one in nine thousand four hundred.

The two routes agreeing is not a coincidence and it is not proof of anything deep: they are the same arithmetic carried out in different units. It is still worth doing both, because a single unchecked derivation of a number nobody stated is exactly the kind of thing that goes wrong quietly.

5. The three beliefs, on one scale

Now all three numbers exist: the belief before any test, the belief after a positive, and the belief after a negative. They span four orders of magnitude, so an ordinary scale cannot show them together and a logarithmic one is used instead: each step to the right is ten times the risk.

The engineering version of this pageA monitoring system with a sensitive alert has the same arithmetic. The alert firing barely shifts your belief that anything is wrong, because most of the time nothing is. The alert not firing is the strong signal, and it is the one nobody looks at. If an on-call rotation feels like it is mostly noise, that is not necessarily a badly tuned detector: it may be a correctly tuned detector pointed at a population where the failure is rare, and the fix is a cheap second check on the flagged cases rather than a less sensitive first one.

6. Check yourself

What does the identity P(D) = P(+)P(D | +) + P(−)P(D | −) assert about the population?
It is a partition statement: the ill people split into the flagged and the missed, with no overlap and nobody left out, so their two shares add back to the prevalence. How often the test agrees with the truth is a separate calculation involving all four cells. Independence would mean P(D and +) equals P(D) times P(+), which here is 0.0099 against 0.000594 and is emphatically false. And nothing in the identity compares the prevalence with the sensitivity; the two are not even measured on the same population.
Why is the all-clear on this test so much more conclusive than the alarm?
The negative group holds 9,405 correctly cleared people against a single missed case, so the ill share of it is minuscule; the same overwhelming well population that ruins the alarm is what makes the all-clear trustworthy. Comparing specificity with prevalence puts two quantities from different populations side by side and concludes nothing. The low miss rate helps but is not sufficient on its own - a common disease with the same miss rate would leave a far riskier all-clear. And counting negatives against positives says nothing about what is inside either group.
Your teacher. The habit to take from this page is not the number, it is the pause. When a lecture, a paper or a colleague sets up an identity and then moves on, notice it and finish it yourself on paper, with the answer clearly labelled as yours. Two things come out of that habit: you find out whether you actually could, and you never end up quoting your own arithmetic back at somebody as though it were theirs.
