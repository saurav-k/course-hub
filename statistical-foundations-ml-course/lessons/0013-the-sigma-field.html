Three axioms decide which sets count as events

working 10 min The sigma-field F is the collection of events from Part 3 with its membership rules written down: three axioms, each drawn, each earning its keep.

The one-minute version

  • A sigma-field, written F and also called a sigma-algebra, is a collection of subsets of Ω obeying three axioms.
  • The axioms: the whole space Ω is in F; the complement of a member is a member; the union of countably many members is a member.
  • Each axiom buys something: the empty set, intersections for free through De Morgan, and differences.
  • Closure is countable, not merely finite, because the deck's own events on infinite spaces are unions of infinitely many outcomes.

1. The definition, one axiom at a time

F is a sigma-field on Ω when: Read as: F, the collection whose members are the events, is a sigma-field if the three axioms below hold. The prefix sigma is the same sigma as the summation sign: it signals that unions of a sequence of sets are allowed, not just pairs.

Axiom 1: Ω is in F. The whole space is itself an event. The picture is barely a picture, which is the point: the box is admitted as a member of the collection, so "something happened" is a legal question.

Axiom 2: if A is in F, so is Ac. Complement closure. Whenever a question is legal, its negation is legal too.

Axiom 3: the union of countably many members of F is in F. If E1, E2, E3, ... is a sequence of events, then their union E1 ∪ E2 ∪ E3 ∪ ... is an event. The deck states this for two sets, A and B events make A ∪ B an event; the sequence version is what the deck's own examples actually use, and the callout below says so plainly.

Where the deck is looseThe deck's homework states closure for two sets: A and B events make A ∪ B an event. But the deck's own worked events, "at least five tosses" and "the count is odd", are unions of infinitely many single-outcome sets, and its probability axioms in Part 5 sum over a sequence of disjoint events. So the closure the deck actually uses is the countable one, and the standard definition - the one this page teaches - states it that way. Nothing in the deck contradicts this; it simply compresses, and the compression is worth naming because the word "sigma" lives exactly in that gap.

2. What each axiom buys

The three axioms look thin. They are not, because the Part 1 laws let each one manufacture more than it seems to. Here is the shopping list, item by item.

The empty set, free with axiom 2

Intersections, free with axiom 2 plus De Morgan

The axioms never mention intersections, yet F is closed under them. The Part 1 De Morgan law does the manufacturing: A ∩ B = (Ac ∪ Bc)c. Both complements are in F by axiom 2, their union is in F by axiom 3, and the complement of that union is in F by axiom 2 again.

Differences, free with axiom 2 plus an intersection

Set difference never appears in the axioms either, and it does not need to: A \ B = A ∩ Bc. Complement Bc by axiom 2, then intersect by the De Morgan trick just proved.

3. Why countable closure, and not just finite?

Take the deck's own event on the toss-until-heads space: "at least five tosses", E1 = {5, 6, 7, ...}. Each single toss-count {5}, {6}, {7}, ... is an event, and E1 is their union. There are infinitely many of them, so no finite run of union operations builds E1. If closure stopped at finite unions, the deck's own question would fall outside F and have no probability. The same applies to "the count is odd", E2 = {1, 3, 5, 7, ...}.

The design decision, in one sentenceThe axioms are not a random list; they are the smallest membership rule that keeps every question you can phrase with and, or, and not - over any countable run - inside the collection. Part 5's measure will then assign a probability to every member of F and to nothing else, and the whole theory stands on that division of labour.

Check yourself

A sigma-field F contains event A. Which other membership does axiom 2 guarantee outright?
Axiom 2 is complement closure: A in F forces Aᶜ into F, nothing else named. The union needs axiom 3 and a B that is itself in F. The difference needs axiom 2 plus a De Morgan intersection, and B in F besides. And F need not contain single outcomes at all: closure works downward from members, never upward into parts.
Why is closure under countable unions required rather than just finite ones?
The deck's own E₁ = {5, 6, 7, ...} is the union of infinitely many single-outcome events, so finite closure would leave it outside F with no probability. Diagrams are a teaching aid, not a constraint on axioms. Nothing here counts outcomes; the measure in Part 5 weighs them. Two-event unions are exactly what finite closure does allow.
The axioms mention only complements and unions. Where does A ∩ B come from?
A ∩ B = (Aᶜ ∪ Bᶜ)ᶜ, and each step is an axiom: complement, union, complement. No extra axiom is needed, which is the economy of the design. Not every subset is in F - Part 3's warning about uncountable spaces says exactly that. And intersections of disjoint sets exist fine; they are just empty, and the empty set is itself a member.
Your teacher. The reusable habit here is "what does the rule buy me?". When you meet any three-rule system - an access policy, a type system, a schema constraint - ask what each rule manufactures beyond its literal text, the way axiom 2 plus De Morgan manufactures intersections. If a rule manufactures nothing, it is a candidate for deletion, and if a needed operation is manufactured by nothing, the rule set is incomplete. The sigma-field passes both tests.
