The Cauchy is symmetric about zero and still has no mean

working 11 min Four expectations came out clean because their integrals converged. This one does not, and the notes stop with three words: expectation E[X] undefined.

After this page

  • You can split the Cauchy expectation at zero and show each half diverging, with the substitution written out.
  • You can say why symmetry does not rescue the answer, and why "infinity minus infinity" is not a number that can be set to zero.
  • You can name what a heavy tail does to a sample average, and recognise the failure in a running average that will not settle.

Before you start: Four expectations worked in full, for the integral definition this page is about to break.

The one-minute version

  • The Cauchy density is fX(x) = 1 / (π(1 + x2)) for every real x. It is a smooth bell shape, centred on zero, symmetric.
  • Its expectation integral splits into a right half and a left half. The right half is plus infinity, the left half is minus infinity.
  • Symmetry is not enough. The definition asks for a number, and the two halves do not produce one to add together.
  • Infinity minus infinity is not a number. It is not zero, it is not large, it is not anything: the subtraction is undefined, so the mean is undefined.
  • The cause is the tail. The density falls off like 1/x2, so x fX(x) falls off like 1/x, and the area under 1/x is famously infinite.
  • In practice this shows up as a sample average that never settles, however many draws you take.

1. The picture first: a bell with the wrong tails

Draw the Cauchy beside a standard Gaussian and the middles look like cousins. Both peak at zero, both are symmetric, both are smooth. The disagreement is entirely out in the tails, and it is not a small disagreement.

This course's arithmetic, not the lecture's. The notes state no numbers anywhere. Evaluating the two densities at x = 4 gives 0.01872 for the Cauchy and 0.00013383 for the standard Gaussian, a ratio of about 140. At x = 5 the values are 0.01224 and 0.00000149, a ratio of about 8,200. Every decimal on this page is this course's own arithmetic on the lecture's own formula.

2. The mechanism, with every symbol named

fX(x) = 1 / (π(1 + x2)), for x in R X is the random quantity. x is any real value it might take, and R means the whole real line, so nothing is excluded on either side. fX is the density: not a probability, but the height whose area over an interval gives the probability of landing in that interval. π is the ordinary constant 3.14159, and it is there only to make the total area equal one, because the area under 1/(1 + x2) across the whole line is exactly π.
E[X] = ∫−∞∞ x fX(x) dx The continuous definition from Part 1. E[X] is the expectation. Inside the integral, x is the value being averaged and fX(x) dx is the weight it carries, so the whole expression is "value times weight, accumulated across every value". dx is the infinitesimal slice of the axis. The two infinity limits are what this page is about to look at closely.

The notes do not attack that integral in one piece. They split it at zero, which is the standard move whenever the limits are infinite in both directions, and it is also the move that exposes the problem.

E[X] = ∫0∞ x/(π(1 + x2)) dx + ∫−∞0 x/(π(1 + x2)) dx The first term accumulates the positive values, the second the negative ones. Both must be finite numbers for their sum to mean anything, and that requirement is the whole content of this page.

3. The right half, worked with nothing skipped

  1. Take the right half alone. I+ = ∫0∞ x/(π(1 + x2)) dx. Every value here is positive and every weight is positive, so whatever this is, it is not negative.
  2. Substitute. Let t = 1 + x2. This is the notes' own substitution, chosen because the numerator already carries an x, which is exactly what the derivative of x2 produces.
  3. Differentiate the substitution. dt/dx = 2x, so dt = 2x dx, so x dx = dt/2. The awkward x in the numerator has been absorbed into the new variable.
  4. Move the limits. When x = 0, t = 1 + 0 = 1. As x grows without bound, so does t. The integral now runs from 1 to infinity, not from 0 to infinity.
  5. Rewrite. I+ = (1/π) ∫1∞ (1/t) · (dt/2) = (1/2π) ∫1∞ dt/t. The 1/2 comes from step 3 and the 1/π was always outside.
  6. Integrate. The antiderivative of 1/t is ln t, so I+ = (1/2π) [ln t]1∞.
  7. Evaluate. ln 1 = 0, and ln t grows without bound as t does. So I+ = (1/2π)(∞ − 0) = ∞. The right half of the expectation is plus infinity.
  8. Do the left half by symmetry. The density satisfies fX(−x) = fX(x), so the left half is the mirror of the right with the sign of the value flipped. It is minus infinity, which is what the notes mark beside it.
  9. Try to add them. E[X] = ∞ + (−∞). There is nothing to compute. The notes write the conclusion in three words: expectation E[X] undefined.

Step 7 is worth pausing on. The divergence is logarithmic, which is the slowest divergence there is, and that is why the failure is easy to miss in practice. It diverges all the same.

How slowly it runs away. Cutting the integral off at a finite ceiling M gives ln(1 + M2) / 2π. At M = 10 that is 0.735. Multiply the ceiling by a hundred, to M = 1000, and it is only 2.199. Push it to a million and it is 4.398; to a trillion, 8.795. Every factor of a hundred in the ceiling buys about 1.466 more. It never stops climbing and it never arrives, which is exactly what divergence looks like from the inside. This course's arithmetic.

4. Why symmetry does not rescue it

The obvious objection: the picture is symmetric, so surely the answer is zero. That reasoning is doing something the definition does not permit, and naming it precisely is the point of this page.

Setting the answer to zero means pairing the value +x with the value −x, cancelling them, and taking the limit with the two cut-offs moving outward together. That is a different quantity, called a principal value, and it depends on the choice to move both edges at the same rate. Move the right edge to 2M while the left edge sits at −M and the same cancellation delivers ln 2 / 2π instead, about 0.110. Move it to M2 and the leftover grows without bound. A quantity that changes when you change the bookkeeping is not the expectation of anything.

Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis close this door in a footnote in Section 2.4 of Introduction to Probability: the expectation is well defined only when the sum of |x| against its weights is finite. Absolute values first, cancellation never. For the Cauchy that absolute-value integral is I+ counted twice, so it is infinite, and the test fails before symmetry gets a turn.

The notes assert the left half and never work it. Page 3 evaluates the right half in full, with the substitution and the logarithm, and then simply writes minus infinity beside the left half. That step is genuinely symmetric and the mirror argument is sound, but it is asserted rather than shown. If you want it worked: substitute u = −x in the left integral, which turns it into −∫0∞ u/(π(1 + u2)) du = −I+, and step 7 has already shown I+ is infinite. The notes also stop at the word undefined and offer no reading of what that means for a practitioner, which is what section 5 supplies under this course's own name.

5. What an undefined mean does to real data

The law of large numbers is the reason averaging works: take more draws, and the sample average closes in on the true mean. It has a hypothesis, and the hypothesis is that the true mean exists. Remove it and averaging stops converging to anything.

You can watch this happen. The chart below runs 2000 draws from the Cauchy and 2000 from a standard Gaussian, and plots each running average as the draws accumulate.

The figures behind that chart, all this course's own. Two thousand draws of each shape from one fixed simulation. From draw 400 to draw 1746 the Cauchy running average stays inside ±0.5 of zero. Draw 1747 is −23,763, and it drops the running average from +0.17 to −13.43 in one step. The remaining 253 draws lift it only to −11.84. The Gaussian running average, over the same 2000 draws, stays within 0.055 of zero from draw 100 onward and never leaves. Nothing here is a quoted figure; the notes contain no simulation and no decimals.

Two practical readings follow. First, a heavy-tailed sample can look well behaved for a long time and then not be, so a stable-looking running average is evidence of nothing on its own. Second, the Cauchy is not centreless: its median is zero, cleanly and robustly, and so is its mode. What fails is the expectation specifically, because expectation weights values by size and this distribution supplies unboundedly large ones often enough to matter.

6. What the class was told, and why this page says something stronger

The Cauchy surfaced in the delivered session too, but only in passing. A student asked whether an expected value must land inside the range of the variable, and whether it can be infinite when that range is the whole real line. The lecturer answered that it can, named the Cauchy as an example of a heavy-tailed distribution used in practice, and said that for that random variable it turns out that the expected value itself can also be infinite. Then he moved on.

Infinite and undefined are not the same verdict, and the class was given the looser wordThe sentence said aloud was a one-line aside in an answer to a different question, and it is loose in the direction that matters here. An infinite mean, which is Part 4, means one half of the integral diverges and the other is finite, so +∞ is a genuine answer. The Cauchy is worse than that: both halves diverge, in opposite directions, and there is no answer at all. The lecturer's own written notes are the stricter authority and say so, ending the block with the words Expectation E[X] undefined. This page follows the notes rather than the aside, and names the gap rather than quietly choosing a side.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the Cauchy expectation undefined rather than equal to zero?
Both halves diverge, so there are no two numbers to add, and infinity minus infinity is not a number. The first option is false: the density is symmetric, and symmetry is exactly what tempts people into the wrong answer of zero. The third is false: the density peaks at a perfectly finite 1/pi, about 0.318. The fourth is false: the pi in the denominator is there precisely to make the total area one.
In the simulation, what threw the Cauchy running average off at draw 1747?
One draw of about minus 23,763 moved the average from +0.17 to minus 13.43 by itself, which is what a heavy tail does. The first option describes a moving centre, but the density never changes and stays symmetric throughout. The second would move the average gradually rather than in one step, and the chart shows a vertical drop. The fourth is not the cause: the arithmetic is exact enough, and repeating the simulation produces the same kind of jump at a different draw.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An engineer proposes to sidestep the problem by truncating: compute the Cauchy mean over [−M, M] for a large but finite M, and call that the answer.

Find the right-half integral up to M, evaluate it at M = 10 and M = 1000, and say what the two values show about the proposal.

Hint

Reuse the substitution from step 2. The only thing that changes is the upper limit, which becomes 1 + M2 instead of infinity. You need ln 101 = 4.615 and ln 1000001 = 13.816.

Show the worked solution
  1. Set up the truncated half. I+(M) = ∫0M x/(π(1 + x2)) dx.
  2. Substitute as before. With t = 1 + x2 and x dx = dt/2, the limits become t = 1 and t = 1 + M2.
  3. Integrate. I+(M) = (1/2π) [ln t]11+M2 = ln(1 + M2) / 2π, since ln 1 = 0.
  4. Evaluate at M = 10. ln 101 = 4.615, and 2π = 6.283, so I+(10) = 4.615/6.283 = 0.735.
  5. Evaluate at M = 1000. ln 1000001 = 13.816, so I+(1000) = 13.816/6.283 = 2.199.
  6. Read the truncated mean. Over a symmetric window the left half is exactly minus the right half, so the truncated mean is 0 for every M. The zero is manufactured by the choice of window, not found in the distribution.
  7. Say what it shows. The half-integral has grown by a factor of three while the window grew by a factor of a hundred, and it will keep growing forever. The proposal reports zero with a straight face while the quantity it is discarding is unbounded, so the truncation is hiding the failure rather than fixing it.

Sanity check. Both half-integrals must be positive and must increase with M, since the integrand is positive throughout. The ratio also has to be modest: going from M = 10 to M = 1000 multiplies 1 + M2 by about ten thousand, and the logarithm turns that into roughly ln 10000 / 2π = 1.466 of extra area, which matches 2.199 minus 0.735 = 1.464 to rounding.

Carry this away

  • Split an expectation with infinite limits at zero, and require each half to be finite before adding anything.
  • For the Cauchy the substitution t = 1 + x2 turns the right half into the area under 1/t, which is infinite; the left half mirrors it.
  • Infinity minus infinity is not a number, so symmetry cannot deliver a mean. A cancelling limit gives a different value for a different bookkeeping, which is how you know it is not the expectation.
  • An undefined mean shows up in data as a sample average that never settles, however many draws you add.

Your teacher. If step 9 still feels like a technicality, put the two failures side by side and say them out loud: the Pareto on the next page answers "how large is the average" with "unboundedly large", which is informative; the Cauchy answers with nothing at all. Then take the strongest version of the objection seriously - the picture really is symmetric - and locate exactly where it breaks, which is the moment you decide to cancel +x against −x at matched rates. Ask an agent to run the same running average for a Cauchy a few times with different starting seeds; the jump lands at a different draw every time, and never stops arriving.
