One outlier, and the average stops describing anything

10 min first real arithmetic Ten days of spend, two summaries of the same ten numbers, and an answer that differs by a factor of three.

The one-minute version

  • A fintech app must set a daily credit limit for a user with no credit history, using ten days of UPI transaction logs.
  • Nine of the ten days are boringly similar. Day 5 is Rs 8,500, roughly twenty times the rest.
  • The mean of the ten days is 1,225. The median is 415. Both are correct arithmetic on the same data.
  • The mean is higher than nine of the ten days it claims to summarise. That is the whole lesson.

1. The problem, in business terms

Slide 5 sets up the case the next three pages develop. A fintech app has to decide a daily credit limit of Rs 10,000 for a "new-to-credit" user, meaning somebody with no credit bureau history at all. The only evidence available is ten days of UPI transaction logs, which the app is using as a proxy for how much cash this person actually moves in a day.

Here is the raw data exactly as the deck gives it.

Day12345678910
Spend (Rs)4003505004208,500380410440390460

Read that row once as a person rather than as a statistician. Nine days look like somebody buying groceries and paying for transport. One day looks like somebody buying a phone. Those are not the same behaviour, and no single number is going to describe both of them well. The question is which single number is least wrong.

2. The arithmetic mean, worked in full

The arithmetic mean, written x̄ and read aloud as "x bar", is the total divided by the count. In symbols, with xᵢ standing for the spend on day i:

x̄ = ( ∑ xᵢ ) / 10 The symbol ∑ ("sigma") means "add up everything that follows". So ∑ xᵢ is just "add the ten daily figures together". The 10 underneath is how many figures there were.
  1. Add the ten numbers. Running the total in order: 400, then 750, then 1,250, then 1,670, then 10,170 after day 5, then 10,550, then 10,960, then 11,400, then 11,790, and finally 12,250.
  2. Notice where the total came from. Day 5 alone contributed 8,500 of that 12,250. One day out of ten is carrying about 69 percent of the total.
  3. Divide by the count. 12,250 ÷ 10 = 1,225.

So the mean daily spend is Rs 1,225. Now hold that against the raw data. There is no day on which this user spent anything close to Rs 1,225. The nearest actual day is Rs 500. The mean is not describing a day that happened; it is describing a day that would have happened if the Rs 8,500 had been smeared evenly across the whole ten days.

3. The median, worked in full

The median, written x̃ and read aloud as "x tilde", is the middle value once the numbers are put in order. Ordering is the entire mechanism, which is exactly why it behaves differently.

  1. Sort the ten values from smallest to largest. That gives [350, 380, 390, 400, 410, 420, 440, 460, 500, 8,500].
  2. Find the middle. With ten values there is no single middle position, because ten is even. The two central positions are the 5th and the 6th, holding 410 and 420.
  3. Average the two central values. (410 + 420) ÷ 2 = 830 ÷ 2 = 415.

Now look at what the sort did to Rs 8,500. It went to the far right of the list and became "the largest one". Its size stopped mattering the moment it was placed. If day 5 had been Rs 85,000 instead of Rs 8,500, the sorted list would be identical apart from that last entry, the 5th and 6th values would still be 410 and 420, and the median would still be exactly 415.

4. The two recipes, side by side

It is worth seeing the two procedures written out as procedures, because the difference in behaviour comes entirely from the difference in mechanism, and not from anything subtle.

Do not delete the outlierNothing on this page says Rs 8,500 was a mistake or should be thrown away. It is a real day, and for some questions it is the most important day in the dataset. A fraud team would care about it far more than about the other nine. The claim is narrower and more useful than "drop outliers": if the question is "what does an ordinary day cost this person", then a summary that a single unusual day can move by a factor of three is answering a different question. Choose the summary that matches the question, and keep the raw data for the other questions.

5. Check yourself

Which of these changes to the ten days would move the mean but leave the median exactly where it is?
Raising the largest value keeps it largest, so the sorted list is unchanged in positions five and six and the median stays at 415, while the total and therefore the mean climb steeply. Lowering all ten values pulls both summaries down together. Shuffling changes neither, because the mean does not care about order and the median sorts anyway. Adding 100 to every day shifts both summaries by exactly 100.
The credit team wants a number that describes what this user's normal day costs. Why is the median the better choice here?
The median lands at 415, which is genuinely typical of nine of the ten days, so it answers the question that was asked. Speed is not the reason: the median needs a sort, so it is arguably the slower of the two. It does not always land below the mean either, and on data with no outlier the two summaries nearly coincide; it happens to be smaller here because the one outlier is on the high side. And using every data point is the mean's property, not the median's, which is precisely why the mean is vulnerable.
Your teacher. Try this on your own data before moving on. Take any metric you own at work that gets reported as an average, pull the raw distribution for one week, and compute the median next to it. If the two numbers are close, the average was safe. If they are far apart, you have just found out that your dashboard has been describing a day that never happened.
