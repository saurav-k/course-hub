Being told one particular image went wrong uses up an error, and that changes everything downstream

9 min set up on the board, finished here A question the typeset handout does not contain, asked live and left at its first line.

The one-minute version

  • The extra question from the room: what is the probability of exactly three errors, given that the second image was wrongly classified?
  • The notes write it as a ratio, Pr(3 errors ∩ 2nd image wrong) / Pr(2nd image wrong), and evaluate the denominator as 0.2.
  • The numerator is set up with a strip diagram and never evaluated. This course finishes it, marked as this course's own arithmetic, and checks the result by a second route.
  • Fixing one error position leaves two errors to place among the other nine images, so the numerator is C(9, 2)(0.2)3(0.8)7 and the answer is C(9, 2)(0.2)2(0.8)7 = 0.3020.
  • Against the unconditional 0.2013 from Part 5, that is exactly one and a half times as likely. Knowing about one error makes three errors in total more plausible, not less.

1. The question, and where the notes stop

The lecturer added a question to the classification-error problem that is not in the typeset handout. It reads: given that the second test image is wrongly classified, what is the probability that the classifier makes exactly three errors in all?

The notes write the definition of a conditional probability, evaluate the denominator, and draw the strip for the numerator.

Pr(3 errors | 2nd image wrong) = Pr(3 errors ∩ 2nd image wrong) / Pr(2nd image wrong) The vertical bar is read "given". The numerator is the probability that both things happen: the total is exactly three errors and the second image is one of them. The denominator is the probability of the thing being conditioned on, taken alone. This is Lecture 3, Part 4's definition with no modification at all.

The denominator is immediate. Every image is misclassified with probability 0.2 and nothing about the other nine affects that, so Pr(2nd image wrong) = 0.2. The notes write that number beside the denominator with an arrow.

The notes set the numerator up and stopBelow the ratio the class notes draw a ten-position strip with the second slot marked E and the remaining nine left blank, and an arrow pointing down from it. That is the set-up for counting the numerator, and it is where the page ends: no count is written and no value is computed. Everything in sections 2 and 3 below is therefore this course's own arithmetic and carries no key-number mark. The policy followed here, including working the result by two independent routes before publishing it, is recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md.

2. Finishing the numerator

The strip is the whole idea. Ten positions, one of them already decided. Count what is left free.

  1. Name the joint event. Exactly three errors, one of which is at position two. So position two holds an E, and exactly two of the other nine positions hold an E as well.
  2. Count the patterns. Choosing which two of the remaining nine positions carry errors is case 3: C(9, 2) = (9 × 8) / 2 = 36.
  3. Price one of them. Each still has three errors and seven correct results, so each is worth (0.2)3(0.8)7 = 0.0016777216, exactly as on Part 5.
  4. Multiply, using Part 7's rule. Pr(3 errors and 2nd image wrong) = 36 × 0.0016777216 = 0.0603979776.
  5. Divide by the denominator. 0.0603979776 / 0.2 = 0.3019898880, which is 0.3020 to four places.
Pr(3 errors | 2nd image wrong) = C(9, 2) (0.2)2(0.8)7 Dividing by 0.2 removes one factor of 0.2 from the numerator, which is why the exponent on 0.2 drops from three to two while the exponent on 0.8 stays at seven. C(9, 2) counts where the two remaining errors go among the nine images not mentioned in the condition. This whole line is this course's own derivation: the notes stop before it.

3. The same answer by a second route

An unstated result should not be published on one derivation alone, so here is a completely different argument that must land on the same number if the first one is right.

The errors on different images are independent. So being told what happened to image two says nothing whatsoever about images one and three to ten: those nine are still a run of nine independent trials, each going wrong with probability 0.2.

Given that image two is already an error, "three errors in total" means exactly two errors among those nine. That is a binomial question with n = 9, k = 2 and p = 0.2, and Part 5's formula answers it directly.

This course's derivationBoth routes give Pr(3 errors | 2nd image wrong) = C(9, 2)(0.2)2(0.8)7 = 36 × 0.04 × 0.2097152 = 0.3020 to four places. The notes state the ratio and the denominator, and nothing else. Nothing in this callout is on the board, so nothing here carries a key-number mark.

4. What the condition actually did

Compare the two answers. Unconditionally, exactly three errors has probability 0.2013. Given that the second image is wrong, it has probability 0.3020, which is exactly one and a half times as large.

It is worth being clear that the condition has not made the classifier worse. The error rate is still 0.2 on every image, including the nine we know nothing about. What changed is our position: we are no longer asking the classifier to produce three errors out of nothing, we are asking it to produce two more on top of one we have been given.

The condition does not raise every answer either. Given that image two is wrong, the probability of zero errors is exactly zero, and the probability of exactly one error falls from 0.2684 to 0.1342. Conditioning redistributes probability; it does not add any.

5. Check yourself

Why is the numerator built on C(9, 2) rather than on C(10, 3)?
The condition nails an error to position two, so only two errors remain to be placed and only nine positions remain to place them in, giving C(9, 2) = 36 rather than C(10, 3) = 120. The second image is not removed from the evaluation: it is still one of the ten and still contributes a factor of 0.2 to every pattern's weight. There is no general rule that a conditional divides a count by anything; the division here is by a probability, 0.2, and it happens after the count. And the question asks about three errors in total, not two, with the third supplied by the condition.
Why does knowing image two is wrong make exactly three errors more likely?
The condition supplies one error for free, so chance only has to produce two more instead of three, and two is easier than three. The classifier's behaviour on the other nine images is completely unaffected, because the errors are independent by assumption. No probability rises to 0.3: the 0.3020 is the answer to the question, not an error rate, and every image is still wrong with probability 0.2. And conditioning does not always make things more likely: this same condition drops the probability of exactly one error from 0.2684 to 0.1342 and the probability of zero errors to zero.
Your teacher. Whenever a condition fixes a particular trial's outcome in a run of independent trials, try the second route first: cross out the trial you were told about, restate the question about what is left, and answer that. Here it turns a conditional probability into a plain binomial with n = 9. That route is shorter, it is harder to get wrong, and it makes the answer feel inevitable rather than computed. Keep the ratio definition as the check, which is exactly how it is used on this page.
