Lecture 4 · Part 1 of 12 · the medical-test set-up

A test's numbers describe the test, not the person holding the result

10 min foundation Three numbers arrive with the example, two more follow for free, and every one of them conditions on something the patient cannot see.

The one-minute version

  • A disease D is present in 1 percent of a population. That figure is the prevalence, and in probability notation it is P(D) = 0.01.
  • A test T catches 99 percent of the people who have the disease. That is the sensitivity, written P(+ | D) = 0.99.
  • The same test clears 95 percent of the people who do not have it. That is the specificity, written P(− | Dc) = 0.95, so a well person still trips the alarm with probability 0.05.
  • Every one of those three conditions on whether you are ill. The patient reading a positive result does not know whether they are ill. That mismatch is the whole example, and the next three pages resolve it.

1. The scene, before any notation

A screening programme runs a blood test across a whole population. Some of those people have a disease and most do not. The laboratory that makes the test has measured how it behaves on each group separately, because that is the only way a test can be measured: you take people whose true status you already know, run the test on them, and count how often it agrees.

So the numbers that ship with a test are always of the form given that we already know the truth, how often does the test agree with it. That sentence is worth reading twice, because it is the sentence the rest of this example unpicks. Nobody buys a test in order to learn about people whose status is already known.

Here is the population, drawn to scale. One hundred people, one of them ill.

2. The three numbers, each named in words

The lecture states three figures. Before using any of them, here is what each one means in a sentence, and which population it is measured inside.

NameNotationValueMeasured insideIn one sentence
Prevalence P(D) 0.01 the whole population Pick a person at random with no test and no symptoms: this is the chance they have the disease.
Sensitivity P(+ | D) 0.99 only the ill Among people who definitely have the disease, this fraction test positive. It is the catch rate.
Specificity P(− | Dc) 0.95 only the well Among people who definitely do not have the disease, this fraction test negative. It is the all-clear rate.

Naming the symbols one at a time, because this page is where they enter the course and every later page reuses them:

D, Dc, +, − D is the event "this person has the disease". Dc is its complement, the event "this person does not have the disease" - the little c is read aloud as "complement", and Lecture 3, Part 1 is where the complement rule was derived. + is the event "the test came back positive", and − is the event "the test came back negative". The vertical bar in P(+ | D) is read aloud as "given", and Lecture 3, Part 4 is where it was defined: it means the world has shrunk to D, and we are asking what fraction of that shrunken world is positive.

3. Two more numbers, which arrive for free

The lecture writes only three figures, but two more follow immediately from the complement rule, and the example cannot be worked without them. Both are this course's arithmetic on the lecture's own numbers, not extra data.

  1. The share of the population that is well. D and Dc are complements, so their probabilities sum to one: P(Dc) = 1 − P(D) = 1 − 0.01 = 0.99. Ninety-nine people in a hundred.
  2. The false-positive rate. Inside the well population, the test either says negative or says positive, and those two are complements within that population. So P(+ | Dc) = 1 − P(− | Dc) = 1 − 0.95 = 0.05. The notes write this figure out explicitly, so it is the lecture's own step and not an inference: specificity 0.95 means a false-positive rate of 0.05.
  3. The false-negative rate. The same move inside the ill population: P(− | D) = 1 − P(+ | D) = 1 − 0.99 = 0.01. One ill person in a hundred is missed by the test.

Notice that step 2 and step 3 both used the complement rule inside a conditional world rather than inside the whole population. That is legitimate, and TA Session 1, Part 5 is the page that proves it: conditioning on a fixed event with positive probability produces a genuine probability space of its own, with its own complement rule. The rule is not being borrowed loosely; it applies because P( · | D) is itself a probability measure.

4. All five numbers on one tree

Every figure now in hand hangs off one two-stage split: first whether the person is ill, then what the test says about them.

5. Which way does the arrow point?

Here is the observation the whole example turns on, and it is worth slowing down for.

Sensitivity is P(+ | D). To use it you must already stand inside D. It answers the laboratory's question: if this person is ill, will my test find it?

The patient sitting with a positive result has the opposite problem. They know they are in +. They do not know whether they are in D. Their question is P(D | +): the test fired, so how likely is it that I am ill?

These are different numbers. They involve the same two events, but the conditioning bar points the other way, and swapping the two sides of a conditional bar is not something arithmetic allows you to do for free. Turning one into the other is exactly the job of Bayes rule, and it needs one extra ingredient that neither number contains: the base rate.

A note on the word "accurate"Everyday language collapses all three numbers into one word. A test described as "99 percent accurate" might mean sensitivity, might mean specificity, and might mean the overall fraction of correct verdicts across the whole population - which for this test would be a fourth number again. The lecture avoids the word entirely and states two conditional probabilities plus a prevalence, which is the only unambiguous way to describe a test. When a claim about accuracy reaches you without saying which population it was measured in, it has not told you anything yet.

6. Check yourself

The figure P(+ | D) = 0.99 is measured on which group of people?
Sensitivity conditions on D, so the world has shrunk to people who have the disease and the 0.99 is the fraction of that group the test catches. The whole screened population is where the prevalence 0.01 lives, not the sensitivity. The group whose test came back positive is the world of P(D | +), the patient's question, which this lecture has not answered yet. And the group already known to lack the disease is where specificity 0.95 and the false-positive rate 0.05 are measured.
Specificity is 0.95. Which figure follows from it by the complement rule alone?
Specificity conditions on the well, so its complement stays inside the well: 1 minus 0.95 is the false-positive rate P(+ given D-complement) = 0.05. The prevalence is a separate stated figure, 0.01, and nothing about the test can produce it. The miss rate for ill people is 1 minus the sensitivity, which is 0.01 rather than 0.05. And how often a positive result is wrong is P(D-complement given +), which needs Bayes rule and the base rate, and is very far from 5 percent.
Your teacher. Before turning the page, say the three numbers aloud with their populations attached: "one in a hundred people, over everybody"; "ninety-nine in a hundred, among the ill"; "ninety-five in a hundred, among the well". If you can do that without looking, the next three pages are arithmetic rather than new ideas. If a number floats free of its population in your head, it will float free on the exam too, and that is precisely where the base-rate trap catches people.
