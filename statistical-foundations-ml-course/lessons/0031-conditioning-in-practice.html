Draw the tree first, and the algebra becomes transcription

working 16 min Tutorial problems 9, 10 and 11. Cards, urns and dice: three chances for the conditional formula to earn its keep, each with its own picture.

The one-minute version

  • Problem 9: given at least five hearts among six cards, the chance of six is C(13,6)/(C(13,5)·39 + C(13,6)) = 4/121 ≈ 3.31%.
  • Problem 10: first red then black without replacement is (4/10)×(6/9) = 4/15 - the second draw's denominator shrinks.
  • Problem 11: the die splits two-to-four between urns, so white arrives at (1/3)(2/5)+(2/3)(3/5) = 8/15 ≈ 53.33%.
  • All three run one machine: disjoint cases, conditional weights, add them up. The pictures below are that machine.

Problem 9, restated

Six cards are dealt at random without replacement from a standard deck of 52, which contains 13 hearts and 39 non-hearts. Let A be the event that all six cards are hearts, and B the event that at least five are. Find P(A|B).

Which theorem is in play, and why the sets must come first

The governing definition is P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B), applied over equally likely hands, where the number of possible hands is counted with combinations - unordered selections - because a dealt hand has no internal order. Before touching arithmetic, notice the containment: a hand with six hearts certainly has at least five, so A sits entirely inside B, and intersecting changes nothing.

  1. Write both probabilities as combinations before evaluating anything, so the structure stays visible. All hands are subsets of size 6 from 52, so
P(A) = C(13,6)/C(52,6)      P(B) = [C(13,5)·C(39,1) + C(13,6)] / C(52,6) Read as: the chance of six hearts is the count of all-heart hands, choose 6 of 13 hearts, over the count of all hands, choose 6 of 52. The chance of at least five adds hands with exactly five hearts - choose 5 of 13 times choose 1 of 39 - to the all-six hands. The common denominator C(52,6) says both events live on the same equally likely ocean of hands.
  1. Form the quotient and watch the ocean cancel. P(A|B) = P(A)/P(B) = C(13,6) / [C(13,5)·39 + C(13,6)]. Nothing was evaluated yet; the C(52,6) divided out whole.
  2. Evaluate the pieces. C(13,6) = 1716; C(13,5) = 1287; C(39,1) = 39.
  3. Combine. 1287 × 39 = 50,193, plus 1716 gives 51,909. So P(A|B) = 1716/51,909 = 4/121 ≈ 0.0331.
Where this goes wrongTwo slips. Treating the deal as ordered inflates every count by the same factor of 6!, which cancels - but only if you actually carry it, and most attempts drop it inconsistently somewhere. And forgetting that B contains the all-six hands, writing its numerator as C(13,5)·39 alone, which asks "exactly five" when the sheet said "at least five". Write the combinations first, cancel second, evaluate last.

Problem 10, restated

An urn holds 4 red balls and 6 black balls. Two balls are drawn successively at random without replacement. Find the probability that the first is red and the second is black.

Which theorem is in play, and what the picture must show

The multiplication rule, P(first ∩ second) = P(first) × P(second | first): the probability of a path through the experiment equals the product of the edge weights along it. The picture exists so the shrinking denominator - ten balls, then nine - is visible rather than remembered.

  1. Name the events. A = first draw red; B = second draw black. Wanted: P(A∩B).
  2. Price the first edge. Ten balls, four red: P(A) = 4/10.
  3. Price the second edge conditionally. After one red leaves, nine balls remain and none of the six blacks was touched: P(B|A) = 6/9.
  4. Multiply along the path. P(A∩B) = (4/10)×(6/9) = 24/90 = 4/15.
  5. Confirm by counting paths. Ordered pairs of distinct balls: 10×9 in total, favourable pairs 4×6, giving 24/90 again - the same number from a different door.
Where this goes wrongThe reflex error is writing the second factor as 6/10, silently pretending the urn refilled - that is independence thinking, and this experiment is precisely not independent. Without replacement, the first draw edits the second draw's world: fewer balls, edited colours. Whenever you see "without replacement", expect a conditional and let the tree show the shrinking denominators.

Problem 11, restated

Urn U1 holds 4 white and 6 black balls; urn U2 holds 6 white and 4 black. A fair six-sided die is rolled: U1 is selected on a 5 or 6, U2 on a 1, 2, 3 or 4. One ball is then drawn from the selected urn. Find the probability it is white.

Which theorem is in play, and the detail a reader skims past

The law of total probability: if E1, ..., Ek carve the sample space into disjoint cases covering everything - a partition - then P(W) = ∑i P(Ei)·P(W|Ei). The detail that decides the answer is the die's split: two faces versus four faces, not three versus three. Skim that and you will compute 1/2 instead of 1/3 for the first weight, and land on exactly 0.5 - a plausible-looking wrong answer, which is what makes this problem a trap.

  1. Name the cases. E1 = urn U1 selected, E2 = urn U2 selected. Exactly one happens per roll, they cannot co-occur, and together they cover all six faces: disjoint and exhaustive, a genuine partition.
  2. Weight the branches. P(E1) = 2/6 = 1/3 and P(E2) = 4/6 = 2/3. Check: 1/3 + 2/3 = 1.
  3. Weight within each urn. P(W|E1) = 4/10 = 2/5 and P(W|E2) = 6/10 = 3/5.
  4. Sum the white paths. P(W) = (1/3)(2/5) + (2/3)(3/5) = 2/15 + 6/15 = 8/15 ≈ 0.5333.
  5. Sanity-read the result. 8/15 sits between the two urns' own white rates 0.4 and 0.6, as any mixture must, and closer to 0.6 because U2 is selected twice as often.
Where this goes wrongThe symmetric-splitting error above is the intended trap; the secondary one is mixing up which urn the die favours - re-read the sheet and U1 is the unlucky one, chosen on just two faces. A third slip is adding P(W|E1) + P(W|E2) directly, which can exceed one and answers nothing: the branch probabilities are weights, and weighting is the entire content of the law.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In problem 9, why is every hand counted by A automatically inside B?
Having six hearts exceeds the threshold of at least five, so membership in A forces membership in B - containment, hence A∩B = A. Suit ranking plays no role anywhere. Both events use unordered hands, and the deal involves six cards, never thirteen.
In problem 11, the die sends us to urn U1 on two faces and U2 on four. What follows from that?
The uneven split weights U2 twice as heavily, dragging the mixture toward its higher white rate: 8/15 against the even-split value of exactly 1/2. The law applies to any partition, balanced or not. The contributions stay proportional to the weights, so U2 carries more. U1 keeps two sixths.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1 - the card question, tightenedworking

Five cards are dealt without replacement. Given that at least four are hearts, find the probability that all five are. Keep every count as combinations until the last line.

Hint

Exactly the problem-9 skeleton with 6 replaced by 5: containment first, quotient second, numbers last.

Show the worked solution
  1. Containment. Five hearts beat the "at least four" bar, so A ⊆ B and P(A|B) = P(A)/P(B).
  2. Combinations form. P(A|B) = C(13,5) / [C(13,4)·39 + C(13,5)], the C(52,5) cancelling unseen.
  3. Evaluate. C(13,5) = 1287, C(13,4) = 715, so the denominator is 715×39 + 1287 = 27,885 + 1,287 = 29,172.
  4. Reduce. 1287/29,172 = 3/68 ≈ 0.0441.

Sanity check. The answer should sit slightly above the six-card version's 4/121 ≈ 0.033, since needing five hearts is a weaker demand than needing six - and 0.044 is indeed the larger of the two.

Practice 2 - reverse the urn drawsfoundation

Back to problem 10's urn of 4 red and 6 black. Find the probability that the first ball is black and the second red. Then answer in one sentence: why did the two orders land on the same value?

Hint

Same tree, mirrored labels.

Show the worked solution
  1. First edge. P(first black) = 6/10.
  2. Conditional edge. After a black leaves, nine balls remain with all four reds intact: P(second red | first black) = 4/9.
  3. Multiply. (6/10)×(4/9) = 24/90 = 4/15 - identical to the red-then-black direction.
  4. Why equal? Multiplication is commutative: one red and one black leave the urn either way, and the pair of factors is the same two fractions in swapped order.

Sanity check. Any specific mixed outcome and its mirror image should agree here, since both describe "one of each in some order", whose combined probability is 8/15 across the two orders.

Your teacher. If the trees felt like decoration, cover the algebra and try recovering each answer from its picture alone - that is the skill being built. Then ask the inverse question of problem 11: given that the ball came out white, how likely was it U2? That reversal is Bayes' rule, waiting one lecture ahead. The mixture habit serves too: a router that splits traffic across models makes P(slow request) a weighted sum over the branches, each with its own P(slow | branch), so a healthy headline can hide one slow model the way one heavy coin hid inside problem 11.
