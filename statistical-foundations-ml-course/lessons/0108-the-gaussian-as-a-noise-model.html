The Gaussian earns its keep by modelling noise, and charges a CDF nobody can write down

working 12 min Lecture 7, IIT Bombay, delivered 5 September 2026. The last shape of the lecture, and the one the rest of the course keeps meeting.

After this page

  • You can read X ~ N(μ, σ2) and say where the bell sits and how wide it is.
  • You can say why a regression writes its error as a Gaussian, and state the objection a height invites.

Before you start: A density is not a probability, and The Gaussian and its missing formula from Lecture 6.

The one-minute version

  • The Gaussian, also called the normal, is written X ~ N(μ, σ2), with mu any real number and sigma strictly greater than zero.
  • Its density is defined for every real x, the only law in this lecture whose support is the whole line.
  • Mu is where it is centred, sigma is its effective width. What the two parameters really mean is deferred to a later lecture.
  • Its CDF is written Φ and has no closed form. Values come from tables or bounds.
  • The reason it is everywhere: it is the default model for noise, in a regression and in a channel alike.

1. Two dials, and what each one does

A Gaussian is fixed by two numbers and nothing else. Change the first and the whole picture slides sideways. Change the second and it spreads or peaks. Nothing else about the shape is yours to choose.

2. The density, symbol by symbol

The class notes write it once, on page 6, and add three characters that matter as much as the formula: x ∈ R.

fX(x) = 1 / (σ √(2π)) · exp( −(x − μ)2 / (2σ2) ),  for every real x Read as: fX(x) is the density of the random variable X at the point x, the thing you integrate to get a probability. μ, mu, is the first parameter and may be any real number. σ, sigma, is the second and must be strictly greater than zero. x − μ is how far the point x lies from mu, and squaring it makes left and right count the same, which is why the bell is symmetric. 2σ2 divides that squared distance, so a larger sigma makes the same gap count for less. exp(−...) is e raised to a negative number, so the height falls away from mu and falls fast. 1 / (σ √(2π)) is the constant in front, and its whole job is to make the total area exactly one. R is the set of all real numbers.

Work the constant once and the abstraction stops being abstract. At x = μ the exponent is zero, so the exponential is 1 and the height is just that leading constant.

  1. Set x to mu. Then x − μ = 0, so the exponent is −0 / (2σ2) = 0 and exp(0) = 1.
  2. What is left is the constant. The peak height is 1 / (σ √(2π)).
  3. Put sigma equal to 2. The square root of two pi is 2.5066, so the denominator is 2 × 2.5066 = 5.0133.
  4. Divide. 1 ÷ 5.0133 = 0.1995. A density value, not a probability: it is well under 1 here, and for sigma below 0.4 the same formula returns a number above 1.

What mu and sigma squared really mean is deferred. A student asked directly whether the two parameters represent anything. The lecturer answered that they do, that mu turns out to be the mean or expected value and sigma squared the variance, and then said plainly: we have not come to that yet. This page keeps the deferral. Until expectation is defined, mu is the centre and sigma is a width, and that is enough to use the formula.

3. The CDF has no closed form

Every other continuous shape in this lecture handed its CDF over. The uniform gave a straight ramp. The exponential gave 1 − e−λx. The Gaussian does not. Its CDF is written with a capital phi and stays an integral.

Φ(x) = ∫−∞x fX(z) dz Read as: Φ(x), capital phi, is the probability that the Gaussian random variable lands at or below x. ∫ is the integral sign, and the two limits under and over it say the area is swept from minus infinity up to x. fX(z) is the density from section 2, with z the dummy name for the point being swept over. dz says the sweep is along z.

The lecturer said it twice, once at the board and once when a student asked him to repeat it: that integral, the one with e to the minus a square in it, has no closed form. Numerical tables exist for it, bounds exist for it, and an exam would hand you the values you need.

Asserted, not provedThat an integral has no expression in the elementary functions is a genuine theorem and a hard one. The lecture states it and moves on, and Lecture 6 Part 13 already carried the same absence with the curve drawn out. What is new here is not the missing formula but what the shape is for. Note what the claim does not say: the values are not unknown. Hajek tabulates them, and this course drew them in Lecture 6.

4. Why it is the default model for noise

The lecturer called it probably the most used distribution, and gave the reason in one word: noise. The class notes carry both of the examples he used, and they are the same sentence written twice.

A student put the lower row into his own words: we have a set of points, we draw a line through them, and the deviation of the points from that line is Gaussian. The lecturer agreed, in that order and those terms.

Two closure properties were named as part of the reason it is used so heavily, both without proof here. A sum of two independent Gaussians is again a Gaussian. And a linear transformation of a Gaussian is again a Gaussian. Both are worked out in Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, the first as Example 4.12 and the second as the boxed result in Section 3.3.

5. What it costs: the whole line is a strong claim

The lecturer modelled the heights of people in the cohort as a Gaussian. A student objected, live, and the objection is correct.

The objection, and the repair the lecturer offered. Heights can never be negative, and a Gaussian puts density everywhere on the line. The lecturer agreed at once: if you want to be strict, the Gaussian is not exactly a good fit for a height. People still use it, he said, when mu is far enough above zero that the area under the curve in the negative part is very small and will not matter in the calculation. The cleaner repair, and the one he preferred if he had to be consistent, is to model the deviation instead: take the n observed heights, form their average, and model hi − h̄. That quantity is genuinely two-sided, since a person is either taller or shorter than the average, so the model's support and the quantity's finally agree.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A student asked whether any distribution in the lecture uses negative values. What is the answer?
The Gaussian is the one, and its density is strictly positive at every real x. A density may certainly be defined on negative values, so the first option is wrong as a general claim. The exponential is zero for x below zero, which is exactly what rules it out in the next page's worked example. And setting mu to zero moves the bell's centre without changing its support, which was already the whole line.
Why does the lecture reach for a Gaussian to model the error term in a regression?
An error is a deviation from a prediction, so it must be free to be positive or negative, and the Gaussian is the shape on this page whose support allows that. A residual is signed, not a distance, so the first option describes something else. The Gaussian's cumulative function is the one shape here that has no closed form at all, so the third is exactly backwards. And a fitted line that needed no error term would be a line every point sat on, which is what regression exists because data does not do.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A sensor reports a temperature in degrees Celsius. Its reading is modelled as the true value plus a Gaussian error with mu equal to 0 and sigma equal to 0.4 degrees.

Find the peak height of that error density, and say in one sentence why the answer does not embarrass the definition of a density.

Hint

The peak sits at x equal to mu, where the exponential term becomes 1. Everything else is the constant in front.

Show the worked solution
  1. Put x at the centre. With x equal to mu the exponent is zero and exp(0) = 1, so the height is the leading constant alone.
  2. Write the constant. It is 1 divided by sigma times the square root of two pi, which is 1 ÷ (0.4 × 2.5066).
  3. Do the multiplication. 0.4 × 2.5066 = 1.0027.
  4. Divide. 1 ÷ 1.0027 = 0.9973 per degree Celsius.
  5. Say why it is fine. A density is not a probability. It is a rate per unit of x, and only its area over an interval is a probability, so a value near or above 1 breaks nothing.

Sanity check. Sigma is a little under 0.4, and the peak height is 1 over sigma times 2.5066, so the answer had to land just under 1. Halving sigma to 0.2 would roughly double it to about 1.99, which is above 1 and still a perfectly good density.

Carry this away

  • A Gaussian is two numbers: mu slides it, sigma widens or peaks it, and its density is positive at every real x.
  • Its cumulative function is written phi, has no closed form, and is read from a table or a bound rather than evaluated.
  • It is the default model for noise because noise falls both ways, and that same two-sidedness is what makes it a poor fit for a quantity that cannot go negative.
