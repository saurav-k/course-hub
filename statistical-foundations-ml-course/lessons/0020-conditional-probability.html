Conditioning does not divide anything: it shrinks the world

12 min working Learning that B happened redraws the boundary of the world. The formula is that picture written down, and the bar is not a division sign.

The one-minute version

  • Once you know B happened, every outcome outside B is dead: it can no longer occur. The world is now B, and probabilities must be recomputed inside it.
  • Counting points in a ten-point picture derives the formula before it is ever written: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B).
  • Worked in full on two coins, one fair and one that always lands heads, where tails and coin 2 cannot co-occur.
  • Fixing B and varying A gives a brand-new, perfectly ordinary probability measure. Conditional probabilities obey every rule you learned in Part 1.

1. The picture: ten equally likely points

The notes draw the idea before defining it. A sample space Ω of ten points, each equally likely, so any event's probability is its point count over ten. Event A holds five of the points. Event B holds four, and the two events share two points. From the picture, before any formula:

  1. Price A and B from the counts. P(A) = 5/10 = 1/2 and P(B) = 4/10 = 2/5. The overlap A ∩ B holds 2 points, so P(A ∩ B) = 2/10.
  2. Ask the conditional question in words. Given that B occurred, how likely is A? Every point outside B is now impossible: it was in the old world, but it did not happen.
  3. Count inside the new world. The new world is B, holding 4 equally likely points. A's footprint inside it is the overlap, holding 2. So the answer is 2 out of 4, which is 1/2.

2. The world shrinks, and the formula is the shrinkage

The counting derivation, written out. In the old world, the chance of landing in the overlap was N(A ∩ B) / N out of N points, and the chance of landing in B was N(B) / N. Once B is known to have happened, the overlap's chance is measured against B alone. Dividing the two old-world fractions cancels the N, which is where the formula comes from:

P(A | B) = [ N(A ∩ B) / N ] / [ N(B) / N ] = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) P(A | B) reads "the probability of A given B": the probability that A occurs, once you know B has occurred. N is the number of equally likely points, N(A ∩ B) the number of those points lying in both events. The two divisions by N cancel, leaving probabilities only.
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), and on the picture: 2/4 = 1/2 The definition the notes write down. The numerator is the event you care about, restricted to the new world; the denominator is the size of the new world itself. It only makes sense when P(B) > 0: conditioning on an impossible event gives you no world to stand in.

One observation to carry forward, because it will matter in Part 8: on this particular picture, P(A | B) = 1/2 and P(A) = 1/2. Learning B happened changed nothing about A, not because the events are disjoint, but because A occupies the same share of B as it does of the whole space. Hold that thought.

3. The two-coin example, worked in full

The notes now build an example where the outcomes are not equally likely, which is where the counting shortcut dies and the formula earns its keep. Two coins: C1 is fair, and C2 is a trick coin that always lands heads. The experiment: choose one of the two coins at random, then toss it once. Four outcomes are possible, and a discrete space is free to give them unequal prices:

OutcomeMeaningProbability
(C1, H)picked the fair coin, it landed heads1/4
(C1, T)picked the fair coin, it landed tails1/4
(C2, H)picked the heads coin, it landed heads1/2
(C2, T)picked the heads coin, it landed tails0

Now name the events the notes use. E is "observe tails", the set {(C1, T), (C2, T)}. A1 is "chose coin 1", the set {(C1, H), (C1, T)}, and A2 is "chose coin 2". Price each from the table: P(E) = 1/4 + 0 = 1/4, and P(A1) = P(A2) = 1/2. Then ask the conditional questions:

  1. Tails, given the fair coin. The overlap E ∩ A1 is the single outcome (C1, T), priced 1/4. So P(E | A1) = (1/4) / (1/2) = 1/2. The fair coin lands tails half the time, as it should.
  2. Tails, given the trick coin. The overlap E ∩ A2 is (C2, T), priced 0. So P(E | A2) = 0 / (1/2) = 0. Knowing the trick coin was chosen kills tails entirely.

4. Conditioning on a fixed event is a genuine probability measure

Here is the fact that makes conditioning safe to use everywhere else. Freeze an event B with P(B) > 0, and for every event A define a new function of A: PB(A) = P(A | B). The notes claim, and then verify, that this PB is itself a valid probability measure, meaning it satisfies the same three axioms from Lecture 2. The checks are one line each:

  1. Nothing negative. PB(A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) is one probability divided by a positive one, so it is never negative.
  2. The new whole space is certain. PB(Ω) = P(Ω ∩ B) / P(B) = P(B) / P(B) = 1. Inside the shrunken world, "something happens" is certain, as it must be.
  3. Disjoint events still add. If A1 and A2 are disjoint, then (A1 ∪ A2) ∩ B splits into the disjoint pieces A1 ∩ B and A2 ∩ B, so PB(A1 ∪ A2) = PB(A1) + PB(A2).

The empty event comes free: PB(∅) = P(∅ ∩ B) / P(B) = 0. So the shrunken world has its own probability space, with B as its sample space and PB as its measure. Every rule from Part 1, the complement rule, containment, the addition rule, inclusion-exclusion, all of it, applies unchanged to conditional probabilities. You will use that silently on almost every remaining page of this course.

Where the notes are looseThe notes draw the ten-point picture and then write P(A | B) with a "defined as" sign, deriving it from the counting in one line. The derivation is honest but it leans on the points being equally likely, which the two-coin example immediately breaks. The formula survives that break because it is the definition, not the counting shortcut; the counting is why the definition is the right one. Also note the order of ideas on the notes' page 7: the measure claim appears after the Bayes example, while this page states it here so that Parts 5 to 7 can lean on it.

Check yourself

On the ten-point picture, you learn that B happened. What is the probability that A happened too?
Given B, the denominator is B's four points, not the original ten, and A's footprint in that world is the two-point overlap, so 2/4 = 1/2. The first option prices B, not A given B. The second is the unconditional overlap probability P(A ∩ B), the answer to a different question where nothing is known. The third is the unconditional P(A), which ignores the information entirely; it happens to land on the same number here, which is a coincidence of this picture and not a general fact.
In the two-coin experiment, what is P(observing tails, given that coin 2 was chosen)?
Given coin 2, the world shrinks to its two outcomes, and the tails outcome carries price 0, so the conditional probability is 0 divided by 1/2, which is 0. The picking probabilities are the priors P(A1) and P(A2), and conditioning on the choice makes them irrelevant to the toss. One quarter is the unconditional P(E), the answer before you learn which coin was chosen. And "always lands heads" makes the heads outcome certain, which is probability 1 for heads, and therefore 0 for tails, not 1 for tails.
Your teacher. Whenever you meet a conditional probability this week, say the shrinkage sentence out loud before computing: "the world is now such-and-such, and I want such-and-such's share of it." The arithmetic is one division; the skill is knowing what the denominator is a measure of. Getting that habit right now is what will make Bayes rule, two pages from now, feel inevitable rather than imposed.
