Lecture 6 · Part 7 of 14 · the first two named shapes

A switch and a fair choice: the two distributions everything else is built out of

10 min two short definitions The notes' first two worked examples of named discrete random variables, and the count that catches people out in the second one.

The one-minute version

  • A Bernoulli random variable has support {0, 1} with pX(1) = p and pX(0) = 1 − p. One parameter, one switch, and nothing else.
  • Its CDF is a two-step staircase: 0 below zero, 1 − p from 0 up to but not including 1, and 1 from there on. The notes draw exactly this.
  • A discrete uniform random variable has support {a, a+1, ..., b}, a run of consecutive whole numbers, and gives every one of them the same mass 1/(b − a + 1).
  • The +1 in that denominator is a fencepost. The run from a to b contains b − a + 1 numbers, not b − a, and forgetting it is the single most common slip with this shape.
  • They are not unrelated. A discrete uniform on {0, 1} is a Bernoulli with p = 1/2, which both formulas agree on.

1. The Bernoulli, symbol by symbol

The notes introduce it under the heading "Bernoulli RVs" with the support written beside the name.

pX(1) = p  and  pX(0) = 1 − p p is the single parameter, a number between 0 and 1, and it is the probability that the variable comes out as 1. 1 − p is then forced, because the two masses have to add to one and the support has only two points in it. pX is the mass function from Part 3, and everywhere off the two points {0, 1} it is zero.

That is the entire definition. Two masses on two points, one number to choose. It is the smallest random variable that is not a constant, and its whole job is to record whether one thing happened.

The labels 1 and 0 are conventional and they carry no arithmetic meaning yet. What makes the choice a good one is that later in the course a sum of Bernoulli variables counts how many of the things happened, which is precisely the counting Lecture 5, Part 5 did by hand.

2. Its CDF, and the shape the notes draw

Apply the running sum of Part 4 to two masses and there are only three answers to give.

  1. Below 0. No support point is at or below the threshold, so FX(x) = 0.
  2. From 0 up to but not including 1. The point 0 qualifies and the point 1 does not, so FX(x) = 1 − p. The notes mark this level on the vertical axis of their drawing.
  3. At 1 and beyond. Both points qualify, so FX(x) = (1 − p) + p = 1.
  4. Check the jumps. The jump at 0 has height 1 − p and the jump at 1 has height 1 − (1 − p) = p, which by Part 6 must equal the mass at each point. Both agree.
The notes draw the CDF symbolically, with no value for pThe source draws only the staircase, with 1 − p and 1 marked on the vertical axis and no numbers anywhere. Any drawing has to choose a height for 1 − p in order to exist, and this one takes p = 0.3 so that the two levels are visibly different. That value is this course's drawing choice and not the lecturer's. The labels on the figure are the truth; the pixel heights are an illustration. Above p = 0.5 the shorter spike and the taller one swap sides, and every formula on this page is unaffected.

3. The discrete uniform, and the fencepost

The second example in the notes gives every value in a run of consecutive integers exactly the same mass.

X ∈ {a, a+1, ..., b}  with  pX(i) = 1 / (b − a + 1) for every i in that set a is the smallest value and b the largest, and both are whole numbers with a not bigger than b. The set runs through every integer between them with none skipped. i is one of those values. b − a + 1 is how many of them there are, and the mass is one divided by that count, so that the total over the support is exactly one.

The count in the denominator is the part worth slowing down on, because it is a fencepost and fenceposts are where careful people lose marks.

  1. Why not b − a. That is the distance from a to b, which is the number of gaps between consecutive values, not the number of values. A run from 2 to 7 has five gaps and six numbers in it.
  2. Check the smallest case. If a = b the set has exactly one member. The formula gives b − a + 1 = 1, so the mass is 1, and the variable is a constant. Correct.
  3. Check a familiar case. A fair die is a discrete uniform with a = 1 and b = 6, so the count is 6 − 1 + 1 = 6 and each face has mass 1/6. Correct, and this is the assumption every dice problem in Lecture 4, Part 8 was quietly making.
  4. Check the total. Adding the same mass b − a + 1 times gives (b − a + 1) × 1/(b − a + 1) = 1, which is what Part 3 requires of any PMF.
This course's instanceThe notes state the discrete uniform symbolically and draw nothing for it. The values a = 2 and b = 7, the six masses of 1/6 and the die check in step 3 are this course's own instances of the notes' own formula, chosen to make the fencepost visible. Neither carries a key-number mark, because the source states no numbers for this shape at all.

4. How the two are related

The two shapes on this page look unrelated, and one instance connects them. Set a = 0 and b = 1 in the discrete uniform. The support is {0, 1} and the count in the denominator is 1 − 0 + 1 = 2, so both masses are 1/2. That is a Bernoulli with p = 1/2, which is the fair coin.

This course's observationThe notes list these two shapes one after the other and draw no connection between them. Section 4 is this course's own, and it uses nothing except the two formulas the notes state. It is here because a reader who has seen why two definitions coincide in exactly one place remembers both definitions better than a reader who has seen them listed.

5. Check yourself

A discrete uniform random variable has support running from 4 up to 11. What mass does each value carry?
The run 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 has eight members, which the formula gives as eleven minus four plus one, so each mass is one eighth. One seventh drops the plus one and counts the gaps instead of the values, which is the fencepost slip the whole of section 3 is about. One eleventh would be right only if the support started at 1, and one fourth would need a support of four values, so both read a single endpoint as if it were a count.
In a Bernoulli CDF, what is the height of the jump at the value 1?
Part 6 makes the jump at a point equal to the mass at that point, and the mass at 1 is p by definition, so the staircase climbs from one minus p to one and the climb is p. One is the height the staircase reaches, not the size of the step it took to get there. One minus p is the level it was standing on before the jump, which is where the step starts rather than how tall it is. And one half is only the answer in the single case p equals one half, which section 4 shows is the fair coin and not the general Bernoulli.
Your teacher. These two are worth learning as gestures rather than as formulas. A Bernoulli is a switch: did the thing happen, yes or no. A discrete uniform is a fair choice from a numbered list: pick one, no favourites. When a problem arrives, try saying it in one of those two sentences before reaching for anything. If the sentence fits, the formula is already written on this page, and if it does not, you have learned something specific about what makes the problem harder. The systems versions: a Bernoulli is the canary probe that either passes or fails, while a discrete uniform is round-robin load balancing choosing equally among b − a + 1 healthy backends.
