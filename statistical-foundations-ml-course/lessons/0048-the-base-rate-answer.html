A ninety-nine percent test on a one percent disease still leaves you probably well

11 min the payoff One division, and an answer most people refuse to believe the first time they meet it. The lecture states it as 0.17.

The one-minute version

  • Bayes rule turns the test's direction around: P(D | +) = P(+ | D) P(D) / P(+). The numerator is Part 2's true-positive road, the denominator is Part 2's total.
  • Putting the numbers in: (0.99 × 0.01) / (0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.05), which the lecture states as approximately 0.17.
  • So a patient holding a positive result from a test that catches 99 percent of cases is still about five times more likely to be well than ill.
  • Nothing is wrong with the test. The base rate is doing the damage: a rare disease means a large well population, and a large well population generates a lot of false alarms. The same arithmetic runs an abuse or safety filter on a serving path: a 99-percent-accurate flagger over a rare violation still returns mostly false positives, so a flag rate is a claim about prevalence, not about skill.

1. The formula, assembled from parts you already have

Lecture 3, Part 7 derived Bayes rule from the definition of conditional probability, so nothing new is being introduced here. What is new is that all three ingredients are now sitting on the table.

P(D | +) = P(+ | D) × P(D) ÷ P(+) Reading it left to right in words: P(D | +) is what we want, the chance of being ill given a positive test - the posterior. P(+ | D) = 0.99 is the sensitivity, the chance the test fires on an ill person - the likelihood. P(D) = 0.01 is the prevalence, what we believed before the test ran - the prior. P(+) = 0.0594 is how often the test fires at all, computed on the previous page - the evidence. Prior times likelihood, divided by evidence.

The lecture writes it in exactly that shape, with the denominator spelled out as the two-road sum rather than as a single symbol:

P(D | +) = (0.99 × 0.01) ÷ (0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.05) ≈ 0.17 The numerator is the ill road from Part 2. The denominator is the ill road plus the well road, which is every way a positive result can happen. So the whole fraction reads: of all the alarms, what share came from the ill road?

2. The division, worked in full

The lecture states the result and does not show the division. These four lines are that step. They are arithmetic on the lecture's own numbers, and the final figure quoted with the key-number mark is the lecture's, not this course's.

  1. The numerator. 0.99 × 0.01 = 0.0099. This is the ill road: the share of the whole population that is both ill and correctly flagged.
  2. The denominator. 0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.05 = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594, exactly as Part 2 worked it.
  3. Divide. 0.0099 ÷ 0.0594. Multiplying both sides by 10,000 turns it into 99 ÷ 594, and 594 = 6 × 99, so the fraction is exactly 1/6.
  4. As a decimal. 1/6 = 0.1666..., which to two places is 0.17. That is the figure the lecture states: 0.17.

Step 3 is worth pausing on, because the answer is not merely close to a sixth, it is a sixth. That is the ten-thousand-person table from Part 2 read as a ratio: 594 alarms went off, 99 of them on people who really were ill, and 99 out of 594 is one in six with nothing left over.

3. What the test did, and what it did not do

It is easy to read 0.17 as "the test is useless". It is not. Look at how far the belief actually moved.

  1. Before the test. This person's chance of being ill was the prevalence, 0.01, or one in a hundred.
  2. After a positive test. Their chance is 1/6, about 0.1667, or one in six.
  3. The size of the move. 0.1667 ÷ 0.01 is very close to 16.7. The evidence multiplied the odds of illness by roughly seventeen.
  4. Where it still stands. Seventeen times a very small number is still a small number. One in six is a long way from one in two.

Both readings are true at once, and holding both is the skill. The test is genuinely informative: it took a hundred-to-one shot and made it a five-to-one shot. It is also genuinely insufficient on its own: nobody should be treated for a disease on a five-to-one chance of not having it. That is why real screening programmes use a cheap sensitive test first and an expensive specific test second, on the much smaller group the first one flagged.

4. What would have to change

The lecture states one number for one setting. It is worth seeing what that number is a function of, because the answer is not the test's quality but the population it is pointed at. The figure below is this course's own extension: it holds the lecture's test fixed at sensitivity 0.99 and false-positive rate 0.05, and sweeps the prevalence. The lecture states only the single point at a prevalence of 0.01.

This figure is derived, not quotedEvery point on that curve except one is this course's arithmetic, computed from the same formula the lecture writes: P(D | +) = 0.99p ÷ (0.99p + 0.05(1 − p)), with p standing for the prevalence. Only the marked point at p = 0.01 is the lecture's own, and it is the 0.17 stated on the board. The curve is included because the lecture's point lands where it does because of the prevalence, and one point cannot show that.

5. Check yourself

The posterior came out at 1 in 6. Which pair of figures produced that ratio directly?
The posterior is the true alarms as a share of every alarm, and 99 out of 594 is exactly one sixth. Sensitivity against specificity is a comparison of two error rates measured in different populations and does not divide into anything meaningful. The 100 against 9,900 is the prior split, before any test result is known. And prevalence over the total, 0.01 divided by 0.0594, is about 0.168 - close to the right answer by coincidence of these particular numbers, but it is the wrong numerator: the ill road is 0.0099, not 0.01.
Screening the same test on a group where 20 percent are ill would give a posterior of about 0.83. Why?
Nothing about the test changes: what changes is that the well slice shrinks from 0.99 to 0.8, so the false road drops to 0.8 times 0.05 = 0.04 while the true road climbs to 0.2 times 0.99 = 0.198, and the true road now dominates. Sensitivity is a fixed property of the test at 0.99 and does not respond to the population. Specificity is likewise fixed at 0.95. And the false-positive rate stays exactly 0.05 per well person; there are simply far fewer well people for it to act on.
Your teacher. This is the page to argue with, out loud, until the argument stops working. Most people meet 0.17 and immediately look for the error, because a 99 percent test producing a 17 percent answer feels like a contradiction. It is not: 99 percent is a promise about the ill, and the answer is about a crowd that is almost entirely well. Say that sentence until it stops feeling like a trick. Then notice how often a monitoring alert at work has the same shape - a rare failure, a sensitive detector, and a pager that mostly cries wolf.
