Cut the world into slices, price the event in each, add them up

10 min working When an event is hard to price directly, find a partition you can condition on, and let the slices hand the answer over piece by piece.

The one-minute version

  • A partition is a set of events that never overlap and together cover everything: every outcome lands in exactly one slice.
  • Any event A splits into one piece per slice, the pieces A ∩ Bi are disjoint, so their probabilities add up to P(A).
  • Each piece is P(Bi) × P(A | Bi) by the multiplication rule, which turns one hard price into a sum of easy ones.
  • Worked on the two coins: P(tails) = 1/2 × 1/2 + 1/2 × 0 = 1/4, matching Part 4's direct count.

1. The word "partition", with its picture

The law is stated in terms of a partition, so the word gets its picture first. A partition of the sample space Ω is a collection of events B1, B2, ..., such that two things hold. The slices are disjoint: Bi ∩ Bj = ∅ whenever i and j differ. And the slices cover everything: their union is Ω. Together that says every outcome belongs to exactly one slice, like floors of a building: no outcome is on two floors, and no outcome is in the stairwell.

2. The law, in two equalities

Both equalities are machinery you already own. The first is Part 1's disjoint-adds axiom applied to the slices; the second is Part 5's multiplication rule applied inside each slice.

P(A) = ∑i P(A ∩ Bi) = ∑i P(Bi) × P(A | Bi) B1, B2, ... form a partition of Ω, and A is any event. The first sum adds A's footprint in each slice; it equals P(A) because the footprints are disjoint and cover A. The second rewrites each footprint with the multiplication rule, P(A ∩ Bi) = P(Bi) × P(A | Bi). The sum runs over the slices; the notes write it as a sum over i in I with I = {1, 2, 3} for their three-slice drawing.

Why the first equality holds: every way A can happen lands in some slice, because the slices cover the world, and in only one slice, because they are disjoint. So A is exactly the union of its footprints A ∩ Bi, glued disjointly, and Part 1's axiom says disjoint probabilities add. Why the second holds: it is the multiplication rule rearranged once per slice, no new probability anywhere.

The practical value is the direction of the arrow. P(A) on the left is often the price you cannot see: a system-level failure rate, a disease rate in a whole population, a click rate over all visitors. The terms on the right are prices you can reason about: the failure rate given this dependency dies, the disease rate given the age band, the click rate given the device. The law trades one invisible number for a sum of visible ones.

3. The two-coin example, reassembled from slices

Part 4 priced P(E), the event of observing tails, by listing outcomes. The notes now reprice it with the law, choosing as the partition the two events A1 = "chose coin 1" and A2 = "chose coin 2". Those two are disjoint, since one toss cannot come from both coins, and they cover the space, since some coin was chosen: a genuine partition. The law then says:

P(E) = P(A1) × P(E | A1) + P(A2) × P(E | A2) E is the event that the toss lands tails. A1 and A2 are the two coin choices, which partition the sample space. P(E | A1) and P(E | A2) were both computed in Part 4: they are 1/2 and 0.
  1. First slice. P(A1) × P(E | A1) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Half the time the fair coin is picked, and it then lands tails half of that.
  2. Second slice. P(A2) × P(E | A2) = 1/2 × 0 = 0. Half the time the trick coin is picked, and it then lands tails never.
  3. Add. P(E) = 1/4 + 0 = 1/4, which is exactly the price Part 4 found by listing the four outcomes. Two routes, one answer, and the notes show both.
Where the notes are looseThe notes state the law with a three-slice picture and an index set I = {1, 2, 3}, then immediately apply it to the two-coin example, whose partition has two slices. Nothing is wrong: the law holds for any finite collection of slices, and for countably many with the sum read the same way. The notes simply draw three slices because three is the picture that shows the idea, and then use the smallest partition their example needs.

Check yourself

For a collection of events to be a partition of the sample space, what must hold?
A partition needs exactly the two properties in the law's setup: slices that never overlap, Bi ∩ Bj = ∅, and slices that cover everything, so every outcome lands somewhere. Probabilities of a half are irrelevant; the two coin choices partition the space while carrying equal probabilities, but three unequal failure modes partition one just as well. Equal size is not required either, only non-overlap plus coverage. And nested boxes are the opposite arrangement: containment means every outcome in the inner box is also in the outer one, which is overlap, not partition.
In the two-coin example, why does the second slice contribute exactly 0 to P(E)?
The slice's contribution is P(A2) × P(E | A2), and the second factor is 0: given the trick coin, tails is impossible, so anything times zero is zero. The choice probability being one half is the other factor, and it is not what zeroes the term. The overlap being empty is a true restatement of the numerator, but the contribution is zero because the conditional probability is zero, which is the sharper and more usable way to say it. The fair coin supplying all observed tails is a consequence, not the cause; the cause lives entirely inside the trick coin's behaviour.
Your teacher. Pick a rate your team reports and find its hidden partition: rate given mobile and rate given desktop, or given new and given returning. You will find the headline number is the law of total probability already, summed invisibly over slices nobody wrote down. Making the slices explicit is usually the moment the number stops being mysterious, and it is also how you spot a Simpson's-paradox-style surprise before it finds you.
