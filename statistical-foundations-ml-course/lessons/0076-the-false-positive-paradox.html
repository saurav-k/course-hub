TA Session 2 · Part 7 of 7 · Example 1.26

A test that is right ninety-nine percent of the time, and a positive result worth half a percent

working 12 min Example 1.26, the last question of the session, and the one the whole week has been building towards.

The one-minute version

  • A disease affects about 1 person in 10,000. A positive result on a healthy person happens 2 percent of the time; a negative result on an ill person happens 1 percent of the time.
  • So the test catches P(+ | D) = 0.99 of real cases, which the notes get as one minus the one percent miss rate.
  • Total probability gives the chance of a positive: 0.99 × 0.0001 + 0.02 × 0.9999 = 0.000099 + 0.019998 = 0.020097.
  • Bayes then gives P(D | +) = 0.000099 / 0.020097 ≈ 0.00493, which the notes state as 0.493%, or about 1 in 203.
  • The test is excellent. The answer is still half a percent, and the reason is entirely the rarity of the disease.

Example 1.26, restated

A certain disease affects about 1 out of 10,000 people. There is a test to check whether a person has the disease, and it is quite accurate. In particular:

  • the probability that the test result is positive, suggesting the person has the disease, given that the person does not have it, is only 2 percent;
  • the probability that the test result is negative, suggesting the person does not have the disease, given that the person has it, is only 1 percent.

A random person gets tested and the result comes back positive. What is the probability that the person has the disease?

Which theorem is in play, and the one figure that has to be derived

Name the events, and notice that one of the four numbers needed is not given directly.

  • D is the event that the person has the disease. P(D) = 1/10000 = 0.0001, and so P(Dc) = 0.9999.
  • + is the event that the test comes back positive.
  1. The false positive rate is given. P(+ | Dc) = 0.02.
  2. The false negative rate is given. P(− | D) = 0.01.
  3. The sensitivity has to be derived. Inside the population of ill people the test comes back either positive or negative, so the two probabilities add to one, and P(+ | D) = 1 − P(− | D) = 0.99. The notes make this step explicitly.

Step 3 is legal because conditioning on a fixed event produces a probability space of its own, in which the complement rule holds exactly as usual. TA Session 1, Part 5 is where that was proved rather than assumed.

Step 1: the chance of a positive result at all

A positive result arrives by two disjoint roads: from an ill person the test correctly flags, and from a healthy person it wrongly flags. The second road starts from a vastly larger population.

This course's presentationThe million-person walkthrough above is this course's own way of showing the same calculation the notes perform in probabilities. Every count in it is exact: 1,000,000 × 0.0001 = 100, and 100 × 0.99 = 99, and 999,900 × 0.02 = 19,998. The notes work in decimals throughout and reach the identical answer; nothing here contradicts them.
  1. Price the ill road. P(+ | D)P(D) = 0.99 × 0.0001 = 0.000099.
  2. Price the healthy road. P(+ | Dc)P(Dc) = 0.02 × 0.9999 = 0.019998.
  3. Add them. P(+) = 0.000099 + 0.019998 = 0.020097. Roughly one person in fifty tests positive.

Step 2: apply Bayes

P(D | +) = P(+ | D)P(D) / [ P(+ | D)P(D) + P(+ | Dc)P(Dc) ] The numerator is the ill-and-flagged road on its own. The denominator is every flagged road together, which is the P(+) just built. Read the whole thing as a share: of all the alarms, what fraction came from someone actually ill? That reading is worth more than the formula, because it makes the answer's size unsurprising once the two road weights are known.
  1. Substitute. P(D | +) = 0.000099 / (0.000099 + 0.019998).
  2. Evaluate the denominator. = 0.000099 / 0.020097.
  3. Divide. ≈ 0.00493.
AnswerP(D | +) ≈ 0.493%, which the notes gloss as about 1 in 203. A positive result on this test leaves the person about ninety-nine and a half percent likely to be healthy.
Where this goes wrongThe dominant error is answering 99 percent or 98 percent, by quoting one of the test's own accuracy figures back. Both describe the test's behaviour inside a known population, and the patient is not in a known population: that is exactly what they are trying to find out. The second error is ignoring the derived sensitivity and using the 1 percent miss rate directly in the numerator. The third is reading "2 percent false positives" as "2 percent of positives are false", which reverses the conditional; it means 2 percent of healthy people are flagged, and since healthy people are almost everybody, that 2 percent is nearly twenty thousand of them.

The same story three times, at three different priors

This session asked one question three times with the numbers changed, and putting the three answers next to each other is the most useful thing on this page.

The third row has by far the strongest evidence and by far the smallest answer. That is not a contradiction, and the middle column is the reason: evidence multiplies, priors anchor. A test that lifts belief forty-nine-fold is a genuinely powerful instrument, and applied to a one-in-ten-thousand starting point it still lands under one percent.

Where the course has met this beforeLecture 4, Parts 1 to 4 worked a screening test with a prevalence of 0.01, a sensitivity of 0.99 and a specificity of 0.95, and reached 0.17. This question keeps the same sensitivity, improves the specificity from 0.95 to 0.98, and drops the prevalence by a factor of a hundred. The answer falls from 0.17 to 0.00493, a factor of about thirty-four. The better test did not save it. Comparing the two examples is this course's own note; the tutorial gives only the second.

A check you can run without redoing the work

  1. Count in people rather than probabilities. Out of a million: 100 ill, 99 flagged; 999,900 well, 19,998 flagged. 99 over 20,097 is about 1 in 203, matching 0.493 percent.
  2. The complement must work. P(well | positive) ≈ 0.99507, and the two add to one.
  3. Sanity-check the direction. A positive result must raise the chance of illness above the prior of 0.0001, and it does, to 0.00493. If a Bayes calculation ever moves belief the wrong way relative to the evidence, the conditional has been read backwards.

Check yourself

Why is the answer so small when the test catches 99 percent of real cases?
Two percent of 999,900 healthy people is 19,998 false positives, against 99 true ones from the hundred who are ill, so the false alarms outnumber the real ones by about two hundred to one. The test is not inaccurate; 99 percent sensitivity and 98 percent specificity are both good, and the answer would be high if the disease were common. The disease is rare rather than common, at one in ten thousand. And the one percent miss rate costs only one case in a hundred and is nearly irrelevant to the answer.
Lecture 4's screening test gave 0.17 and this one gives 0.00493. What mainly changed?
The sensitivity is 0.99 in both examples, and the specificity actually improved from 0.95 to 0.98, so the only substantial change is the prevalence, which fell by a factor of a hundred and took the answer down by about thirty-four. Sensitivity did not fall. Specificity did not fall either; it rose, which pushed the answer up slightly and was overwhelmed. And the population size is a presentational choice: the answer is a ratio and does not depend on whether you count in thousands or millions.
Your teacher. Do the million-person count by hand for any screening claim you ever meet, because the probabilities hide what the counts make obvious. Write the population, split it by the base rate, apply each error rate to its own half, and then read the answer off the two columns of flagged people. It takes a minute, needs no formula, and it is the single most valuable habit in this entire session. Every one of the three Bayes questions here yields to it, and so does every headline about a test, a screening programme or a fraud detector that quotes an accuracy figure without a base rate.
