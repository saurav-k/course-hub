Three axioms, and four rules you never have to memorise

10 min working Lecture 2 established three axioms. Everything on this page is squeezed out of them, one short derivation at a time.

The one-minute version

  • The axioms say probabilities are never negative, the whole space has probability 1, and disjoint events add.
  • From those three lines alone: the empty event has probability 0, complements sum to 1, an event inside another is no more likely, and overlapping events add with the overlap subtracted.
  • None of these is a new assumption. Each one is a two-line consequence, and this page works all four.

1. The three axioms, named in words

Lecture 2 built a probability space: a sample space Ω of all possible outcomes, events as sets of outcomes, and a probability measure P that assigns each event a number. Three axioms pin that assignment down. In words, with no symbols yet:

  1. Non-negativity. No event has a negative probability. P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A.
  2. The whole space is certain. P(Ω) = 1, where Ω is the sample space, the set of every outcome that can possibly occur.
  3. Disjoint events add. If A and B are disjoint, meaning they share no outcome, so A ∩ B = ∅, then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B). The symbol ∪ is the union, "A or B", and ∩ is the intersection, "A and B".

That is the entire input for this page. Notice what is not an axiom: nothing so far says what happens when two events overlap, what the complement is worth, or what probability the empty event carries. Those all arrive now, as consequences.

2. The empty event has probability zero

The empty event ∅ is the event that nothing happens: the set with no outcomes in it. The axioms never mention it directly, so derive it. Apply the disjoint-adds axiom to the pair ∅ and ∅. They are certainly disjoint, and their union is ∅ again, so:

P(∅) = P(∅ ∪ ∅) = P(∅) + P(∅) P is the probability measure; ∅ is the empty event, the set containing no outcomes at all. The first equality holds because the union of the empty set with itself is itself; the second is the disjoint-adds axiom, which applies because a set is disjoint from itself only when it has nothing in it.

A number that equals itself plus itself can only be zero. Subtract P(∅) from both sides and P(∅) = 0. Nothing here was assumed; the axiom did all the work.

3. The complement rule

The complement of A, written Ac and read "A complement", is the event that A does not happen: every outcome outside A. Now split the world. A and Ac are disjoint by construction, and together they cover everything, so their union is Ω. Apply the disjoint-adds axiom, then the certainty axiom:

P(A) + P(Ac) = P(A ∪ Ac) = P(Ω) = 1 A is any event; Ac is its complement, everything in the sample space Ω that lies outside A. The union A ∪ Ac is the whole sample space, because every outcome either is in A or is not. The last step is the second axiom, P(Ω) = 1.
P(Ac) = 1 − P(A) The complement rule. Read it aloud: the probability that A does not happen is one minus the probability that A happens. This is the rule you will use most often in this whole course.

4. Containment: the bigger event is never less likely

Say event A sits inside event B, written A ⊆ B: every outcome of A is also an outcome of B. "It rained" sits inside "it rained or it snowed". The claim is P(A) ≤ P(B), and the derivation is one split. Because A sits inside B, you can write B as A glued to the part of B outside A:

B = A ∪ (B ∩ Ac), and the two pieces are disjoint B ∩ Ac is the part of B that lies outside A. The union is disjoint because the first piece is inside A and the second is outside it, so no outcome can be in both.
P(B) = P(A) + P(B ∩ Ac) ≥ P(A) The first equality is the disjoint-adds axiom applied to the split above. The inequality holds because the second piece is an event, and the first axiom says no event has negative probability. So P(B) is P(A) plus something non-negative.

5. Two events that overlap: the addition rule

Now the case the disjoint axiom does not cover: A and B share outcomes. If you write P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) anyway, you have counted every outcome in the overlap twice, once as a member of A and once as a member of B. The fix is to subtract the overlap back off once:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) A ∪ B is the union, the event that at least one of A and B happens. A ∩ B is the intersection, the event that both happen. The subtraction removes the double count.

The two-line derivation, so you see it is the axioms again and not a new rule. First split A ∪ B into disjoint pieces, A and the part of B outside A. Second, split B itself into two disjoint pieces, the overlap A ∩ B and the part of B outside A, which makes P(B ∩ Ac) = P(B) − P(A ∩ B). Substitute the second into the first and the rule falls out.

6. A quick numeric check

Constructed for this page rather than taken from the notes: roll one fair die, so the sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} and each face has probability 1/6. Let A be "the face is even", {2, 4, 6}, and B be "the face is a multiple of three", {3, 6}.

  1. Price each event by counting outcomes. P(A) = 3/6, P(B) = 2/6, and the overlap A ∩ B = {6}, so P(A ∩ B) = 1/6.
  2. Apply the addition rule. P(A ∪ B) = 3/6 + 2/6 − 1/6 = 4/6.
  3. Check by counting directly. A ∪ B = {2, 3, 4, 6}, which is 4 faces out of 6. The rule and the count agree.
Where the notes are looseThe faculty notes list the complement rule, containment, and the addition rule one after another, as a recap, without the derivations. The derivations are short, and they are the entire point: they are what makes these four lines consequences rather than assumptions. This page works each one in full, because a rule you have derived once is a rule you never have to trust on authority again.

Check yourself

A and B are disjoint events, both with positive probability. What is P(A ∪ B)?
Disjoint means A ∩ B = ∅, and part 2 showed P(∅) = 0, so the general rule collapses to P(A) + P(B). The first option is not wrong as a formula, it is just unearned: its last term is zero, and the disjoint-adds axiom gets you there without it. The second has the sign backwards; you subtract an overlap, never add it, and here there is nothing to subtract anyway. The fourth describes a different idea entirely, multiplication, which arrives at Part 5 and needs independence, not disjointness.
Event A sits inside event B, and P(B) = 0.9. What values can P(A) take?
Containment gives P(A) ≤ P(B) = 0.9, and the non-negativity axiom gives P(A) ≥ 0, so the answer is the band from 0 up to 0.9. The first option has the inequality pointing the wrong way: A is the smaller event, so it cannot out-probability the one containing it. The third forgets the ceiling that containment imposes. The fourth confuses "sits inside" with "equals": A can be any subset of B, from the empty event up to all of B, and only the extreme case has P(A) = 0.9.
Your teacher. Close the page and derive the addition rule from the axioms on paper, without looking. If you can, you own the move that matters: split into disjoint pieces, apply the axiom, recombine. That exact move, done twice more with more pieces, is inclusion-exclusion on the next page.
