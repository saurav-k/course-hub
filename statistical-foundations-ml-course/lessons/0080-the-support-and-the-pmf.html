Lecture 6 · Part 3 of 14 · types of random variable

A discrete random variable lands on countably many points, and the PMF says how heavy each one is

working 11 min The first of the two functions that describe a random variable completely, drawn on the example the notes draw it on.

The one-minute version

  • The support of a random variable is the range of the function: the set of values it actually takes. The notes define it in exactly those words.
  • A random variable is discrete when its support is a countable set, and countable splits two ways: finite, or countably infinite.
  • The probability mass function is pX : R → [0, 1] with pX(x) = P(X = x). It answers one question at every real number: how much probability sits exactly here.
  • Its domain is the whole real line, not just the support. Off the support the answer is zero, and the notes write "zero everywhere else" beside the drawing to say so.
  • Two tosses of a coin, four equally likely outcomes, and the PMF that comes out is 1/4 at 0, 1/2 at 1, 1/4 at 2.

1. The support is just the range

The notes head this section "Types of RVs" and give the first type immediately: a discrete random variable is one whose support is a countable set. They then define support with an arrow: it is the range of the function corresponding to the random variable.

That is worth restating in plain English, because "support" sounds like a technical term and is not. Run through every outcome in the sample space, write down the value X gives on it, and throw away the duplicates. What is left is the support. It is a set of numbers, it lives on the real line, and it usually has far fewer members than the sample space does.

  • Three tosses of a coin, counting heads. The sample space has eight members. The support is {0, 1, 2, 3}, which has four.
  • Two dice, summed. The sample space has 36 members. The support is {2, 3, ..., 12}, which has eleven.

Both of those supports are finite. The notes are careful to say that countable is the wider condition, and it splits in two.

What "countable" is doing here. A set is countable when its members can be arranged in a list with a first, a second, a third and so on, even if the list never ends. The whole numbers are countable. So is every finite set. The real numbers between 0 and 1 are not, and that is precisely the case this definition excludes, which is why Part 11 has to build something different for it. Lecture 2, Part 4 met the same word when it asked for countable unions.

2. The PMF, symbol by symbol

The notes write the definition in two lines, and every piece of both lines needs naming.

pX : R → [0, 1] pX is the name of the function, lower case p for a mass and a subscript X saying which random variable it belongs to. Its domain is R, the whole real line, so it may be asked about any number at all. Its codomain is [0, 1], the closed interval from zero to one, because every answer it gives is a probability.
pX(x) = P(X = x) x is a real number you choose: the point you are asking about. X = x is the event described in Part 2, the set of outcomes on which X gives exactly x. P prices that event. So the whole line reads: the mass at x is the probability that the experiment produces an outcome on which X equals x.

Two consequences fall straight out and both matter.

  1. The domain is bigger than the support, on purpose. You may ask pX(1.5) for the head count of two tosses. The answer is a perfectly good zero, because no outcome gives one and a half heads. The notes make this explicit with the phrase zero everywhere else, written beside the drawing.
  2. The masses over the support add to one. Every outcome produces some value, so the events {X = x} across the support are disjoint and their union is the whole sample space. Adding disjoint probabilities is the axiom from Lecture 2, Part 5, and the total is P(Ω) = 1.
This course's derivationThe notes do not state that the masses add to one on this page. The two lines above derive it from the definition of the PMF plus the axioms this course already has, and it is stated here because every named shape later in the lecture is checked against it. It carries no key-number mark because the source does not assert it.

3. The example the notes draw

The notes use two tosses of a coin. The sample space is Ω = {HH, HT, TH, TT} and each of the four outcomes carries probability 1/4. Let X be the number of heads, so X is the same head-counting function as in Part 1 with n set to two.

  1. Evaluate X on all four outcomes. HH has two heads, HT has one, TH has one, TT has none. So the support is {0, 1, 2}.
  2. Collect the outcomes by value. Value 0 comes from one outcome, value 1 from two outcomes, value 2 from one outcome. Four outcomes in, three values out.
  3. Add the probabilities in each group. pX(0) = 1/4. pX(1) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. pX(2) = 1/4. The notes mark 1/4 and 1/2 on the vertical axis of their drawing.
  4. Everywhere else is zero. At −1, at 0.5, at 3, at every real number outside the support, the mass is zero.
  5. Check the total. 1/4 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 1, as section 2 requires.

The stems, the flat grey stretches, the wider x range and the labels on the negative side are this course's own drawing. Nothing is added to the mathematics by them; they are there because the phrase "zero everywhere else" is much easier to believe once you have seen it.

5. Check yourself

For the two-toss head count, what is the value of the PMF at the number 1.5?
The PMF has the whole real line as its domain, so it is defined at 1.5, and the event that X equals 1.5 is empty, so its probability is zero. Nothing is undefined here: that is exactly the point of writing the domain as R rather than as the support. No rounding happens anywhere in the definition, and inventing one would break the total, since the masses already add to one without 1.5 contributing. And a constant quarter across the line is impossible, because infinitely many quarters could not add to one.
Why does the value 1 carry twice the mass of the value 0 in this example?
HT and TH both give a head count of one, so their two quarters add, while only TT gives zero. Position in the support has nothing to do with it: the geometric shape in Part 9 has its largest mass at the leftmost point of an endless support. Comparing the values themselves is a category error, since the mass measures how often a value occurs, not how big it is. And the coin is fair here, since all four outcomes carry an equal quarter; the asymmetry comes entirely from the grouping.
Your teacher. The PMF is a histogram that has stopped being an approximation. A histogram of real data has bars whose heights depend on how many samples you drew and how wide you made the bins. A PMF has no samples and no bins: it is the exact weight the model puts on each value, and it would not change if you ran the experiment a billion times. When you meet a bar chart in this course, ask which of the two you are looking at. Confusing them is the most common way an engineer imports a wrong intuition from plotting libraries into probability.
