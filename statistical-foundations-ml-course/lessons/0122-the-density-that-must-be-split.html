An interval that hangs off the edge of the density has to be cut in two before you integrate

foundation 9 min Two intervals on the same uniform density. One is a subtraction and the other took the room ten minutes, because the answer is not where the arithmetic looks.

After this page

  • You can compute an interval probability from a density without guessing whether the constant applies.
  • You can say why an interval that leaves the support must be split rather than scaled.

Before you start: A density is not a probability, for what the height of the curve is and is not.

The one-minute version

  • For a continuous variable the mass at any single point is zero, and a probability is an area: P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = ∫ fX(x) dx from c to d.
  • The uniform on [0, 1] has density 1 on that interval and 0 everywhere else.
  • Inside the support the integral is a subtraction: P(1/2 ≤ X ≤ 3/4) = 1/4.
  • Off the edge it is not. P(−1/2 ≤ X ≤ 1/2) must be split at 0, and the answer is 1/2, not 1.
  • The reason is that the density is a different function on the two halves, and one integral cannot use two rules at once.

1. What the density gives you

The revision block gave three facts about a continuous random variable, and the third is the one that does the work.

P(X = x) = 0   and   P(c ≤ X ≤ d) = ∫cd fX(x) dx X is the continuous random quantity. x is one particular real number. fX is the probability density function, a height rather than a probability, which is why it may exceed 1. c and d are the two ends of the interval you are asking about, with c the smaller. The integral sign reads "the area under the height, between c and d", and that area is the probability.

The tutor made the contrast with the discrete case explicit: a mass function is a probability and therefore sits between 0 and 1, while a density is any non-negative real value, because it only becomes a probability once an interval has been swept out under it.

The uniform on [a, b] has density 1/(b − a) on the interval and 0 outside it. The block then fixed a and b at 0 and 1, which makes the height exactly 1 and takes the arithmetic out of the way, and that is the version worked below.

2. The interval that lies inside the support

  1. Write the question. P(1/2 ≤ X ≤ 3/4), with density 1 across the whole of [1/2, 3/4].
  2. Set up the integral. The area is ∫ from 1/2 to 3/4 of fX(x) dx, and on that whole stretch fX(x) = 1.
  3. Substitute the constant. The integral becomes ∫ from 1/2 to 3/4 of 1 dx, whose antiderivative is x.
  4. Evaluate at the two ends. [x] from 1/2 to 3/4 is 3/4 − 1/2.
  5. Read the answer. 1/4. On a uniform, an interval inside the support carries its own length as its probability, which is exactly what "uniform" is claiming.

3. The interval that hangs off the edge

Now the same question from minus 1/2 to 1/2. The room's first answer was 1, on the reasoning that the interval has length 1 and the density is 1. That is the trap, and the tutor drew the picture rather than arguing.

  1. Write the question. P(−1/2 ≤ X ≤ 1/2) = ∫ from −1/2 to 1/2 of fX(x) dx.
  2. Notice the obstacle. The density is not one function across that range. It is 0 on [−1/2, 0] and 1 on [0, 1/2], so no single substitution is legal.
  3. Split at the boundary. The additivity of the integral lets you cut at 0: the whole is ∫ from −1/2 to 0 of 0 dx, plus ∫ from 0 to 1/2 of 1 dx.
  4. Evaluate the first piece. The integrand is the constant 0, so its antiderivative is the same constant at both ends and the difference is 0.
  5. Evaluate the second piece. [x] from 0 to 1/2 is 1/2 − 0 = 1/2.
  6. Add them. 0 + 1/2 = 1/2.

The tutor also gave the general form for an interval whose left end falls outside: if c sits below a, the answer is (d − a)/(b − a) rather than (d − c)/(b − a). The support has clipped the interval, and the clipping happens before the division.

The zero piece was written and then read as the answerTwice in the room the first integral, the one over the empty half, was read as producing the 1/2. The tutor had to say it twice: the first term gives 0 and the second term gives 1/2. It is worth naming because the confusion is not about probability at all. It is about which of two stacked lines on a whiteboard belongs to which term, and it is the single most common way this calculation is got wrong on paper.

Chapter 5 of Ross, A First Course in Probability opens continuous random variables the same way, with the density defined by the interval integral rather than by any point probability.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why can the interval from minus a half to a half not be integrated in one step?
An integral needs one integrand, and here the density is 0 to the left of zero and 1 to the right, so the range has to be cut where the rule changes. The first option is false: a probability of one is perfectly legal. The second is not the reason either; a negative endpoint is fine when the density has one rule across the whole range. The third is false as stated, since this interval and the support are both one unit long.
A uniform on [2, 6]. What is the probability that X lands between 5 and 9?
The interval is clipped at the top of the support, so the live part is 5 to 6, of length 1, over a support of length 4, giving 1/4. The second option treats the interval as if the density continued past 6, which it does not. The third confuses clipping with certainty. The fourth uses the raw length of the requested interval and never checks the support at all, which is the mistake the page exists to break.

Practice about 8 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A bus is equally likely to arrive at any moment in the ten minutes after nine o'clock, so its arrival time is uniform on [0, 10] minutes past nine.

Find the probability it arrives between minute 8 and minute 14, and then between minute 3 and minute 7.

Hint

Draw the density as a rectangle of height 1/10 over 0 to 10, mark each interval underneath it, and shade only what sits under the rectangle.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the density. Uniform on [0, 10] means height 1/(10 − 0) = 1/10 on the interval and 0 outside it.
  2. Check the first interval. 8 to 14 leaves the support at 10, so it must be split there.
  3. Split and evaluate. From 8 to 10 the height is 1/10, giving (10 − 8)/10 = 2/10. From 10 to 14 the height is 0, giving 0.
  4. Add. The answer is 0.2.
  5. Check the second interval. 3 to 7 lies wholly inside [0, 10], so one integral does it: (7 − 3)/10 = 0.4.

Sanity check. Both answers must sit between 0 and 1, and they do. The first interval is six minutes long but scores only 0.2, which is what clipping should look like; the second is four minutes long and scores 0.4, which is its own length over ten. If the first had come out at 0.6 you used the raw length and forgot the support.

Carry this away

  • A density is a height, not a probability, and only an area under it is a probability.
  • Inside the support a uniform gives the interval its own length, divided by the length of the support.
  • An interval that leaves the support is split at the boundary, and the outside piece contributes nothing.
  • Run the support check first, because it is what decides which of the two calculations you are doing.

Your teacher. Never write an integral for a density before you have drawn the support on the axis and marked the interval underneath it. The drawing takes five seconds and it makes the split obvious rather than remembered. The exponential and the Gaussian are next in this course and both have supports that will clip an interval the same way, so the habit is worth more than the uniform is.
