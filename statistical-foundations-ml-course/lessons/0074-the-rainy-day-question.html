TA Session 2 · Part 5 of 7 · Question 25

Hot days are 40 percent of the year and 16 percent of the rainy ones

working 11 min Question 25. The first of three Bayes questions, and the gentlest: the prior is close to even, so the answer is surprising without being shocking.

The one-minute version

  • A city is hot with probability 0.4 and cold with probability 0.6. Rain falls on a hot day with probability 0.2 and on a cold day with probability 0.7.
  • Rain has two roads in, so the law of total probability prices both: P(R) = (0.2)(0.4) + (0.7)(0.6) = 0.08 + 0.42 = 0.50.
  • Bayes then turns the conditional around: P(H | R) = (0.2)(0.4) / 0.50 = 0.08 / 0.50 = 0.16.
  • So a rainy day is hot 16% of the time, against 40% for a day picked at random.
  • The notes give the reason in a sentence: rain is much more likely on cold days, so seeing rain is evidence of cold.

Question 25, restated

Assume a certain city X experiences a hot day with probability 0.4 and a cold day with probability 0.6. Each hot day experiences rain with probability 0.2, while a cold day experiences rain with probability 0.7. Given that a particular day is rainy, what is the probability that the day is hot?

Which theorem is in play, and which way the given numbers point

Name the three events, and then read carefully which direction each given probability runs, because every one of them points the opposite way to the question.

  • H is the event that the day is hot. P(H) = 0.4.
  • C is the event that the day is cold. P(C) = 0.6. Every day is one or the other, and the two probabilities add to one.
  • R is the event that the day is rainy. Its probability is not given and has to be built.

The two rain figures are conditionals with the temperature on the right of the bar: P(R | H) = 0.2 and P(R | C) = 0.7. They say how often it rains once you already know the temperature. The question asks for P(H | R), which has the temperature on the left. That reversal is what Bayes is for, and it is the whole difficulty of the question.

P(H | R) = P(R | H) P(H) / P(R) P(H | R) is what a person standing in the rain wants to know: given this weather, how likely is it hot? P(R | H) is what the climate record gives: on hot days, how often does it rain? The numerator is the probability of the road that is both hot and rainy, and the denominator is the probability of every rainy road put together. Read as a share: of all the rain, what fraction fell on hot days?

Step 1: build P(R) with the law of total probability

Rain can arrive in exactly two ways, since a day is either hot or cold and never both. Price each road and add, which is Part 3's weighted average with the weights being how common each temperature is.

  1. Price the hot road. Hot and then rainy: P(R | H)P(H) = (0.2)(0.4) = 0.08.
  2. Price the cold road. Cold and then rainy: P(R | C)P(C) = (0.7)(0.6) = 0.42.
  3. Add them, because the roads are disjoint. P(R) = 0.08 + 0.42 = 0.50.

Half of all days are rainy, and it is worth noticing where that came from: not from either given rain figure, but from the mix. Neither 0.2 nor 0.7 is anywhere near 0.5, and the answer sits between them because the weights pulled it there.

Step 2: apply Bayes

  1. Write the formula. P(H | R) = P(R | H)P(H) / P(R).
  2. Substitute. P(H | R) = (0.2)(0.4) / 0.50.
  3. Evaluate. = 0.08 / 0.50 = 0.16.
AnswerP(hot | rainy) = 0.16, that is 16%. The notes state it in exactly that form.

The notes add an interpretation, and it is the part worth carrying away: even though hot days occur 40 percent of the time, once we know it is raining the probability that it is hot drops to 16 percent, because rain is much more likely on cold days.

Where this goes wrongTwo errors, and the first is the classic. Answering 0.2, by reading "given a rainy day, how likely is it hot" as if it were the given figure P(R | H). That is the conditional running the other way and it answers "given a hot day, how likely is rain", which is a fact about hot days rather than about rainy ones. The second is forgetting to build P(R) and dividing by something that was given instead, usually 0.7 or 0.6. If a Bayes question hands you two conditional rates and two priors, the denominator is never among them: it is the sum of the two products.

A check you can run without redoing the work

  1. The two posteriors must add to one. The same machinery gives P(C | R) = 0.42 / 0.50 = 0.84, and 0.16 + 0.84 = 1. A rainy day is hot or cold and nothing else.
  2. The four leaves must add to one. 0.08 + 0.32 + 0.42 + 0.18 = 1.00, so no road was mispriced.
  3. The direction must feel right. Rain prefers cold days, so learning it rained should make hot less likely than its prior of 0.4. It did, falling to 0.16. Had the answer come out above 0.4 the arithmetic would have been reversed somewhere.

The third check is the one worth internalising, because it costs nothing and catches the commonest error. Ask which way the evidence should push before you compute, then confirm that it pushed that way.

Check yourself

Why is P(R) computed as 0.2 times 0.4 plus 0.7 times 0.6?
A day is hot or cold and never both, so rain reaches you along exactly two disjoint roads, and the law of total probability prices each as a rate times a weight and adds them. Rain and heat are emphatically not independent here: the rain rate is 0.2 on hot days and 0.7 on cold ones, which is what the whole question turns on. Nothing is being averaged in the plain sense; the two rates are weighted by how common each temperature is. And the two temperatures are not equally common, being 0.4 and 0.6.
Why does knowing the day is rainy push the chance of heat below its prior of 0.4?
Rain falls on 70 percent of cold days against 20 percent of hot ones, so rain is evidence for cold, and conditioning on it moves probability away from hot. That cold days are also more common is true and contributes, but on its own it would not move the posterior at all: if both rain rates were equal the answer would stay at 0.4 whatever the prior. There is no rule that conditioning lowers things; conditioning on a dry day would raise the chance of heat, to 0.32 over 0.50. And the rates do not average out, they differ sharply, which is exactly why the evidence carries weight.
Your teacher. Every Bayes question you will meet has the same tell: the numbers you are given all condition on the thing you are trying to find out, and the thing you can actually observe is on the wrong side of the bar. Get into the habit of writing the four given quantities down with their bars drawn explicitly, then writing the question's own bar underneath, and looking at which way each one points. Once you can see that every arrow runs backwards, reaching for Bayes stops being a decision and becomes the obvious next move.
