Two roads lead to a positive result, and the wrong one carries five times the traffic

working 11 min Before asking what a positive result means, ask how often one happens at all. The answer splits cleanly in two, and the split is the whole story.

The one-minute version

  • A person tests positive either because they are ill and the test caught it, or because they are well and the test misfired. Those two routes are disjoint and together they are everything.
  • The lecture writes exactly that as P(+) = P(D) P(+ | D) + P(Dc) P(+ | Dc) = 0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.05, which is the law of total probability from Lecture 3 with the partition being ill against well.
  • Working the two products out: the ill road contributes 0.0099 and the well road contributes 0.0495, so P(+) = 0.0594.
  • The false road is five times larger than the true one. Not because the test is bad, but because it is applied to ninety-nine times as many people.

1. The question this page answers

Part 1 left a question hanging: the patient wants P(D | +), and no number on the box gives it. Getting there needs Bayes rule, and Bayes rule needs a denominator: how likely is a positive result at all, across the whole population, before anybody knows who is ill?

That is a forward question rather than a backward one, and the forward direction is the direction all the given numbers already point in. So it can be answered directly.

2. Cutting the world into two slices

The law of total probability says: cut the world into slices that never overlap and together cover everything, price the event inside each slice, weight each by how big the slice is, and add. Here the cut is the simplest one available.

Ω = D ∪ Dc,   D ∩ Dc = ∅ Ω, read "omega", is the sample space: every person the screening programme could pick. D is the set of ill people in it and Dc is everybody else. The first line says the two together are the whole population; the second says nobody is in both. A pair like that is called a partition, and it is exactly what the law of total probability needs.

Now the event +, "the test came back positive", is split by that partition into two pieces that cannot both happen:

+ = (+ ∩ D) ∪ (+ ∩ Dc) Read the intersections aloud: + ∩ D is "positive and ill", and + ∩ Dc is "positive and well". Every positive result is one or the other and never both, so by the third axiom their probabilities simply add.

The lecture writes the sum in exactly this two-piece form, as P(+ ∩ D) + P(+ ∩ Dc), before turning each piece into a product with the multiplication rule.

3. The arithmetic, worked in full

The notes write the sum 0.01 × 0.99 + 0.99 × 0.05 and leave it there, moving straight on to use it inside Bayes rule. The three lines below are that compressed step written out. They are ordinary arithmetic on the lecture's own stated figures, not extra data.

  1. Price the ill road. The slice D holds 0.01 of the population, and 0.99 of that slice tests positive. So P(+ ∩ D) = 0.01 × 0.99 = 0.0099. In a population of ten thousand, that is 99 people.
  2. Price the well road. The slice Dc holds 0.99 of the population, and 0.05 of that slice tests positive anyway. So P(+ ∩ Dc) = 0.99 × 0.05 = 0.0495. In ten thousand people, that is 495 people.
  3. Add the two disjoint pieces. P(+) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594. Just under six alarms per hundred people screened, or 594 alarms per ten thousand.

Stop on the two products for a moment. Each one is a small number times a large one, and they land an order of magnitude apart in opposite directions from what intuition suggests. The road with the excellent per-person behaviour, 0.99, produces the smaller contribution, because its slice is tiny. The road with the rare mistake, 0.05, produces the larger one, because its slice is nearly the whole population.

4. The same thing in whole people

Probabilities are hard to hold in the head; counts of people are not. Multiplying every figure by ten thousand changes nothing mathematically and changes a great deal psychologically.   1. The two row totals. 10,000 × 0.01 = 100 ill, and 10,000 × 0.99 = 9,900 well.
  2. Splitting the ill row. 100 × 0.99 = 99 caught, and 100 × 0.01 = 1 missed.
  3. Splitting the well row. 9,900 × 0.05 = 495 false alarms, and 9,900 × 0.95 = 9,405 correctly cleared.
  4. Check the corners. 99 + 1 + 495 + 9,405 = 10,000, and the positive column is 99 + 495 = 594, which is 0.0594 of ten thousand exactly as computed above.
Why the counts came out wholeTen thousand was not chosen at random. It is the smallest population size that makes all four cells of this table whole people, because the smallest probability in the problem, P(D) × P(− | D) = 0.01 × 0.01, is one in ten thousand. Working in counts rather than probabilities is a legitimate technique and not a shortcut: it is the same arithmetic with a common factor of 10,000 carried through every term, and it cancels again the moment a ratio is taken. It is used here because 594 alarms of which 99 are real is a sentence a person can reason about, and 0.0594 is not.

5. Check yourself

Why does the well population contribute more positives than the ill population does?
Volume is the answer: 0.05 of 9,900 people beats 0.99 of 100 people, and the ratio 495 to 99 is exactly five. The test is not worse at clearing the well in any meaningful sense - it errs on 5 percent of them against 1 percent of the ill. Nothing makes a false positive intrinsically easier; the per-person chance of one is twenty times smaller. And the ordering of the two rates is a fact about this test, not a rule, and it would not on its own decide which road carries more traffic.
Which single change would cut the false-positive road roughly in half, leaving the true road alone?
The false road is P(D-complement) times the false-positive rate, and specificity 0.975 halves that rate from 0.05 to 0.025, taking 0.0495 down to about 0.0248 while the ill road is untouched. Raising sensitivity moves the true road only, and barely: 0.0099 becomes 0.00995. Screening more people scales both roads together and changes no probability at all. And changing how the result is reported changes the conversation, not the arithmetic.
