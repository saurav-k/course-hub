Lecture 6 · Part 8 of 14 · the binomial, revisited

The formula has not changed since Lecture 5, and calling it a distribution changes everything else

working 11 min The one page in this lecture where the mathematics is entirely familiar and the language is entirely new.

The one-minute version

  • The notes give the binomial as a named shape parameterised by (n, p), with support {0, 1, ..., n} and mass pX(k) = C(n, k) pk(1 − p)n−k.
  • That is character for character the formula Lecture 5, Part 5 built by counting patterns of errors. Nothing in it is new.
  • What is new is that it is now the PMF of a random variable. It has a support, a CDF, a shape you can draw, and a place in a family of named shapes.
  • Lecture 5 could answer one question: how likely is exactly three. This page can ask at most three, more than three, between two and four, and every other question Part 6 listed, with no new counting.
  • A binomial is also what you get by counting the ones among n independent Bernoulli variables, which is Part 7's switch repeated n times.

1. The definition, as the notes give it

The notes write the heading "Binomial Dist", then the phrase "parameterized by (n, p)", then two lines.

X ∈ {0, 1, ..., n} This is the support in the sense of Part 3: the values the variable actually takes. It runs from 0 to n inclusive, so it holds n + 1 values, and it is finite. Nothing outside it has any mass.
pX(k) = C(n, k) pk(1 − p)n−k, for k in {0, 1, ..., n} n is the first parameter, how many independent trials are run, fixed before anything happens. p is the second parameter, the probability that any single trial is a success, the same for all of them. k is the value being priced, how many successes. C(n, k) counts which k of the n trials were the successful ones, and it is a plain whole number carrying no probability. pk prices those k trials all succeeding and (1 − p)n−k prices the remaining trials all failing.

Every symbol there has been on the site since Lecture 5, Part 5, where the same expression arrived as the answer to a question about a classifier making errors on ten test examples. The notes here add exactly two things: the words binomial distribution, and the pair of parameters written as a pair.

2. What the change of language actually buys

It is fair to ask whether renaming an answer is worth a page. It is, and the table says why.

Lecture 5's languageThis lecture's language
What the object is A formula that answers one question. A random variable X, with a PMF that answers every question about it.
What you can ask How likely is exactly k. Anything else needs the counting argument re-run. Exactly k, at most k, more than k, between two values, all from Part 6 and one CDF.
What it is comparable to Nothing. It is a standalone calculation. Every other named shape in this lecture, because they are all PMFs on a support.
What you can do next Nothing yet. There is no object to take an average of. Take its expectation and its variance, which is the next lecture's whole subject.
How it is specified By re-telling the story of the trials each time. By two numbers, n and p. The story is compressed into the parameters.

The last row is the practical one. Once a shape is named and parameterised, a whole problem can be handed over in five words: "X is binomial with n = 10 and p = 0.2". Everything else follows from the definition, and nobody has to re-derive the counting argument.

3. The shape, drawn

Take the case n = 5 and p = 1/2, which keeps the arithmetic in exact fractions. Every mass has the same denominator, because pk(1 − p)n−k = (1/2)5 = 1/32 for every k, so the masses are just the binomial coefficients over 32.

  1. The coefficients. C(5, 0) through C(5, 5) are 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. Each one counts the patterns of five trials with that many successes.
  2. The common factor. With p = 1/2, both powers collapse into (1/2)5 = 1/32, which is the same for every value of k. That is the special case, not the general one.
  3. The masses. 1/32, 5/32, 10/32, 10/32, 5/32, 1/32.
  4. The check. The numerators add to 1 + 5 + 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 = 32, so the masses add to 32/32 = 1, as any PMF must.

4. What the two parameters do

Naming a shape is only useful if you know what its knobs do. The notes state the parameters and draw nothing, so here is the same n with three different values of p.

The exact masses at n = 5 are 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1 over 32 at p = 0.5; at p = 0.2 they are 0.3277, 0.4096, 0.2048, 0.0512, 0.0064 and 0.0003 to four places, and at p = 0.8 the same six numbers in reverse order. Each set adds to 1. The notes state no numbers for this shape and draw nothing, so none of these carries a key-number mark.

5. Where a binomial comes from

One more connection is worth drawing, and it uses only pages this course has already written. Part 7 gave the Bernoulli: one switch, on with probability p. Run n independent copies of that switch and count how many came out on. The count is a binomial.

It uses nothing beyond the definitions on those two pages plus the counting argument of Lecture 5, Part 5, which built the coefficient by choosing which trials succeed. The notes state the binomial PMF and move straight on to the geometric, with no mention of Bernoulli trials underneath it.

6. Check yourself

What did calling the binomial a distribution add that Lecture 5 did not already have?
The formula is unchanged, and what is new is that it is now the mass function of a random variable, which brings the support, the CDF and all four computations of Part 6 with it. The Lecture 5 version was already correct and complete as an answer to its own question. Both pages carry the same two parameters, n and p, and Lecture 5 used both of them. Lecture 5 proved the formula by counting patterns, so nothing was taken on trust there; if anything the debt ran the other way, since Lecture 1 had used the formula before it was built.
A binomial has n = 5 and p = 1/2. Why do all six masses share the denominator 32?
With p equal to one half, p to the k times one minus p to the n minus k is one half to the k plus n minus k, which is one half to the fifth for every k, so only the coefficient varies. The coefficients do total thirty-two, but that is why the masses add to one rather than why they share a denominator. A support of six values forces nothing about denominators, as the p equal to nought point two panel shows. And thirty-two is indeed the pattern count, but that fact only becomes the denominator because p is one half; at any other p the patterns are not equally likely, which is exactly the point of Lecture 5, Part 6.
Your teacher. This page is the moment the course stops solving problems and starts building vocabulary, and it is worth noticing the change of gear. Everything from here on has the same shape: a name, a support, a formula with parameters, and a picture. When the next four shapes arrive, read them in that order every time, and specifically read the support before the formula. The support tells you what kind of quantity you are looking at, and half the confusion between these shapes is a reader who never checked whether the answers were bounded, endless, or spread across an interval.
