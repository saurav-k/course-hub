TA Session 3 · Part 10 of 10 · Problem 16

Two things that almost always happen almost always happen together, and the shortfall at most doubles

working 11 min Problem 16 of the practice set, worked on the slow Saturday sitting. The only problem of the week whose answer is a bound rather than a number.

After this page

  • You can bound an intersection from below using inclusion-exclusion and the fact that a probability is at most one.
  • You can read the resulting bound as a statement about how error accumulates.

Before you start: Inclusion-exclusion on a million, for the identity this whole page rearranges.

The one-minute version

  • Two events A and B, each with probability above 1 − δ for a very small positive δ. Show that P(A ∩ B) is close to 1.
  • Start from inclusion-exclusion and rearrange it so the intersection is alone: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∪ B).
  • Bound each piece. The first two exceed 1 − δ. The union is a probability, so it is at most 1, and subtracting it costs at most 1.
  • Add up: P(A ∩ B) > 1 − 2δ.
  • Read it as an error budget. Each event may miss certainty by δ, and together they miss by at most the sum.

1. The question, restated in full

Consider two events A and B such that P(A) > 1 − δ and P(B) > 1 − δ, for very small δ > 0. Prove that P(A ∩ B) is close to 1. The problem comes from the course's practice set rather than from the tutorial's own question sheet, and it was worked on the slow Saturday sitting.

Nothing is assumed about how A and B relate. They are not given as independent, and they need not be, which is what makes the result useful: it holds for any two events at all.

2. Rearranging the identity

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∪ B) A and B are events, subsets of the sample space. A ∩ B, read "A intersect B", is the event that both happen. A ∪ B, read "A union B", is the event that at least one happens. δ, delta, is a small positive number measuring how far each event falls short of certainty. The identity is inclusion-exclusion with the intersection moved to the left.

Inclusion-exclusion is usually written the other way round, with the union alone on the left, and the reason for flipping it is simply that the intersection is what the question asks about. Nothing is added or removed; the same three quantities appear in both forms.

3. The bound, line by line

  1. Start from the union form. P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B), which holds for any two events.
  2. Isolate the intersection. Move the terms across: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∪ B).
  3. Bound the first term. The question gives P(A) > 1 − δ.
  4. Bound the second term. Likewise P(B) > 1 − δ.
  5. Bound the third term, carefully. The union is a probability, so 0 ≤ P(A ∪ B) ≤ 1. It enters with a minus sign, so replacing it by its largest value gives the smallest possible right-hand side: −P(A ∪ B) ≥ −1.
  6. Substitute all three. P(A ∩ B) > (1 − δ) + (1 − δ) + (−1).
  7. Simplify. The three whole numbers give 1 + 1 − 1 = 1, and the two deltas give −2δ. So P(A ∩ B) > 1 − 2δ.
  8. Finish the argument. If δ is very small then so is 2δ, so P(A ∩ B) is close to 1, which is what was asked.

Step 5 is the one to be careful with, and it is the only place a sign can go wrong. A quantity entering with a minus sign is bounded from above to make the whole expression small, which is the opposite of what the first two terms needed. Bounding the union below by 0 would have given the useless statement that the intersection exceeds 1 − 2δ plus one, which is impossible and signals the error immediately.

4. What the bound is for

Read δ as an error budget. If a component fails at most one time in a thousand, and a second, unrelated component also fails at most one time in a thousand, then both working together fails at most two times in a thousand. No knowledge of how the two failures relate is required, which is precisely why this bound is used in practice: it survives dependence.

The same shape extends: n events each above 1 − δ give an intersection above 1 − nδ. It is the same union bound that the Bonferroni problem in this course uses, written for intersections rather than unions, and it goes slack once nδ reaches 1, where it says only that the probability exceeds zero.

The inclusion-exclusion identity this rearranges is Chapter 2 of Ross, A First Course in Probability, and the union bound it generalises to is that chapter's Boole inequality.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the union term replaced by 1 rather than by 0 in the substitution?
Subtracting the largest possible union gives the smallest possible intersection, which is what a lower bound needs. The first option is false: a union is at most one and rarely exactly one. The third is wrong too, since a union of two impossible events would be zero. The fourth adds an assumption the problem never makes.
Five events each have probability above 0.99. What does this argument guarantee?
Here delta is 0.01 and there are five events, so the guarantee is above 1 minus 0.05, that is 0.95. The first option keeps the single-event figure, which is exactly what the bound exists to correct. The third and fourth both smuggle in an independence requirement, and the whole value of this argument is that it never needs one.

Practice about 8 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A deployment depends on three checks. Each passes with probability at least 0.998, and nothing is known about how they relate to one another.

Find the guaranteed lower bound on all three passing, then say how many such checks you could add before the guarantee becomes worthless.

Hint

Here delta is 0.002. Extend the two-event argument by applying it repeatedly, or use the n-event form directly.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name delta. Each check falls short of certainty by at most δ = 1 − 0.998 = 0.002.
  2. Apply the bound to the first two. Both pass with probability above 1 − 2(0.002) = 0.996.
  3. Fold in the third. Treat "the first two pass" as one event of shortfall 0.004 and the third as an event of shortfall 0.002, giving a total shortfall of 0.006.
  4. State the bound. All three pass with probability at least 0.994.
  5. Find where it fails. The guarantee is 1 − n(0.002), which reaches 0 at n = 500. From 500 checks onward the bound says only that the probability exceeds zero, which is no information at all.

Sanity check. The bound must fall as checks are added, and it does: 0.998, then 0.996, then 0.994. It must also stay below the weakest single check, and 0.994 is below 0.998. If your answer had risen with each extra check, you added the shortfalls with the wrong sign.

Carry this away

  • Rearrange inclusion-exclusion so the quantity you want stands alone, then bound each remaining piece.
  • A term entering with a minus sign is bounded from above, not below.
  • The shortfalls add: two events each within delta of certain give an intersection within two delta.
  • The bound needs no independence, which is exactly why it is worth having.

Your teacher. Whenever you bound an expression that has a subtraction in it, write beside each term whether you want it large or small, before you substitute anything. Two words in the margin catch the only error this problem can produce, and the same habit is what keeps every later tail inequality in this course pointing the right way.
