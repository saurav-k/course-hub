The mean answers to size; the median answers to how often

12 min expectation and a binomial Part 2 showed that the two summaries disagree. This page shows exactly why, and puts a probability on how hard each one is to fool.

The one-minute version

  • Assume a day is Rs 500 with probability 0.9 and Rs 50,000 with probability 0.1. The expected value is 5,450, which is more than ten times what happens on nine days out of ten.
  • The mean is pulled by magnitude. One rare, enormous value moves it, and it moves further the bigger that value is.
  • To drag the median of ten days up to 50,000 you would need at least six of the ten days to be extreme.
  • The chance of that, under the same model, is about 0.01%. That is the price of fooling the median, and it is why the deck adopts 415 as the behavioural baseline.

1. Switching engines on purpose

Something changes on slide 7, and it is worth naming because it is the first time this lecture uses the machinery from Part 1 deliberately. Up to now we ran the backward engine: ten observed days went in, and a summary came out. Slide 7 turns around and runs the forward engine. It invents a model, states it out loud, and then asks what that model implies. No new data is collected. The model does all the work.

The model is deliberately crude, and that is fine, because it is being used to isolate one effect rather than to describe a real user.

Daily spend X = 500 with probability 0.90, or 50,000 with probability 0.10 Read this as "nine days in ten are quiet at Rs 500, and one day in ten is a Rs 50,000 event". It is the ten-day table from Part 2 with the noise stripped out and the contrast turned up.

2. Expectation, worked in full

The expected value of X, written E[X] and read aloud as "the expectation of X", is the long-run average of X if you could watch this process for a very long time. Mechanically it is a weighted average: take each possible value, multiply it by how likely that value is, and add the results.

  1. Weight the quiet outcome. 0.90 × 500 = 450. Nine days in ten contribute 450 to the average between them.
  2. Weight the extreme outcome. 0.10 × 50,000 = 5,000. One day in ten contributes 5,000, which is more than eleven times what the other nine contribute together.
  3. Add the two contributions. E[X] = 450 + 5,000 = 5,450.

Sit with step 2 for a moment, because it is the whole point. The rare outcome is ten times less likely, but a hundred times bigger, and a hundred beats ten. Rarity divides its influence by ten; magnitude multiplies it by a hundred. The mean has no defence against that trade, because the mean's definition invites the magnitude straight into the sum.

3. What it would take to fool the median

The mean can be moved by a single value, so asking "how likely is that" is not interesting: it happened, once, in ten days. The median is a different proposition, and slide 8 asks the right question about it. Not "can it be moved" but "how much of the data would have to conspire to move it".

With ten sorted values, the median is the average of the 5th and the 6th. To drag both of those up to 50,000, the six largest positions must all be filled by extreme days, so you need at least six of the ten days to be extreme. Five is not enough: with five quiet and five extreme, the 5th sorted value is still the largest quiet day, and the median lands halfway between the two groups rather than up at 50,000.

Now switch to the forward engine again and ask how likely six-or-more is. Under the model, each day is independently extreme with probability p = 0.1, and there are n = 10 days. Counting how many successes happen in a fixed number of independent trials is exactly what the binomial distribution describes.

P(X ≥ 6) = ∑k=610 C(10, k) (0.1)k (0.9)10−k C(10, k), the binomial coefficient, is the number of different ways to choose which k of the 10 days are the extreme ones. (0.1)k is the chance those k days are all extreme, and (0.9)10−k is the chance the remaining days are all quiet. The ∑ adds up every case from six extreme days through to all ten.
  1. Count the arrangements for exactly six. C(10, 6) = 10! / (6! × 4!) = 210. There are 210 different ways to pick which six of the ten days blow up.
  2. Price one such arrangement. (0.1)6 = 0.000001, and (0.9)4 = 0.6561. Multiplying, one specific arrangement has probability 0.000000656.
  3. Multiply by the count. 210 × 0.000001 × 0.6561 = 0.000137, matching the deck's P(X = 6).
  4. Do the same for exactly seven. C(10, 7) = 120, (0.1)7 = 0.0000001, (0.9)3 = 0.729, giving 120 × 0.0000001 × 0.729 = 0.000009, again matching the deck.
  5. Notice the collapse. Each extra extreme day costs another factor of about ten, so the terms for eight, nine, and ten days are smaller again by roughly that much each time and contribute almost nothing.
  6. Add them. The deck reports the total as ≈ 0.01%. Adding the terms gives about 0.000147, which is roughly one chance in seven thousand.

4. Magnitude against frequency

Both summaries can be broken. The difference is the bill. Breaking the mean costs one unusual number, and it is not even a particularly unusual number: the more extreme it is, the cheaper the attack becomes. Breaking the median costs a majority of the dataset, and no single value, however enormous, gets you closer to that majority.

What robustness costsRobustness is not free, and this lecture does not have room to say so, which makes it worth saying here. Because the median throws away magnitude, it also throws away information. On clean, symmetric data with no outliers, the mean is the more efficient estimator: it uses more of what the data knows and wobbles less from sample to sample. The right rule is not "always use the median", it is "use the median when a small number of extreme values would otherwise decide the answer". On the ten days in Part 2, they would.

5. Check yourself

Day 5 is re-checked and it was actually Rs 85,000, not Rs 8,500. What happens to the two summaries of the original ten days?
The total gains 76,500, so the mean gains 7,650 and lands near 8,875. The sorted list is unchanged except in its last position, so the 5th and 6th values are still 410 and 420 and the median is still exactly 415. The second option reverses the two behaviours. The third is wrong because the median uses only the rank of the largest value, never its size. The fourth is wrong for the mean, which a single day demonstrably does move.
Slide 8 computes P(X ≥ 6) rather than P(X ≥ 1). Why is six the number that matters for ten days?
The median of ten sorted values is the average of the 5th and the 6th, so both of those positions have to be filled from the extreme group before the median moves up to join it, and that takes six values. Five leaves the 5th position holding the largest quiet day. The model permits up to ten extreme days, not six. The observed sample had one extreme day, not six. And the mean has no threshold at all, which is exactly the property being contrasted.
Your teacher. The reusable idea on this page is not the median. It is the question "how much of my data would have to be wrong before this number lies to me". Ask it of any metric you rely on. Some collapse under one bad row; others need half the table to be wrong. Knowing which is which is most of what robustness means in practice. It is also the exact question a serving team asks of a p50 latency: how many slow requests can the traffic absorb before the p50 stops describing anybody's experience - and the sorted-positions picture above answers it the same way.
