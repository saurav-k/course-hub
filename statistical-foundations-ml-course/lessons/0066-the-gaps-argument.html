Lecture 5 · Part 8 of 10 · the consecutive-errors board

Put the correct results down first, and the errors have only the gaps between them to live in

11 min the cleverest count in the lecture A conditional question whose probabilities cancel completely, leaving a pure count against a pure count.

The one-minute version

  • Given that the classifier made exactly three errors, how likely is it that at least two of them landed on consecutive test examples?
  • Counting the adjacent arrangements directly is awkward. Counting the non-adjacent ones is easy, and then the complement finishes it.
  • Three errors means 7 correct results. Lay those seven down first. They create 8 gaps, one before the first, one after the last, and six in between.
  • No two errors are adjacent exactly when no gap holds more than one error, so the non-adjacent arrangements number C(8, 3).
  • Every three-error pattern carries the same probability, so it cancels top and bottom, and the answer is a ratio of counts: 1 − C(8, 3) / C(10, 3) = 1 − 56/120 = 8/15.

1. The question, and the two events in it

The problem. Given that the classifier makes exactly three errors on the ten test examples, what is the probability that at least two of the errors occur on consecutive test examples?

The handout names the two events, and naming them is most of the work.

  • A is the event that at least two errors occur consecutively, meaning somewhere in the string of ten letters two E's sit side by side.
  • B is the event that X = 3, exactly three errors in all.

The question asks for Pr(A | B), read "the probability of A given B", which is Lecture 3, Part 4's definition applied to a new pair of events.

Pr(A | B) = Pr(A ∩ B) / Pr(B) A ∩ B, read "A and B", is the event that both happen: exactly three errors, and at least two of them adjacent. Pr(B) is the probability of exactly three errors, already worked out on Part 5 as C(10, 3)(0.2)3(0.8)7. Conditioning on B means throwing away every evaluation that did not produce exactly three errors and asking what fraction of what remains has an adjacent pair.

2. Why the complement again, and what it is here

Counting the three-error patterns that do contain an adjacent pair is unpleasant, because a pattern might have exactly one adjacent pair, or all three errors in a block, and those cases would need separating and then recombining without double counting.

Counting the patterns with no two errors adjacent turns out to be easy, and there is a standard picture for it. Note that this is the third distinct use of the complement in this lecture, on a third kind of event, which is not a coincidence: it is what "at least" almost always calls for.

3. The gaps picture

Here is the move. Do not try to place the errors into ten positions while watching for collisions. Instead, lay the seven correct results down first, in a row, and then look at where an error could be slotted in.

Seven items in a row create eight places an eighth item could go: before the first, between the first and second, and so on, and after the last. Those places are the gaps.

Two facts make the correspondence exact, and both are worth stating rather than assuming.

  1. Every gap choice gives a non-adjacent pattern. If the three errors go into three different gaps, then between any two of them stands at least one correct result, so no two errors touch.
  2. Every non-adjacent pattern comes from one gap choice. Take any pattern with three isolated errors, delete the errors, and read off which gaps of the remaining seven C's they occupied. That reading is unique.
  3. So the count is a choice of three gaps out of eight, unordered and without repeats, which is case 3 again: C(8, 3).
  4. Evaluate it. C(8, 3) = 8! / (3! × 5!) = (8 × 7 × 6) / 6 = 56.

4. From the count to the answer

There are C(10, 3) = 120 three-error patterns in all, and 56 of them have no two errors adjacent, so the rest have at least one adjacent pair. That subtraction is the count the question actually wants.

  1. Patterns with at least two consecutive errors. C(10, 3) − C(8, 3), which is 120 − 56 = 64.
  2. Price them. Each such pattern has exactly three errors and seven correct results, so each is worth (0.2)3(0.8)7, and Part 7's rule applies: count times shared weight. So Pr(A ∩ B) = (C(10, 3) − C(8, 3)) (0.2)3(0.8)7.
  3. Divide by Pr(B). The denominator is C(10, 3) (0.2)3(0.8)7 from Part 5.
  4. Cancel. The factor (0.2)3(0.8)7 appears in both, so it goes, leaving (C(10, 3) − C(8, 3)) / C(10, 3) = 1 − C(8, 3) / C(10, 3).
  5. Put the numbers in. 1 − 56/120 = 8/15.
Pr(A | B) = 1 − C(8, 3) / C(10, 3) = 1 − 56/120 = 8/15 C(8, 3) = 56 counts the three-error patterns with no adjacent pair and C(10, 3) = 120 counts all three-error patterns. Their ratio is the share of three-error evaluations that are cleanly spread out, and one minus it is the share that bunch. The error rate 0.2 has vanished entirely from the answer, which is the point of the next section.
This course's arithmeticThe handout states 56, 120 and 8/15, all of which are quoted above. As a decimal, 8/15 is 0.5333. So given that a classifier produced exactly three errors, slightly more than half the time at least two of them will be neighbours. That decimal is this course's own and carries no key-number mark.

5. Why the probabilities cancelled, and why that is not a fluke

Something worth pausing on happened in step 4. The error rate disappeared. The answer 8/15 would be the same for a classifier with an error rate of 0.2, 0.5 or 0.001, which sounds too good to be true and is not.

The reason is Part 6 read in reverse. Inside the conditional world of B, every remaining outcome has exactly three errors, so every remaining outcome carries exactly the same weight. Conditioning on B produced a space that really is equally likely, and on such a space the favourable-over-total formula is legal again.

6. Check yourself

Why does the factor 0.2 cubed times 0.8 to the seventh cancel in this conditional?
Both the numerator and the denominator are a count multiplied by the same shared weight, since every pattern in either of them has exactly three errors, so the weight divides out and only the counts remain. Errors and correct results are not equally likely: 0.2 and 0.8 are different numbers, and the cancellation works despite that, not because of it. Conditional probabilities very often do depend on the underlying rates; this one does not, and the reason is specific to conditioning on the error count. And the two count different sets: 64 patterns above and 120 below.
Why do seven correct results create eight gaps rather than six or seven?
Seven items in a row have six spaces between them plus the two ends, giving eight places an error can go, and the two ends matter because an error at the very start or the very end is still not adjacent to another error. Counting only the interior spaces gives six and would wrongly exclude patterns such as an error on the first test example. There are ten examples but only eight gaps, so the two counts are not the same thing. And the eight free positions is a restatement of the answer rather than a reason for it, and it is also not what a gap is, since one gap can be chosen without any position being fixed in advance.
Your teacher. The gaps trick generalises far beyond this problem, and it is worth storing under a slogan: place the things that may touch first, then slot the things that may not into the spaces between them. It answers "no two people from the same team sit together", "no two heads in a row", "no two failures back to back in a retry sequence" and a dozen more. The count is always C(gaps, items), and the number of gaps is always one more than the number of things you laid down first. That last sentence is the whole trick.
