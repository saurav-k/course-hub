Conditioning does not change the world, it shrinks it and rescales what is left

theory 11 min The one part of the session that is not a question. Both days taught it, word for word, and everything from Part 4 onwards runs on it.

The one-minute version

  • Conditioning shrinks the sample space. Once F is known to have happened, F is the world, and E can only happen inside E ∩ F.
  • So the answer is the size of E ∩ F measured against F rather than against everything: P(E | F) = P(EF) / P(F).
  • If E and F are independent, then P(EF) = P(E)P(F), the P(F) cancels, and P(E | F) = P(E). Learning F was worth nothing.
  • Splitting E by whether F happened gives the law of total probability: P(E) = P(E|F)P(F) + P(E|Fc)[1 − P(F)].
  • Turning the conditional around gives Bayes: P(F | E) = P(E | F)P(F) / P(E). Parts 5, 6 and 7 are three uses of that one line.

What the notes actually say

The definition is stated in words before it is stated in symbols, and the wording is the teaching. The conditional probability of E given that F has occurred is written P(E | F). If the event F occurs, then in order for E to occur it is necessary that the actual occurrence be a point in both E and F, that is, in EF. Since we know F has occurred, F becomes the new, reduced sample space, and the probability that EF occurs equals the probability of EF relative to the probability of F.

P(E | F) = P(EF) / P(F) E and F are any two events. EF is the notes' shorthand for E ∩ F, the event that both happen. P(E | F) is read "the probability of E given F". The formula needs P(F) to be greater than zero, since a world that cannot happen cannot be conditioned on. Everything on the right is measured in the original space; the division is what re-expresses it in the shrunken one.

What independence does to it

The notes follow the definition immediately with the independent case, because that is where the formula pays out in intuition rather than arithmetic.

  1. Start from independence. If E and F are independent then, by the product test, P(E)P(F) = P(EF).
  2. Substitute into the conditional. P(E | F) = P(EF) / P(F) = P(E)P(F) / P(F).
  3. Cancel. P(E | F) = P(E).
  4. And symmetrically. P(F | E) = P(F).

Read the last two lines as a sentence: knowing F happened does not change what you believe about E, and knowing E happened does not change what you believe about F. That is what independence means in practice, and it is a better working definition than the product test, which is merely the version you can compute with. Part 1 of this session is the computable version; this is what it was telling you.

Splitting E in two: the law of total probability

The notes then build the second formula from the first, and the construction is three lines with one axiom in the middle.

  1. Split E by whether F happened. E = EF ∪ EFc. Every occurrence of E either came with F or came without it, and there is no third option.
  2. Note the two halves are mutually exclusive. They cannot both happen, since F either occurred or did not, so the third axiom lets their probabilities be added: P(E) = P(EF) + P(EFc).
  3. Rewrite each half with the multiplication rule. P(E) = P(E|F)P(F) + P(E|Fc)P(Fc).
  4. Replace the last factor. Since P(Fc) = 1 − P(F), the notes box the final form: P(E) = P(E|F)P(F) + P(E|Fc)[1 − P(F)].

The notes then say in words what the equation means, and it is worth quoting the shape of it: the probability of E is a weighted average of the conditional probability of E given that F occurred and the conditional probability of E given that F did not, with each conditional given as much weight as the event it is conditioned on has of occurring.

Turning the conditional around: Bayes

The two conditionals, P(E | F) and P(F | E), are built from the same intersection over different divisors. Writing both and eliminating the intersection gives the formula the notes label the standard Bayes formula.

  1. Write both directions. P(E | F) = P(EF)/P(F) and P(F | E) = P(EF)/P(E).
  2. Solve the first for the intersection. P(EF) = P(E | F)P(F).
  3. Substitute into the second. P(F | E) = P(E | F)P(F) / P(E).
  4. Expand the denominator if you have to. P(E) is usually not given directly, so it comes from the law of total probability above, which is exactly how Parts 5, 6 and 7 proceed.

Where this goes wrongTwo reversals, and both are the same reversal. Confusing P(E | F) with P(F | E) is the error the whole of Bayes exists to fix, and it is why the standard formula is worth memorising in the direction that has the given on the left of the numerator. The other is reading the law of total probability as an average of two numbers rather than a weighted one: if F happens only one percent of the time, the P(E | F) term contributes almost nothing to P(E), however dramatic that conditional is. Part 7 is that sentence with numbers attached.

Check yourself

Why does conditioning on F divide by P(F) rather than leaving the intersection alone?
Once F is known to have occurred it becomes the sample space, and a sample space must carry total probability one, so everything inside it is rescaled by dividing by P(F). That P(F) is at most one makes the conditional larger than the raw intersection, but "dividing helps" is a description of the effect rather than a reason. The intersection being small is true and irrelevant. And the multiplication rule is a consequence of this definition rather than its justification, so using it here would be circular.
Why may P(EF) and P(EF-complement) simply be added to give P(E)?
F either happened or it did not, so the two pieces cannot overlap, and between them they cover every way E can occur: disjoint plus exhaustive is exactly the condition the third axiom needs. Independence is a different relation and is not what licenses addition; the third axiom asks for disjointness. The two pieces are generally quite unequal, and nothing forces them to match. And they are not complements of each other: their union is E, not the whole space.
Your teacher. Say the reduced-space sentence out loud once and you will not need to memorise the formula again: F happened, so F is the world now, and I want E's share of it. Every conditional probability you will ever compute is that sentence. The division by P(F) is not a step in a procedure, it is the act of changing what you are measuring against, and the four questions that follow are all easier once you are asking "share of what?" before you write anything down.
