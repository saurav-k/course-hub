A density can be larger than 1, because only its area is ever a probability

working 12 min Implications 2 and 3 of the definition, and the point a student pushed back on hardest in the session.

After this page

  • You can say why the total area under any density is exactly 1, starting from the CDF.
  • You can turn a question about an interval into an area, in either the CDF form or the integral form.
  • You can explain to somebody else why a density of 2 breaks nothing.

Before you start: the definition of a continuous random variable, because every line on this page is read off it.

The one-minute version

  • Implication 2: the CDF tends to 1 as x grows, so the density integrated over the whole line is exactly 1.
  • Implication 3: P(a < X ≤ b) = FX(b) − FX(a), and the two integrals collapse into one integral from a to b.
  • So a probability is an area, and the two implications are the same statement at two different widths.
  • The height is not a probability and carries no ceiling of 1. A uniform on [0, 0.5] has height 2 throughout.
  • The cost: the closed-interval version of implication 3 quietly borrows a result that does not arrive until Part 8.

1. The total area is 1, and the CDF is why

The lecturer did not assert this one. He asked the class what happens to the CDF as x runs off to the right, got the answer, and then pushed x to infinity inside the integral.

limx→∞ FX(x) = limx→∞ P(X ≤ x) = 1, so ∫−∞∞ fX(z) dz = 1 Read as: limx→∞ means "the value this approaches as x is pushed further and further to the right". FX(x) is the CDF, the probability that X lands at or below x. P(X ≤ x) is the same thing written out. fX is the density. z is the dummy integration variable. The limits −∞ and ∞ mean the integral now covers the entire real line. The line says: the CDF has to arrive at 1 eventually, and the CDF is the accumulated area, so the whole area is 1.

Note which fact is doing the work. That a CDF tends to 1 was proved back in Lecture 6, Part 5, for every random variable and with no assumption of discreteness. This page inherits it and translates it into the new language.

2. An interval is an area, in two steps

The second implication starts from a difference of CDF values that Lecture 6 already established, and the whole move is to substitute the integral form into it and cancel.

  1. Start from the CDF difference. P(a < X ≤ b) = FX(b) − FX(a). This is not new; it holds for any random variable at all.
  2. Substitute the definition twice. FX(b) is the integral from −∞ to b of fX(z) dz, and FX(a) is the integral from −∞ to a of the same thing.
  3. Subtract. Both integrals start at −∞, so everything to the left of a appears in both and cancels.
  4. Read what is left. P(a < X ≤ b) = ∫ from a to b of fX(z) dz. The strip of area between the two thresholds, and nothing else.

This is the working formula of the whole subject. Almost every question anyone asks about a continuous quantity turns into it: pick two thresholds, take the area between them.

3. Why the height is free, and the area is not

A student interrupted here, twice, and was asked to hold the question until the implication was finished. When it came, it was the right question: can the density really be greater than 1? The answer was yes, and that examples later in the lecture would show it.

Here is such an example now, one page early, worked out rather than promised. Take a variable spread evenly over the interval from 0 to 0.5 and nowhere else. The uniform density on an interval is 1 divided by the width of the interval, which is the constant the normalisation forces.

  1. Write the general uniform density. On the interval from a to b, f(x) = 1 ÷ (b − a), and 0 outside it.
  2. Put in the numbers. Here a = 0 and b = 0.5, so f(x) = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2 on the whole interval.
  3. Check implication 2. The area is a rectangle: height 2 × width 0.5 = 1. Legal.
  4. Ask for a real probability. P(0.1 < X ≤ 0.3) = the area of the strip = 2 × 0.2 = 0.4.
  5. Compare the two numbers. A height of 2, and a probability of 0.4. The number above 1 was never a probability, and the number that is one came out below 1 on its own.
This course's exampleThe lecture promises that a density can exceed 1 and defers the demonstration to the named distributions later in the session. The uniform on [0, 0.5] above, its height of 2, and the probability 0.4 are this course's own arithmetic, worked from the general uniform density that the lecture states on class-notes page 4 and that the NIST handbook gives as f(x) = 1/(B − A). None of these three numbers is the lecturer's.

4. The units settle the whole confusion

A probability is a pure number. A density is a probability per unit of x, in the way that speed is distance per unit of time. Nobody objects that a car can travel at 60 miles per hour without travelling 60 miles, and the density objection has the same shape.

Implication 3 borrows from Part 8What the lecture derives is the half-open form, P(a < X ≤ b), because that is the interval the CDF difference gives directly. The fully closed form on class-notes page 4 is written as P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X ≤ b) + P(X = a), and it only collapses to the same integral because P(X = a) = 0 - which is implication 4, one page later. Nothing here is wrong, and the order is worth noticing: taken alone, implication 3 does not yet license swapping a strict inequality for a non-strict one. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, Introduction to Probability, Section 3.1, states the four equal forms only after establishing P(X = a) = 0, in that order.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Where does the fact that a density integrates to 1 actually come from?
The total area is the limit of the CDF, and every CDF approaches 1 on the right, so the area is 1. Non-negativity stops the area from ever falling but says nothing about where it stops rising. A ceiling of 1 on the density is false outright, as the uniform on nought to nought point five shows. And the interval formula is implication 3, which is derived from the CDF difference rather than the other way about.
A density is measured at 2 at some point. What follows?
A density is a probability per unit of x, so its height has no ceiling, and no probability appears until a height is multiplied by a width. There is no ceiling of 1 on a density to break. The total area stays fixed at 1 by implication 2 whatever the heights are, and a tall narrow density is exactly how that is arranged. And a mass really is a probability outright, which is why a mass is the thing that may not exceed 1.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A continuous variable has density f(x) = 3x² for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, and 0 elsewhere.

Find the largest value the density reaches, confirm the total area is 1, and compute P(0.5 < X ≤ 1).

Hint

The integral of 3x² is x³. Do the area check and the interval probability with the same antiderivative, and evaluate it at the two ends.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the peak. 3x² is increasing on [0, 1], so the largest value is at x = 1: f(1) = 3. That is well above 1 and perfectly legal.
  2. Check the total area. ∫ from 0 to 1 of 3x² dx = 1³ − 0³ = 1. It is a valid density.
  3. Take the interval. P(0.5 < X ≤ 1) = ∫ from 0.5 to 1 of 3x² dx = 1³ − 0.5³.
  4. Finish the arithmetic. 0.5³ = 0.125, so the answer is 1 − 0.125 = 0.875.

Sanity check. The density leans heavily to the right, so the upper half of the range should carry well over half the probability, and 0.875 does. The peak of 3 is nearly three times the reported probability's ceiling, which is exactly the point: the two numbers are not on the same scale.

Carry this away

  • The total area under a density is 1 because that is where the CDF has to end up, and the area between two thresholds is the probability of landing between them.
  • The height of a density is a probability per unit of x, so it carries no ceiling, and a uniform on [0, 0.5] sits at 2 all the way across.
  • The closed-interval form of the interval rule leans on a result that has not been shown yet, so the reading order is doing real work here.

Next: Every single point has probability zero, which shrinks the interval on this page down to nothing and collects what is left.

Your teacher. If the height-is-not-a-probability point keeps sliding, make it concrete with units and refuse to drop them. Ask an agent to restate every quantity on this page with its unit attached: a density in "probability per minute", a width in "minutes", a probability in nothing at all. The moment the units are written down, the objection dissolves on its own, because "2 per minute" was never a candidate probability in the first place. The serving read: a latency histogram in production is an empirical density, and a tall spike at 15 ms means requests cluster tightly there rather than that P(latency = 15 ms) is large.
