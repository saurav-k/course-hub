TA Session 1 · Part 7 of 8 · Problem 12

Every pair can be independent while the trio conspires

frontier 14 min Tutorial problem 12, part A and part B: the single most important distinction on the sheet, drawn first and proved second.

The one-minute version

  • On uniform {1,2,3,4}, the events A={1,4}, B={2,4}, C={3,4} have every pairwise intersection worth exactly 1/4 = product of marginals.
  • Yet the triple intersection is also {4}, worth 1/4, against the required product 1/8. Mutual independence fails.
  • Moral, stated plainly: pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence.
  • Independence survives complementing either or both events - proved for all three pairings via one subtraction and one De Morgan.

Problem 12, restated

Part A. Take Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4} with its power set as sigma-field and all four outcomes equally likely: P({i}) = 1/4 for i = 1, 2, 3, 4. Consider A = {1, 4}, B = {2, 4}, C = {3, 4}. Calculate P(A), P(B), P(C) and all three pairwise-intersection probabilities, show the three are pairwise independent, then compute P(A∩B∩C) and decide whether they are mutually independent.

Part B. For arbitrary independent events E and F, prove that each of the pairs Ec&F, E&Fc, and Ec&Fc is again independent.

Which definitions are in play, and why both matter

Two definitions sit side by side. Events are pairwise independent when every individual pair satisfies P(pair's intersection) = product of the pair's probabilities. They are mutually independent when that holds for every subset of two or more of them - for three events, the three pairwise conditions plus the triple condition P(A∩B∩C) = P(A)P(B)P(C). Mutual implies pairwise trivially. Whether the converse holds is exactly what part A settles, and it settles it by example rather than argument: one concrete counterexample kills a conjecture forever. Because all four outcomes carry probability 1/4, any event's probability is its size divided by 4 - the finite uniform model doing the counting for us.

  1. Marginals. Each event owns two of four equally likely outcomes: P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 2/4 = 1/2.
  2. Pairwise intersections. A∩B = {1,4}∩{2,4} = {4}; likewise A∩C = {4} and B∩C = {4}. All three have probability 1/4.
  3. Check each pair. P(A)P(B) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = P(A∩B), and identically for the other two pairs. All three pairwise conditions hold: A, B, C are pairwise independent.
  4. The triple. A∩B∩C = {4}, so P(A∩B∩C) = 1/4. But mutual independence demands P(A)P(B)P(C) = 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8.
  5. Compare and conclude. 1/4 ≠ 1/8, the triple condition fails, so A, B, C are not mutually independent. The example proves the general fact: pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence.
Where this goes wrongThe trap this problem was built around: after three green pairwise checks, declaring mutual independence by reflex. The second slip is miscomputing P(A)P(B)P(C) as 1/4 by reusing a pairwise result instead of multiplying three halves. Keep the two definitions physically separate on your page when you revise: pairwise means three checks, mutual means four.

What general lesson does the three-event example on {1, 2, 3, 4} establish?
One explicit example where pairs pass and the triple fails settles the implication question forever, negatively. Mutual independence holds fine on small spaces with other events. Uniformity helps counting but confers no independence. The triple came out twice the pairwise value here by construction, not by law.

Part B, restated and proved: complements preserve independence

E and F are independent, meaning P(E∩F) = P(E)P(F). Claim: replacing either or both by complements preserves the product rule. The tool is the difference rule wearing its set-theoretic coat: an event splits into the part sharing E and the part avoiding it, disjointly, so subtracting peels E off cleanly.

  1. Pairing one, Ec with F. Split F into disjoint parts: F = (E∩F) ∪ (Ec∩F), since a point of F either belongs to E or avoids it, never both. Additivity gives P(Ec∩F) = P(F) − P(E∩F).
  2. Use the hypothesis once. Substituting the independence relation: = P(F) − P(E)P(F) = (1 − P(E))P(F) = P(Ec)P(F). Product rule verified for the first pairing.
  3. Pairing two, E with Fc. Swap names in pairing one - the argument used nothing asymmetric - or rerun it: E = (E∩F) ∪ (E∩Fc) disjointly, so P(E∩Fc) = P(E) − P(E)P(F) = P(E)(1 − P(F)) = P(E)P(Fc). Verified.
  4. Pairing three, Ec with Fc. Here De Morgan enters: Ec∩Fc = (E∪F)c, the world outside both events. So P(Ec∩Fc) = 1 − P(E∪F) = 1 − P(E) − P(F) + P(E∩F), expanding the union with the addition rule.
  5. Collapse using independence twice. Replace P(E∩F) by P(E)P(F): the expression becomes 1 − P(E) − P(F) + P(E)P(F) = (1 − P(E))(1 − P(F)) = P(Ec)P(Fc). Verified.
  6. Verdict. All three pairings inherit the product rule from the original one: independence is a statement about probabilities that survives complementation intact, in every position.
Where this goes wrongThe tempting move is proving pairing three by "complementing both sides" of pairing one - but there is no algebraic rule letting you complement both members of an independence statement in one step; the honest route runs through De Morgan and the addition rule, as above. Second slip: writing F = (E∩F) ∪ (Ec∩F) without noting the pieces are disjoint, which is precisely what licenses turning the union into a sum. One shared point between them would sink the whole step.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Proving that E-complement paired with F-complement stays independent leans on which identity?
Ec ∩ Fc equals the complement of E ∪ F by De Morgan, and P(E ∪ F) expands by the addition rule before independence collapses the cross term. Singletons never appear. Bayes concerns reversed conditioning, absent here. The union bound is an inequality and cannot prove an exact product rule.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1 - verify part B inside part A's modelworking

Take E = A = {1,4} and F = B = {2,4} from part A, which are independent. Check all three complemented pairings numerically: Ec&F, E&Fc, and Ec&Fc.

Hint

List the complements explicitly first: everything on this space has probability size-over-four.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the complements. Ec = {2,3} and Fc = {1,3}; each has probability 1/2, as do E and F.
  2. First pairing. Ec∩F = {2,3}∩{2,4} = {2}, probability 1/4, and P(Ec)P(F) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Holds.
  3. Second pairing. E∩Fc = {1,4}∩{1,3} = {1}, probability 1/4, matching the product 1/4. Holds.
  4. Third pairing. Ec∩Fc = {2,3}∩{1,3} = {3}, probability 1/4, matching 1/4. Holds.
  5. Observe. Every nonempty proper intersection on this four-point model lands on exactly one outcome - the geometry that made part A work keeps part B's checks clean.

Sanity check. Part B promised all three pairings survive, so three matches out of three is the expected scoreboard, not a lucky streak.

Practice 2 - a second conspiracy, differently shapedfrontier

On uniform {1,2,3,4}, let D = {1,2}, G = {1,3}, H = {1,4}. Determine whether the three are pairwise independent, and whether they are mutually independent.

Hint

This family shares a common atom instead of trading a diagonal - compute the intersections and watch where they all live.

Show the worked solution
  1. Marginals. Each event holds two outcomes: probabilities all 1/2.
  2. Pairwise intersections. D∩G = {1}; D∩H = {1}; G∩H = {1}. Each has probability 1/4, equalling each product 1/2 × 1/2. Pairwise independent.
  3. Triple. D∩G∩H = {1}, probability 1/4, against the demanded product 1/8. Fails.
  4. Conclude. Same verdict as part A by a second geometry: three events stacked on one common outcome break the triple while satisfying every pair. Any such construction makes the point; memorise one, not both.

Sanity check. As in part A, every pairwise intersection is the single shared outcome, worth 1/4, while independence demands only 1/8 - so three matches on the pairs and one failure on the triple is exactly the scoreboard part B and part A predicted.

Your teacher. If the moral still feels abstract, ask where machine learning meets it: models that assume features independent "in pairs" do not thereby assume the full joint, and problem 12 is the standing counterexample to quote. If part B felt like symbol shuffling, redo pairing three without looking, keeping the two uses of the hypothesis visibly separate. The systems version of the same trap: two replicas that fail independently of a third can still conspire with it through the shared rack or database they all sit on, which is why "each pair looks safe" is never the whole reliability argument.
