One definition, three genuinely different routes to a mean

working 12 min The definition is a single sum or a single integral. Getting it to close is where the work is, and each of these three shapes needs a different move.

After this page

  • You can compute the exponential mean by a change of variable, and say why the substitution is the whole trick.
  • You can compute the binomial mean by differentiating the binomial theorem, without inventing a combinatorial argument.
  • You can compute the Gaussian mean by splitting x into its centre plus a deviation, and say what kills the leftover integral.

Before you start: What an average has to mean, for the definition every line below is applying.

The one-minute version

  • Exp(μ): substitute t = μx, and the integral becomes a standard one worth exactly 1. Mean 1/μ.
  • Bin(n, p): the factor k in front of the binomial coefficient is what a derivative produces, so differentiate the binomial theorem and read the sum off. Mean np.
  • N(μ, σ2): write x = μ + (x − μ). The first piece integrates to μ, and the second is an odd function whose two halves cancel. Mean μ.
  • The notes leave Geo(p) and Poi(λ) as exercises. This page works both, and labels them as this course's derivations.
  • Every mean here is a closed form, not a decimal: the source states no numbers anywhere.

1. The mental model: the definition never changes

For a discrete X the definition is a weighted sum, and for a continuous X it is a weighted integral.

E[X] = Σk k · pX(k)   and   E[X] = ∫ x · fX(x) dx X is the random quantity. k runs over the values a discrete X can take, and pX(k) is the probability of the value k. x is a point on the real line for a continuous X, and fX(x) is the density there, which is a probability per unit length rather than a probability. E[X] is the expectation, the value-weighted-by-likelihood average.

So there is no new idea on this page. There are five calculations, and the interesting part of each is the one manoeuvre that turns an integral or a sum you cannot see into one you already know.

2. The exponential, by substitution

Here μ is the exponential's rate: the notes write the density as μe−μx, so μ plays the role λ played in Lecture 7. The answer will be 1/μ, which is a time, not a rate.

  1. Write the definition. The density is zero below zero, so the integral runs from 0 upward: E[X] = ∫0∞ x · μe−μx dx.
  2. Pull the constant out. μ does not depend on x, so E[X] = μ ∫0∞ x e−μx dx. The notes stop here and substitute.
  3. Substitute. Let t = μx. Then x = t/μ and dx = dt/μ. The limits are unchanged, because t is zero when x is zero and runs to infinity with x.
  4. Rewrite the integrand. x e−μx dx = (t/μ) e−t (dt/μ) = (1/μ2) t e−t dt. Two factors of 1/μ come out, one from x and one from dx.
  5. Collect. With the μ still outside, E[X] = μ · (1/μ2) ∫0∞ t e−t dt = (1/μ) ∫0∞ t e−t dt.
  6. Evaluate the standard integral. The notes underline this factor as an Erlang density with rate 1. Integrating by parts once gives [−t e−t]0∞ + ∫0∞ e−t dt = 0 + 1 = 1, since t e−t is zero at both ends.
  7. Read the answer. E[X] = 1/μ. A faster rate means a shorter wait, which is the sanity check.

3. The binomial, by differentiating the binomial theorem

Here n is the number of independent trials, p the success probability on one trial, and N the number of successes. Write q = 1 − p for the failure probability to keep the lines short.

  1. Write the definition. E[N] = Σk=0n k · C(n,k) pk qn−k, where C(n,k) is the number of ways to choose which k of the n trials succeeded.
  2. Factor the failures out. pk qn−k = qn (p/q)k, because qn−k = qn/qk. So E[N] = qn Σk k C(n,k) xk with x = p/q. Now everything variable is inside one sum.
  3. Recognise what is missing. The binomial theorem gives (1+x)n = Σk C(n,k) xk, which is the same sum without the factor k. A factor of k in front of xk is exactly what differentiating with respect to x produces.
  4. Differentiate both sides. n(1+x)n−1 = Σk k C(n,k) xk−1. The k = 0 term differentiates to zero and drops out on its own.
  5. Multiply by x. This restores the exponent the derivative lowered: n x (1+x)n−1 = Σk k C(n,k) xk. The right side is now precisely the sum in step 2.
  6. Substitute the sum. E[N] = qn · n (p/q) (1 + p/q)n−1.
  7. Simplify the bracket. 1 + p/q = (q + p)/q = 1/q, since p and q sum to one. That single step is where the whole expression collapses.
  8. Cancel. E[N] = qn · n (p/q) · q−(n−1) = n p · qn − 1 − n + 1 = np. Every power of q cancels.

Part 6 will get np again in three lines, by writing N as a sum of n indicator variables and using linearity. That route is shorter and this one is worth doing once, because differentiating a known identity to manufacture a missing factor is a technique, not a trick for this one distribution.

4. The Gaussian, by splitting off its centre

Here μ is the centre of the bell and σ2 its variance, both still just parameters at this point in the lecture: the claim that μ is the mean is what this calculation establishes.

  1. Write the definition. E[X] = (1/√(2πσ2)) ∫ x e−(x−μ)2/2σ2 dx, over the whole real line.
  2. Split the x. Write x = μ + (x − μ). This is an identity, not an approximation, and it is the entire idea: it separates the part that is constant from the part that measures distance from the centre.
  3. Split the integral. The first piece is μ · (1/√(2πσ2)) ∫ e−(x−μ)2/2σ2 dx = μ · 1 = μ, because what multiplies μ is the total area under a density, which is one.
  4. Name the leftover. E[X] = μ + (1/√(2πσ2)) ∫ (x − μ) e−(x−μ)2/2σ2 dx. Everything now depends on x only through x − μ.
  5. Substitute y. Let y = x − μ, so dy = dx and the limits stay infinite both ways. The leftover integral is ∫ y e−y2/2σ2 dy over the whole line.
  6. Cut it at zero. The notes split it into the part over (0, ∞) and the part over (−∞, 0).
  7. Evaluate the right half. Insert a factor of σ2 and take it back out: ∫0∞ y e−y2/2σ2 dy = σ2 ∫0∞ (y/σ2) e−y2/2σ2 dy. What remains inside is the Rayleigh density, which the notes underline by name, and a density over its own support integrates to one. So the right half equals σ2.
  8. Evaluate the left half. Replace y by −y. The exponential is unchanged, since it sees y2, while the factor y flips sign. The left half is therefore −σ2, the mirror image of the right one.
  9. Add and finish. σ2 + (−σ2) = 0, so the leftover contributes nothing and E[X] = μ. Note that σ2 never appears in the answer: the spread does not move the balance point.

5. The two the notes leave as exercises

The notes write "Show" and stop, so the next two derivations are this course's ownBeside the Gaussian calculation the source writes Show − and lists two results with no working: that N ~ Geo(p) has mean 1/p, and that N ~ Poi(λ) has mean λ. Neither is worked anywhere in the nine pages. Both derivations below are written by this course, not transcribed from the lecture, in the same way Lecture 4, Part 5 finished an arithmetic its lecture left open. The two stated results are the lecturer's; every line of reasoning that reaches them is ours, so check it rather than trusting it, and expect a different textbook to reach the same answers by a different route.
  1. Geometric, the definition. With pN(k) = (1−p)k−1p for k ≥ 1, where k is the trial the first success lands on, E[N] = Σk≥1 k (1−p)k−1 p.
  2. Set q = 1 − p and pull p out. E[N] = p Σk≥1 k qk−1. The factor k again sits where a derivative would put it.
  3. Differentiate the geometric series. For |q| < 1, Σk≥0 qk = 1/(1−q). Differentiating both sides with respect to q gives Σk≥1 k qk−1 = 1/(1−q)2.
  4. Substitute back. 1 − q = p, so the sum is 1/p2 and E[N] = p · (1/p2) = 1/p. A one-in-ten chance per trial means ten trials on average, which is the check.
  5. Poisson, the definition. With pN(k) = e−λλk/k! for k ≥ 0, E[N] = Σk≥0 k e−λλk/k!.
  6. Drop the k = 0 term. Its factor k is zero, so the sum starts at k = 1 with nothing lost.
  7. Cancel k against k factorial. k/k! = 1/(k−1)!, so E[N] = e−λ Σk≥1 λk/(k−1)!. This is the Poisson counterpart of the derivative move: the factorial absorbs the k for free.
  8. Peel off one λ and reindex. Write λk = λ · λk−1 and set j = k − 1, so E[N] = λ e−λ Σj≥0 λj/j!.
  9. Use the exponential series. Σj≥0 λj/j! = eλ, so E[N] = λ e−λ eλ = λ. The parameter of a Poisson is its mean count, which is why it is usually quoted as a rate per interval.

6. What the delivered session added

The class worked one more mean than these pages had, and closed the block with an example that answers the question every reader of a probability course eventually asks out loud.

The Bernoulli, in two terms

  1. Write the support and the weights. A Bernoulli variable takes only the values 1 and 0, with P(X = 1) = p and P(X = 0) = 1 − p. Two values means two terms and nothing else.
  2. Apply the definition. E[X] = 1 · P(X = 1) + 0 · P(X = 0) = 1 · p + 0 · (1 − p).
  3. Collect. The second term is multiplied by zero and vanishes whatever 1 − p happens to be, so E[X] = p.
  4. Read it back. Run the experiment many times and roughly a fraction p of the runs give a 1 and the rest give a 0, so the average of what you saw is about p. The definition and the frequency argument agree, which is the reassurance this example exists to give.

"Why do I need this, and where does it touch machine learning?"

A student asked exactly that, and said he had stopped doing mathematics fifteen years ago. The lecturer answered with the smallest honest example there is, and it is worth carrying because it makes an expectation into something an engineer already computes every day.

  1. Take a training set. One thousand examples, with feature values x1 through x1000. The lecturer's reading of them: hours a student studied, against the marks they got.
  2. Pick one at random. Call the picked value Z. It is a random variable, because it takes a different value depending on which example the draw landed on.
  3. Write its distribution. Each example is equally likely, so P(Z = xi) = 1/1000 for every i. This is a discrete uniform over the dataset.
  4. Apply the definition. E[Z] = Σi xi · (1/1000) = (x1 + x2 + … + x1000)/1000.
  5. Read the answer. That is the plain average of the whole dataset. Sampling one training example at random has an expected value equal to the average of the entire training set, which is the reason a mini-batch is a usable stand-in for the full set, and the reason the batch of size one already gets the right answer on average.

The lecturer also had to correct a follow-up: the expectation is not 1 just because the sum runs to a thousand and is divided by a thousand. The xi are feature values, arbitrary real numbers, not counts.

Three more answers from the floor

  • "The geometric mean is 1/p and p is below one, so the answer is always above one?" Yes, and it must be: the variable itself is never below 1, so no weighted average of its values can be. Small p means many trials before the first success, large p means few, and 1/p says exactly that.
  • "Must the expected value be one of the values the variable can take?" No. It must lie within the range of the support, not in the support. A binomial takes only whole numbers between 0 and n, and its mean np is usually not a whole number at all.
  • "Isn't the geometric the same as the binomial?" No, and the difference is what is fixed in advance. A binomial fixes the number of trials at n and counts the successes, which is why a binomial coefficient appears. A geometric fixes nothing and counts the trials until the first success, so its support runs to infinity. The lecturer walked the same coin through both readings before the room accepted it.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the binomial theorem differentiated rather than simply quoted?
The definition of the mean carries a factor k in front of each term, and the plain theorem has no such factor; differentiating with respect to x is what puts one there. The first option is false, since the binomial theorem is an identity for every x. The second describes a side effect, as the k = 0 term does drop out, but that is not the reason for differentiating. The fourth confuses a later step: the powers of q cancel because 1 + p/q equals 1/q, whatever route produced the sum.
In the Gaussian calculation, what makes the leftover deviation integral vanish?
Each half is worth sigma squared in magnitude, one positive and one negative, so they cancel exactly. The second option is a true-sounding comparison that plays no part in the argument. The third is invented, and the normalising constant is fixed by sigma rather than arbitrary. The fourth is the Cauchy's problem, not the Gaussian's, and Part 3 shows what genuinely undefined looks like.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A build step fails independently on 30% of runs, and you run it 10 times. Separately, a request's service time is modelled as Exp(0.25) with time in minutes.

Find the expected number of failed builds and the expected service time, then say which of the two answers would change if the ten runs were correlated.

Hint

Both are direct substitutions into the closed forms above. For the second question, look at what the binomial derivation assumed about the trials and whether the exponential derivation assumed anything at all about repetition.

Show the worked solution
  1. Identify the shapes. Failed builds is Bin(n = 10, p = 0.3); service time is Exp(μ = 0.25 per minute).
  2. Apply np. 10 × 0.3 = 3 failed builds expected.
  3. Apply 1/μ. 1 / 0.25 = 4 minutes expected. The rate was per minute, so the answer is in minutes.
  4. Answer the third part. Neither expectation changes. The exponential answer never involved repetition. The binomial answer does assume independence to have a binomial distribution at all, but Part 6 will show that the expected count of a sum is the sum of expected counts whether or not the parts are independent, so 3 survives even when the runs are correlated. What dependence would change is the spread, not the mean.

Sanity check. The binomial mean must sit between 0 and n, and 3 does. The exponential mean must be larger for a smaller rate: halving 0.25 to 0.125 should double the wait to 8 minutes, and it does. Both decimals here are this course's own arithmetic; the source states no numbers.

Carry this away

  • Exp(μ) has mean 1/μ, reached by the substitution t = μx that turns the integral into a standard one worth 1.
  • Bin(n, p) has mean np, reached by differentiating the binomial theorem to manufacture the missing factor k.
  • N(μ, σ2) has mean μ, reached by splitting x into centre plus deviation and letting the two symmetric halves cancel.
  • Geo(p) gives 1/p and Poi(λ) gives λ; the notes assert both and this course works both.
