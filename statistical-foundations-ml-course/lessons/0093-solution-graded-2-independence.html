Independent inside C, dependent in the whole space

working 13 min The five marks of graded problem 2, carried to a final answer four separate ways.

The one-minute version

  • Part (a) is proved twice: from the axioms in three lines, and from a picture of where B cuts A.
  • Part (b)(i): yes. P(A ∩ B | C) = 1/4, and P(A | C) × P(B | C) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. A and B are conditionally independent given C.
  • Part (b)(ii): no. P(A ∩ B) = 5/8, while P(A) × P(B) = 9/16. The two differ by 1/16, so A and B are not independent.
  • Four routes reach those verdicts: counting, an area-true picture, the axioms, and splitting the space on C, which also explains the disagreement.

1. The space, counted once

The sheet gives Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}, the eight outcomes; F = 2Ω, meaning every subset is an event; and P(D) = |D| / 8, the uniform measure, where |D| counts the points in D, so each point carries one eighth. The events are A = {1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8}, B = {1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8} and C = {1, 2, 3, 4}.

Six points in A and six in B, so P(A) = P(B) = 6/8 = 3/4. Four in C, so P(C) = 1/2. Every other count is read off the picture.

2. Part (a), proved two ways

The claim: if A and B are independent, so are A and Bc, the complement of B, the event that B does not happen. Independent is the product test, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).

To prove: P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) × P(Bc) On the left, the probability that A happens and B does not. On the right, A's probability times the probability that B fails. Equality is the pair passing its own product test.

Route a1, from the axioms

  1. Split A on B. A ∩ B and A ∩ Bc are disjoint and their union is A, so finite additivity, the third axiom, gives P(A) = P(A ∩ B) + P(A ∩ Bc). Rearranged: P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B).
  2. Substitute the hypothesis. A and B are independent, so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B), giving P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A) P(B).
  3. Factor, then use the complement rule. P(A) − P(A) P(B) = P(A) (1 − P(B)), and the complement rule, P(Bc) = 1 − P(B), turns that into P(A) P(Bc). That is the product test for A and Bc, passed. Proved.

Nothing was divided by, so the argument survives P(B) = 0 and P(B) = 1, where the conditional form P(A | B) = P(A) cannot be written at all.

Route a2, from the picture

A check, not a proofThe result runs in reverse, in the sheet's own space. Section 3 finds A and B dependent, with P(A ∩ B) − P(A) P(B) = 1/16. Then P(A ∩ Bc) = 1/8 against P(A) P(Bc) = 3/16, a gap of −1/16: complementing B flipped the sign and never moved the size.

3. Route 1: count the points

Each test is an equation between two numbers: compute both sides and compare. Inside C, A's trace is {1, 2}, B's is {1, 3}, and they share only point 1.

  1. (b)(i), left side. A conditional probability is a share of C: P(A ∩ B | C) = |A ∩ B ∩ C| / |C| = 1/4, point 1 being the only point of C in both.
  2. (b)(i), right side. P(A | C) = |A ∩ C| / |C| = 2/4 = 1/2, and P(B | C) = 2/4 = 1/2. Their product is 1/4.
  3. (b)(i), verdict. 1/4 = 1/4, so the conditional product test holds: A and B are conditionally independent given C.
  4. (b)(ii), left side. A ∩ B = {1, 5, 6, 7, 8}, five points, so P(A ∩ B) = 5/8.
  5. (b)(ii), right side. P(A) P(B) = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.
  6. (b)(ii), verdict. 5/8 is 10/16, so the test fails by 1/16: A and B are not independent.

4. Route 2: make the picture area-true

Lecture 3 says flatly that no Venn diagram shows independence, and it is right about the diagrams it means: blobs whose areas stand for nothing. This measure changes that. P is uniform, so every point weighs the same and area is probability. Drawn to scale, the set picture becomes an argument.

5. Route 3: from the axioms, never touching the overlap

Routes 1 and 2 look straight at A ∩ B; this one never does, so it checks them rather than repeating them. Run the addition rule, P(X ∪ Y) = P(X) + P(Y) − P(X ∩ Y), which Lecture 3, Part 1 derives from the axioms, backwards.

  1. Price the union. A ∪ B = {1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8}, seven points, since only point 4 is in neither. So P(A ∪ B) = 7/8.
  2. Invert the addition rule. P(A ∩ B) = 3/4 + 3/4 − 7/8 = 12/8 − 7/8 = 5/8, agreeing with route 1 through a different equation.
  3. Build the conditional world. Define Q(D) = P(D ∩ C) / P(C). TA Session 1 proves Q obeys all three axioms, so the addition rule is available inside it too.
  4. Invert it again, inside C. The traces cover {1, 2, 3} of C's four points, so Q(A ∪ B) = 3/4 and Q(A ∩ B) = 1/2 + 1/2 − 3/4 = 1/4.
  5. Compare. Q(A) Q(B) = 1/4, which Q(A ∩ B) matches: conditionally independent. P(A) P(B) = 9/16, which P(A ∩ B) = 10/16 misses: not independent.

It costs a longer argument and buys this: once Q is a probability measure, conditional independence is ordinary independence read in Q.

6. Route 4: split the space on C

The first three routes answer the questions without saying why the answers differ. This one does, by the law of total probability over the partition {C, Cc}.

P(D) = P(D | C) P(C) + P(D | Cc) P(Cc) D is any event and Cc is everything outside C. The conditional probabilities are D's share of each half and the weights are the halves' sizes, both 1/2 here.
  1. The half outside C. Cc = {5, 6, 7, 8}, and all four are in A and in B, so P(A | Cc) = P(B | Cc) = P(A ∩ B | Cc) = 1. Since 1 = 1 × 1, A and B are conditionally independent inside Cc too.
  2. Reassemble the singles. P(A) = 1/2 × 1/2 + 1 × 1/2 = 3/4, and P(B) the same. Matching the counts is the check that the weights are right.
  3. Reassemble the overlap. P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 × 1/2 + 1 × 1/2 = 1/8 + 4/8 = 5/8, route 1's number out of a third equation.
  4. Locate the failure. The gap is 5/8 − 9/16 = 1/16, which is exactly (1/2)(1/2) × (1/2 − 1) × (1/2 − 1): the weights times how far each event's share moves between the halves.

Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis build the same effect from two biased coins. It is the standard warning: independence and conditional independence imply each other in neither direction.

7. Four routes, one verdict

Route 1 is the exam answer: four counts and two multiplications, which is the shape "justify" asks for. Route 2 is the one to keep in memory, a broken line being easier to recall than 9/16, but it dies once the measure stops being uniform. Route 3 generalises, because it counts nothing. Route 4 is the one to carry into machine learning, where conditional independence given a hidden variable is an assumption made on purpose.

Where this page runs aheadThe sheet asks for a verdict with justification, which is route 1 alone. The other three routes and the reading of the 1/16 gap are this course's own additions and would earn no extra marks. They are here because one line of arithmetic is thin evidence.

Check yourself

Which single feature of the sorted picture settles both halves of part (b) at once?
One point per cell is an even two-by-two table, which is what a product looks like, so the conditional test passes; the four points outside C pile into one cell, which breaks the table for the whole space. Overlap decides nothing either way, C being half the space only fixes route 4's weights, and point 4 lies in neither A nor B.
Route 4 splits the space on C. What does it explain that the direct count does not?
Both events are half-likely inside C and certain outside it, so each carries news about which half you are in and therefore about the other: dependence built out of the mixing alone. The count is right, and route 4 reproduces 5/8. Conditional independence holds in both halves rather than failing, and neither sub-question depends on the other.
Your teacher. Work part (b) with a pencil before reading route 1, and stop once both sides of both tests are written as numbers. That is the whole answer; everything after section 3 is checking. The habit worth taking is smaller than it looks: when an answer surprises you, find a second argument that shares no step with the first.
