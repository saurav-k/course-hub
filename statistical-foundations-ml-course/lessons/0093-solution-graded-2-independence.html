Independent inside C, dependent in the whole space

working 8 min The five marks of graded problem 2, carried to a final answer.

After this page

  • You can prove that independence survives taking a complement, in three lines and with no division anywhere.
  • You can run the plain and the conditional product tests on a finite uniform space and state each verdict with both numbers under it.

Before you start: Graded 2: independence, which states both parts and names both tests, and your own attempt at them.

The one-minute version

  • Part (a) is three lines from the axioms: split A on B, substitute the hypothesis, factor.
  • Part (b)(i): yes. P(A ∩ B | C) = 1/4, and P(A | C) × P(B | C) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. A and B are conditionally independent given C.
  • Part (b)(ii): no. P(A ∩ B) = 5/8, while P(A) × P(B) = 9/16. The two differ by 1/16, so A and B are not independent.
  • The measure is uniform, so every number either test needs is a count of points.

1. The space, counted once

The sheet gives Ω = {1, ..., 8}, F = 2Ω so every subset is an event, and the uniform measure P(D) = |D| / 8, where |D| counts the points in D. With A = {1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8}, B = {1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8} and C = {1, 2, 3, 4}, that gives P(A) = P(B) = 3/4 and P(C) = 1/2. Every other count is read off the picture.

This course's derivationThe sheet publishes no answer, so every number below is this course's own arithmetic, none of it quoted.

2. Part (a), from the axioms

If A and B are independent, so are A and Bc, the event that B does not happen. Independent is the product test, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).

To prove: P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) × P(Bc) On the left, the probability that A happens and B does not. On the right, A's probability times the probability that B fails. Equality is the pair passing its own product test.
  1. Split A on B. A ∩ B and A ∩ Bc are disjoint and their union is A, so finite additivity, the third axiom, gives P(A) = P(A ∩ B) + P(A ∩ Bc). Rearranged: P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B).
  2. Substitute the hypothesis. A and B are independent, so P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B), giving P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) − P(A) P(B).
  3. Factor, then use the complement rule. P(A) − P(A) P(B) = P(A) (1 − P(B)), and the complement rule, P(Bc) = 1 − P(B), turns that into P(A) P(Bc). That is the product test for A and Bc, passed. Proved.

Nothing was divided by, so the argument survives P(B) = 0 and P(B) = 1, where P(A | B) = P(A) cannot be written at all. Reading independence as a share of A makes the result visible rather than derived, but a share is a ratio and needs P(B) positive, so these three lines are what the mark is for.

3. Part (b), both tests counted

Each test is an equation between two numbers. Inside C, A's trace is {1, 2} and B's is {1, 3}, sharing only point 1.

  1. (b)(i), left side. A conditional probability is a share of C: P(A ∩ B | C) = |A ∩ B ∩ C| / |C| = 1/4, point 1 being the only point of C in both.
  2. (b)(i), right side. P(A | C) = |A ∩ C| / |C| = 2/4 = 1/2, and P(B | C) = 2/4 = 1/2. Their product is 1/4.
  3. (b)(i), verdict. 1/4 = 1/4, so the conditional product test holds: A and B are conditionally independent given C.
  4. (b)(ii), left side. A ∩ B = {1, 5, 6, 7, 8}, five points, so P(A ∩ B) = 5/8.
  5. (b)(ii), right side. P(A) P(B) = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.
  6. (b)(ii), verdict. 5/8 is 10/16, so the test fails by 1/16: A and B are not independent.

That is the whole answer, in the shape "justify" asks for. The picture below is the same six numbers drawn to scale, since P is uniform and area is therefore probability.

The dependence has a cause worth one line: outside C both events are certain and inside C both are half-likely, so learning that A happened makes the outside half likelier and drags B up with it. Splitting on C prices that mixing exactly, at the cost of a longer argument than the sheet asks for.

Where this page runs aheadThe sheet asks for a verdict with justification, which is the counting above and nothing more. The reading of the 1/16 gap is this course's own addition and earns no extra marks.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which single feature of the sorted picture settles both halves of part (b) at once?
One point per cell is an even two-by-two table, which is what a product looks like, so the conditional test passes; the four points outside C pile into one cell, which breaks the table for the whole space. Overlap decides nothing either way, C being half the space is true but settles neither test, and point 4 lies in neither A nor B.
A and B pass the test inside C and fail it in the whole space. What does that show?
The plain test prices everything over the eight points; the conditional test prices everything inside C after dividing by P(C), where the outside world is invisible. Different rulers, different numbers, and no implication in either direction. The counting is right and reproduces both sides of both tests, conditional independence in fact holds here rather than failing, and neither sub-question depends on the other's answer.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Same eight points, same A and B, but condition on Cc = {5, 6, 7, 8} instead of on C.

Decide whether A and B are conditionally independent given Cc, then say what your answer plus the page's verdicts prove about the two tests.

Hint

Write down which of the four points of Cc lie in A, and which lie in B, before computing anything.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the traces. All four of 5, 6, 7 and 8 lie in A and all four lie in B, so A ∩ Cc = B ∩ Cc = A ∩ B ∩ Cc = Cc.
  2. Run the conditional test. P(A | Cc) = P(B | Cc) = P(A ∩ B | Cc) = 4/4 = 1, and 1 = 1 × 1, so A and B are conditionally independent given Cc.
  3. Read what that proves. The pair passes the conditional test inside C and inside Cc, yet fails the plain test on the whole space. So conditional independence on every part of a partition does not imply independence.

Sanity check. Reassemble the overlap from the two halves: 1/4 × 1/2 + 1 × 1/2 = 5/8, which is the P(A ∩ B) the page counted. If your two conditional numbers do not rebuild 5/8, one of the traces is wrong.

Carry this away

  • The complement proof is split, substitute, factor, and it never divides, so it holds even when P(B) is 0 or 1.
  • The conditional test is the product test run inside C after dividing by P(C), and it can pass while the plain test fails.
  • "Justify" means both sides of the test as numbers and then the comparison, not a verdict with nothing under it.
