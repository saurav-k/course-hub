Sheet problem 15 - a floor under an intersection

The question, in plain English. Two proofs are asked for. Part (a): for any two events A and B, the probability that both occur is never less than the probability of A plus the probability of B, minus one. Part (b): the same statement for many events - for events A1 through An, the probability that all of them occur is never less than the sum of their individual probabilities, minus n minus one. Both are prove-it questions: the answer is a short argument, not a value.

P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1     and     P(A1 ∩ A2 ∩ ... ∩ An) ≥ P(A1) + ... + P(An) − (n − 1) Read as: A and B are two events, and A1 through An are n of them. The left side of each is "the probability that every listed event occurs", an intersection. The right side adds the one-event probabilities and then subtracts 1, or n − 1 in the many-event version. The symbol ≥ says the left side is at least the right, and the whole claim is that this holds for every choice of events in any probability space.

What it is really testing. Whether you can produce a Bonferroni-type inequality from the axioms instead of from memory. The course states this family on The union bound and Bonferroni - there the inequalities fence in a union, truncation by truncation - and the two-event addition rule lives on What the axioms force. The sheet's statement and the lesson's family are relatives, and the page will not say how; making the connection is the exercise.

Where the wording bitesThree things get misread. One: both parts ask for a lower bound on an intersection - a floor - while several of the lesson's inequalities are ceilings, so copying a direction from the lesson is the classic slip. Two: part (b) is not part (a) applied once to two cleverly chosen events; the n-event claim needs the argument to climb, so decide early whether it climbs by grouping events or by stepping through n. Three: nothing may assume independence - the inequality must hold for events overlapping in any pattern at all, which is why it is useful.

How to start. For part (a), write down what the complement of the intersection A ∩ B is, in set language, before touching any numbers. Then ask which one-course-tool prices a union. For part (b), decide the climbing strategy first - grouping or induction - and check that the two-event result really is the first rung of the ladder you chose. Stop as soon as the first move is on paper; the algebra from there is yours.

Weight. No marks are printed against this problem - it is practice. Difficulty: moderate, and easier than it looks once the direction of the inequality is firmly a floor.

Sheet problem 16 - two nearly-certain events must share ground

The question, in plain English. Take two events A and B whose probabilities both beat 1 minus δ, where δ is a very small positive number. So each event, on its own, is almost sure to happen - each misses certainty by less than δ. The claim to prove: the probability that they both occur, P(A ∩ B), is close to 1. This is problem 15 part (a) turned into a statement about the world: the inequality becomes powerful the moment the individual probabilities are pushed near certainty.

P(A) > 1 − δ   and   P(B) > 1 − δ   should force   P(A ∩ B) ≈ 1 Read as: δ ("delta") is a small positive number, the size of each event's shortfall from certainty. The strict inequalities say A happens with probability exceeding one minus δ, and the same for B. The conclusion symbol ≈ reads "is close to" - and making that closeness precise, in terms of δ, is part of what a proof must do.

What it is really testing. Whether you can use an inequality rather than merely recite it, and whether you can finish a proof honestly by saying what "close to 1" means. The tool is proved on this very sheet in problem 15; the underlying family is the one on The union bound and Bonferroni, and the complement rule it needs is on What the axioms force. The pairing is deliberate on the setter's part: one sheet problem feeds the next.

Where the wording bitesThe inequalities on P(A) and P(B) are strict - each probability exceeds 1 − δ, and whether that strictness matters to the claim deserves one conscious thought. Second, "close to 1" is not a mood: a proof that ends without saying how the gap depends on δ is not a proof. Third, "very small δ" is motivation; the algebra should not need the smallness, and noticing that is a bonus, not a requirement.

How to start. Write the problem-15 part (a) inequality for this A and B. Then translate each hypothesis - each event beats 1 minus δ - into a statement about its complement's probability, using the complement rule. At that point both sides of the inequality are priced, and what remains is reading off the guarantee. Stop there; the two lines of algebra in between are the whole problem and they are yours.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: routine once problem 15(a) is in hand; the only real work is saying "close to 1" precisely.

Sheet problem 21 - one sentence, two squeezes

The question, in plain English. You have N events, called A1 through AN. Three facts are given. First, at least one of them occurs almost surely: the probability of their union is exactly 1. Second, no more than two of them occur almost surely: it is certain that you never see three or more happening at once. Third, the events are interchangeable in the sense that every single one has the same probability p, and every pair meets with the same probability q, for any two distinct indices. The task: show that p must be at least 1 over N, and q must be at most 2 over N.

P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ AN) = 1,   P(An) = p,   P(An ∩ Am) = q   for m ≠ n   ⇒   p ≥ 1/N   and   q ≤ 2/N Read as: A1 through AN are N events; the union symbol means "at least one occurs", and its probability is 1, meaning certainly. Every individual event has the same probability p, and every two distinct events - the condition m ≠ n - overlap with the same probability q. The arrow claims these givens force p to be at least 1 over N and q to be at most 2 over N. "Almost surely" means "with probability 1", which is the course's standing caveat that certainty and probability 1 are the same thing here.

What it is really testing. Whether the axioms and the bound family have become instruments you can point in a chosen direction. The union-side squeeze is the union bound of The union bound and Bonferroni read from one end; the pair-side squeeze needs the inclusion-exclusion bookkeeping of Inclusion-exclusion applied to a triple; and the whole argument runs on the monotonicity and addition facts of What the axioms force. It is the hardest problem on this page precisely because it needs three tools aimed at two different targets.

Where the wording bites"Almost surely" appears twice with different jobs: once to say the union is certain, once to say triples never happen. Read them as two givens, not one. Second, the symmetry - every p equal, every q equal - is a genuine assumption doing real work; with unequal probabilities the claims would need restating. Third, "no more than two occur" forbids three or more, so the natural object to price is a threefold intersection, not a pair. Fourth, the two conclusions are about p and q separately - the sheet does not ask whether both bounds can be met at once.

How to start. Take the two squeezes one at a time, and for each, write in words what quantity you plan to bound and from which side. For the floor on p: the union covers everything, so ask what the union bound says when its left side is exactly 1. For the ceiling on q: pick any three distinct events, write the probability that all three occur - the given says it is zero - and ask what the inclusion-exclusion tally of those three says about their singles and pairs. Stop once each squeeze is set up as "theorem, applied to this, gives this direction"; carrying the algebra further is the work the sheet is testing.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: the hardest on this page; two squeezes, three tools, and wording that rewards a slow second reading.

Sheet problem 24 - the heaviest a union and an intersection can be

The question, in plain English. Two events are given, with fixed probabilities: P(A) is exactly one quarter, and P(B) is exactly one half. Nothing else is fixed - the two events may overlap a lot, a little, or not at all. Find the largest values that P(A ∪ B) and P(A ∩ B) could possibly take, across every arrangement of two events with those masses inside some sample space. The sheet asks for the two maxima; each is a value, and each needs a reason that no arrangement can beat it.

P(A) = 1/4,   P(B) = 1/2   ⇒   max P(A ∪ B) = ?,   max P(A ∩ B) = ? Read as: A and B are events whose individual probabilities are pinned at one quarter and one half. The union, "at least one occurs", and the intersection, "both occur", still have freedom - their values depend on how the events sit inside each other. The question asks for the largest value each of the two can attain, over all legal arrangements.

What it is really testing. Whether the two cap facts from What the axioms force - containment never loses mass, and the addition rule - have become reflexes you can point at an optimisation. It is also quietly testing a habit: the sheet asks for two separate maxima, and a careful answer treats each on its own terms. The numeric side is one-digit arithmetic; the content is the capping argument and the reachability sentence.

Where the wording bitesThe sheet writes "P(A ∪ B) & P(A ∩ B)" with an ampersand - it asks for two separate maxima, not for one arrangement maximising both at once, and not for whether that is possible. Second, "maximum possible" means largest over all legal pairs of events in any probability space; the sample space is yours to choose. Third, the union also has a trivial cap - no probability exceeds 1 - and which cap actually binds is a one-line comparison the sheet wants made, not assumed.

How to start. For each of the two quantities, name the axiom that caps it from above - one comes from the addition rule on the Lecture 3 page just linked, the other from containment. Then ask what arrangement of the two blocks would reach the cap, using the sliding picture: just touching for one, full coverage for the other. The last step is the sentence a grader looks for - why nothing can beat the cap. Stop before computing; the values fall out of two lines you have not yet written.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: quick, and a favourite exam warm-up; the only trap is answering a joint question the sheet did not ask.

Sheet problem 29 - when adding costs nothing

The question, in plain English. Suppose a finite list of events A1 through An has a remarkable property: the probability of their union - "at least one occurs" - equals the sum of their individual probabilities, added one by one. Normally the sum overshoots the union, because an outcome living in several events is counted several times. The hypothesis says there is no overshoot at all. Prove that this forces every pair to be disjoint in the strongest possible sense: for any two distinct indices i and j, the probability of Ai ∩ Aj is exactly zero.

∑i=1n P(Ai) = P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An)   ⇒   P(Ai ∩ Aj) = 0   for all i ≠ j Read as: the left side adds the n individual probabilities, one per event. The right side is the probability that at least one of them occurs. The hypothesis says adding counts nothing twice. The conclusion says every pairwise overlap - Ai ∩ Aj, for any two different indices - must carry probability exactly zero.

What it is really testing. Whether you can run inclusion-exclusion backwards - not to compute a union, but to read a hypothesis off against it. The two-event addition rule on What the axioms force settles the two-event case in one line; the n-event claim then needs a decision about how the general case follows, and the inclusion-exclusion bookkeeping of Inclusion-exclusion is the measuring stick the hypothesis is held against. It is the most elegant problem on this page: short once seen, invisible before.

Where the wording bitesThe hypothesis equates a sum of probabilities with a probability of a union - two objects that live on opposite sides of the addition rule, and that are equal only in the disjoint case you are asked to force. Second, the conclusion concerns every pair, not the stronger "no point in three events" statement; a proof that handles only the pair you happened to pick has not finished. Third, "for all i, j, i ≠ j" includes pairs far apart in the list, so the argument cannot lean on neighbours only.

How to start. Prove the two-event version first: write the addition rule for A1 and A2, hold it against the hypothesis applied to just those two, and see what the comparison forces - the probability of an intersection is never negative, and that fact is load-bearing. Then decide how the n-event case follows: either peel events off the union one at a time, or expand the full union with inclusion-exclusion and stare at what the equality does to every term. Stop once the strategy is written down in one sentence; the sheet wants the execution.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: medium; the two-event case is a one-liner and the n-event climb is bookkeeping you have already done once on the inclusion-exclusion page.

Sheet problem 15 wants a floor under an intersection. Which course tool, reached through one complement rewrite, points the right way?
The intersection hides inside a union once you complement, and the union bound prices any union from above - which is a floor on the intersection after rearranging. Bayes belongs to the reversal problems on the next page, not here. The multiplication rule chains conditional paths, which no part of this problem needs. And nothing here may assume independence - the claim must hold for every pair of events, dependent or not.
In sheet problem 21, which single hypothesis forces p to sit at or above one over N?
If the union is certain, the union bound pins the sum of all N single probabilities above 1, and with every p equal that lifts each one - the floor comes from the union hypothesis alone. The equal-pairs assumption feeds the other squeeze, on q. The ban on triples likewise belongs to the q ceiling, not to p. The numbering is just labels; it carries no probability content at all.
For sheet problem 24, which fact caps the union's probability before you slide the events anywhere?
The addition rule prices the union at the sum of the masses minus the overlap, so the sum is a ceiling before any geometry enters. The cap of 1 also exists, and deciding which of the two caps binds here is exactly the comparison the problem wants - so neither is assumed away. Twice the smaller mass is a bound on nothing in the axioms. And the larger mass caps the intersection, not the union - swapping the two caps is the classic slip.
