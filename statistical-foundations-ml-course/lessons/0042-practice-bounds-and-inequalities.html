Five problems where the win is a proven inequality, not a number

working 12 min Practice Set 1, sheet problems 15, 16, 21, 24 and 29, each worked through to a finished answer.

After this page

  • You can turn a ceiling on a union into a floor on an intersection, in one complement step.
  • You can decide which axiom caps a quantity, and show the cap is actually reached.

Before you start: What the axioms force and The union bound and Bonferroni.

The one-minute version

  • All five problems are fenced in by two facts: the addition rule, and the fact that no probability exceeds 1 or falls below 0.
  • Problem 15 is the Bonferroni floor P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1, and 16 is that floor with the numbers pushed to the edge.
  • Problem 21 carries two separate squeezes: the union gives a floor on p, the ban on triples gives a ceiling on q.
  • Every problem below is solved in full, one method each, in the practice section.
Practice, not submittedThese belong to the practice half of Practice Set 1. The four graded problems have their own pages and are due Sunday 6 September for 15 marks.

The one unit everything must fit inside

Two events drawing on the same unit of probability cannot both be large and disjoint. That single observation is the engine of problems 15, 16 and 21, and the picture below is what it looks like.

When a cap is a maximum

Problems 24 and 29 want the other move: name the axiom that caps a quantity, then show an arrangement that touches the cap. A cap nobody reaches is not a maximum, so both halves are owed.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Sheet problem 15 wants a floor under an intersection. Which fact, used once, points the right way?
The addition rule prices the union as P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B), and capping that at 1 rearranges straight into the floor. Bayes belongs to the reversal problems on the next page. The multiplication rule chains conditional paths, which no part of this problem needs. And nothing here may assume independence - the claim must hold for every pair, dependent or not.
In sheet problem 21, which single hypothesis forces p to sit at or above one over N?
If the union is certain, the union bound pins the sum of the N single probabilities above 1, and with every p equal that lifts each one. The equal-pairs assumption feeds the other squeeze, on q. The ban on triples likewise belongs to the q ceiling. The numbering is just labels and carries no probability content.
For sheet problem 24, which fact caps the union's probability before you slide the events anywhere?
The addition rule prices the union at the sum minus the overlap, so the sum of three quarters is a ceiling before any geometry enters. The cap of 1 also exists, and comparing the two is exactly the step the problem wants made. Twice the smaller mass bounds nothing in the axioms. And the larger mass caps the intersection, not the union.

Practice about 45 minutes, with paper

Sheet 15 - a floor under an intersectionworking

Two proofs are asked for. Part (a): for any two events A and B, the probability that both occur is never less than the probability of A plus the probability of B, minus one. Part (b): the same statement for many events - for events A1 through An, the probability that all of them occur is never less than the sum of their individual probabilities, minus n minus one.

P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1     and     P(A1 ∩ ... ∩ An) ≥ P(A1) + ... + P(An) − (n − 1) Read as: the left side of each is the probability that every listed event occurs, an intersection. The right side adds the one-event probabilities and subtracts 1, or n − 1 in the many-event version. The claim holds for every choice of events in any probability space, with no independence assumed.
Hint

Part (a) is the addition rule with the union capped at 1. Part (b) is part (a) applied n − 1 times, by induction.

Show the worked solution
  1. Part (a), start from the addition rule. For any two events, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). This is an equality, not a bound, and it holds with no assumption on how A and B overlap.
  2. Cap the union. A ∪ B is an event, so P(A ∪ B) ≤ 1. Substituting: P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) ≤ 1.
  3. Rearrange. Add P(A ∩ B) to both sides and subtract 1: P(A) + P(B) − 1 ≤ P(A ∩ B). That is part (a), proved.
  4. Part (b), set up the induction on n. The claim for n = 2 is exactly part (a), so the base case is done. Assume the claim holds for some n − 1 events, that is P(A1 ∩ ... ∩ An−1) ≥ P(A1) + ... + P(An−1) − (n − 2).
  5. Take the inductive step. Write B = A1 ∩ ... ∩ An−1, a single event. Part (a) applied to B and An gives P(B ∩ An) ≥ P(B) + P(An) − 1.
  6. Substitute the inductive hypothesis for P(B). P(B ∩ An) ≥ [P(A1) + ... + P(An−1) − (n − 2)] + P(An) − 1 = P(A1) + ... + P(An) − (n − 1). Since B ∩ An is the full intersection, this is the claim for n, and the induction closes.

Sanity check. Put n = 2 into part (b) and the subtraction is n − 1 = 1, recovering part (a). Put every P(Ai) = 1 and the bound reads P(all) ≥ n − (n − 1) = 1, which is correct: n certain events certainly all occur. And when the probabilities are small the right side goes negative, so the bound says nothing - which is why it only earns its keep near certainty, as problem 16 shows.

Sheet 16 - two near-certain eventsfoundation

Take two events A and B whose probabilities both beat 1 minus δ, where δ is a very small positive number. So each event, on its own, misses certainty by less than δ. Show that the probability that they both occur, P(A ∩ B), is close to 1, and say precisely how close in terms of δ.

Hint

Feed the two hypotheses straight into the floor proved in problem 15(a).

Show the worked solution
  1. Quote the floor. Problem 15(a) gives P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1 for any two events, this pair included.
  2. Substitute the hypotheses. P(A) > 1 − δ and P(B) > 1 − δ, so P(A) + P(B) > 2(1 − δ) = 2 − 2δ.
  3. Chain the two. P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1 > (2 − 2δ) − 1 = 1 − 2δ.
  4. Say what "close to 1" means. The shortfall of P(A ∩ B) from 1 is less than 2δ: each event contributes at most its own δ of missing mass, and the two shortfalls add. As δ tends to 0, P(A ∩ B) tends to 1. That is the precise statement the proof owes.

Sanity check. Take δ = 0.01, so each event has probability above 0.99; the bound gives P(A ∩ B) > 0.98. It is tight: let Ω be the unit interval, A everything except the top 0.01, B everything except the bottom 0.01, and the intersection has probability exactly 0.98. Note the smallness of δ was never used - the algebra holds for any δ, and for δ ≥ 1/2 the bound simply stops saying anything.

Sheet 21 - one sentence, two squeezesfrontier

You have N events, A1 through AN. Three facts are given. First, at least one of them occurs almost surely: the probability of their union is exactly 1. Second, no more than two of them occur almost surely: it is certain that you never see three or more happening at once. Third, every single event has the same probability p, and every pair meets with the same probability q. Show that p must be at least 1 over N, and q must be at most 2 over N.

P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ AN) = 1,   P(An) = p,   P(An ∩ Am) = q   for m ≠ n   ⇒   p ≥ 1/N   and   q ≤ 2/N Read as: the union symbol means "at least one occurs", and its probability is 1. Every individual event has probability p, and every two distinct events - the condition m ≠ n - overlap with probability q. "Almost surely" means "with probability 1".
Hint

The ban on triples makes inclusion-exclusion stop after two terms, and it becomes an exact equation you can solve.

Show the worked solution
  1. The floor on p, from the union alone. The union bound gives P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ AN) ≤ P(A1) + ... + P(AN). The left side is 1 by hypothesis and the right side is Np, since all N singles equal p. So 1 ≤ Np, that is p ≥ 1/N.
  2. Turn the ban on triples into vanishing terms. For any three distinct indices i, j, k, the event Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak is contained in the event "three or more occur", which has probability 0. So every triple intersection has probability 0, and so does every intersection of four or more, each being contained in a triple.
  3. Write inclusion-exclusion and delete what vanishes. P(∪ An) = Σ P(An) − Σi<j P(Ai ∩ Aj) + (terms of order three and higher). Every term from the third group onward is 0 by step 2, so the identity is exact after two terms.
  4. Count the terms. There are N singles, each p, and N(N − 1)/2 distinct pairs, each q. With the union equal to 1: 1 = Np − q·N(N − 1)/2.
  5. Use the only remaining cap. Rearrange step 4 as q·N(N − 1)/2 = Np − 1. Now p is a probability, so p ≤ 1 and therefore Np − 1 ≤ N − 1, giving q·N(N − 1)/2 ≤ N − 1.
  6. Divide. For N ≥ 2 the factor N − 1 is positive, so q ≤ 2(N − 1) / [N(N − 1)] = 2/N. Both claims are proved.

Sanity check. Test N = 2: the claims read p ≥ 1/2 and q ≤ 1, and step 4 reads 1 = 2p − q, which with p ≤ 1 indeed forces q = 2p − 1 ≤ 1 and p = (1 + q)/2 ≥ 1/2. Both bounds are attained together when p = 1/N and q = 0, that is N disjoint events each of probability 1/N filling the space - no triples, no pairs, and the union certain.

Sheet 24 - the two heaviest valuesfoundation

Two events are given, with fixed probabilities: P(A) is exactly one quarter, and P(B) is exactly one half. Nothing else is fixed - the two events may overlap a lot, a little, or not at all. Find the largest values that P(A ∪ B) and P(A ∩ B) could possibly take, across every arrangement of two events with those masses inside some sample space.

Hint

Two separate maxima. Cap each with one axiom fact, then build the arrangement that touches the cap.

Show the worked solution
  1. Cap the union. The addition rule gives P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 + 1/2 − P(A ∩ B) = 3/4 − P(A ∩ B). Since P(A ∩ B) ≥ 0, we get P(A ∪ B) ≤ 3/4.
  2. Check the other cap does not bind. Every probability is at most 1, and 3/4 < 1, so the addition-rule cap is the binding one.
  3. Reach it. Take Ω = [0, 1) with length as probability, A = [0, 1/4) and B = [1/4, 3/4). They are disjoint, so P(A ∩ B) = 0 and P(A ∪ B) = 3/4 exactly. Maximum union = 3/4.
  4. Cap the intersection. A ∩ B is contained in A, and containment never gains mass, so P(A ∩ B) ≤ P(A) = 1/4. Containment in B gives only P(A ∩ B) ≤ 1/2, which is weaker, so 1/4 is the binding cap.
  5. Reach it. Take the same Ω, A = [0, 1/4) and B = [0, 1/2). Now A is contained in B, so A ∩ B = A and P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 exactly. Maximum intersection = 1/4.
  6. Answer the question asked. The two maxima are 3/4 and 1/4, each over its own arrangement. They are not attained together: the disjoint arrangement has intersection 0, and the nested arrangement has union 1/2.

Sanity check. Both answers respect the general fences: max(P(A), P(B)) = 1/2 ≤ P(A ∪ B) ≤ 3/4, and 0 ≤ P(A ∩ B) ≤ 1/4. The Bonferroni floor from problem 15 gives P(A ∩ B) ≥ 1/4 + 1/2 − 1 = −1/4, which is vacuous here - as it should be, since disjointness is genuinely allowed at these masses.

Sheet 29 - when adding costs nothingworking

Suppose a finite list of events A1 through An has the property that the probability of their union equals the sum of their individual probabilities. Prove that this forces every pair to be disjoint in the strongest possible sense: for any two distinct indices i and j, the probability of Ai ∩ Aj is exactly zero.

∑i=1n P(Ai) = P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An)   ⇒   P(Ai ∩ Aj) = 0   for all i ≠ j Read as: the left side adds the n individual probabilities, one per event. The right side is the probability that at least one of them occurs. The hypothesis says adding counts nothing twice, and the conclusion says every pairwise overlap carries probability exactly zero.
Hint

Fix one pair, glue that pair into a single event, and run the union bound over the shortened list.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix a pair. Take any two distinct indices i and j and hold them fixed for the whole argument. Nothing below depends on which pair was chosen.
  2. Regroup the union. A1 ∪ ... ∪ An = (Ai ∪ Aj) ∪ (the union of the remaining n − 2 events). This is a union of n − 1 events, one of which is the glued pair.
  3. Apply the union bound to those n − 1 pieces. P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An) ≤ P(Ai ∪ Aj) + Σk ≠ i, j P(Ak).
  4. Price the glued pair exactly. The addition rule gives P(Ai ∪ Aj) = P(Ai) + P(Aj) − P(Ai ∩ Aj). Substituting into step 3, the right side becomes Σk=1n P(Ak) − P(Ai ∩ Aj).
  5. Bring in the hypothesis. The left side of step 3 equals Σk P(Ak) by assumption, so Σk P(Ak) ≤ Σk P(Ak) − P(Ai ∩ Aj). The sums cancel, leaving 0 ≤ −P(Ai ∩ Aj), that is P(Ai ∩ Aj) ≤ 0.
  6. Close with non-negativity. A probability is never negative, so P(Ai ∩ Aj) = 0. Since i and j were an arbitrary distinct pair, this holds for every pair, however far apart in the list.

Sanity check. Run n = 2 through the argument: the regrouping is trivial, and step 5 reads P(A1) + P(A2) ≤ P(A1) + P(A2) − P(A1 ∩ A2), the one-line proof by hand. The converse also holds: pairwise null overlaps make the union bound an equality, so the hypothesis and the conclusion describe the same situation.

Carry this away

  • The addition rule plus the cap of 1 is a two-step machine: it produced the Bonferroni floor, the p floor and both maxima on this page.
  • A ceiling on a union becomes a floor on an intersection by rearranging, never by memorising a second inequality.
  • Killing triple intersections turns inclusion-exclusion from a bound into an exact equation, and an equation can be solved.
  • A maximum owes two things: the cap, and an arrangement that reaches it.
