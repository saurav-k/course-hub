Counting the failures instead of the successes gives the same distribution read backwards

working 12 min Problem 11 on the sheet, the last thing the slow Sunday sitting worked. Two identities, and the easier one is what tells you how to do the harder one.

After this page

  • You can verify a binomial identity at a point using the symmetry of the binomial coefficient.
  • You can re-index a sum by substituting a new counter and tracking what its limits become.
  • You can say what the mirror relationship means in terms of the underlying experiment.

Before you start: The binomial as a distribution, for the mass function both sides of this page use.

The one-minute version

  • X ~ Bin(n, p) and Y ~ Bin(n, 1−p). Think heads and tails on the same n tosses.
  • Two identities to verify: (a) P(X ≤ i) = P(Y ≥ n − i) and (b) P(X = k) = P(Y = n − k).
  • Do (b) first. It is one line, on the counting fact C(n, k) = C(n, n−k), and it is what suggests the substitution for (a).
  • For (a), substitute j = n − k in the right-hand sum. The limits flip and the terms become the left-hand ones exactly.
  • What it says: Y's mass function is X's read backwards, so a lower tail of one is the upper tail of the other.

1. The question, restated in full

If X and Y are binomial random variables with respective parameters (n, p) and (n, 1−p), verify and explain the following identities: (a) P(X ≤ i) = P(Y ≥ n − i); (b) P(X = k) = P(Y = n − k). The sheet cites Ross, Chapter 5, end-of-chapter Problem 7, p. 212.

The tutor pointed out that the question says verify rather than prove, and the concrete reading is the one to hold on to: if X is the number of heads in n tosses of a coin that lands heads with probability p, then Y is the number of tails on the same tosses, because tails has probability 1−p.

2. (b) The identity at a point

P(X = k) = C(n, k) pk(1−p)n−k n is the number of trials, fixed. k is the number of successes being asked about. p is the probability one trial succeeds. C(n, k), read "n choose k", counts the ways to pick which k of the n trials succeeded. The two powers are the probability of one particular such pattern, and the coefficient counts how many patterns there are.
  1. Write the left side. P(X = k) = C(n, k) pk(1−p)n−k, straight from the definition.
  2. Write the right side. Y has parameters (n, 1−p), so its success probability is 1−p and its failure probability is p. At the value n−k this gives P(Y = n−k) = C(n, n−k)(1−p)n−k pk.
  3. Compare the powers. Both sides carry pk and (1−p)n−k, only written in the other order. Nothing has to be done to them.
  4. Compare the coefficients. The only difference is C(n, k) against C(n, n−k), and those are equal: choosing which k succeed is the same act as choosing which n−k fail.
  5. Conclude. The two expressions are identical, so P(X = k) = P(Y = n−k). No manipulation was needed anywhere.

A student objected that C(n, k) = C(n, n−k) needs no proof, since it is visible from the formula. The tutor agreed and said he was taking it as given: the thing being verified is not the coefficient identity but the statement about two different random variables, and the coefficient fact is what makes it come out.

3. (a) The identity across a tail

Part (a) compares whole tails rather than single points, and the route is to re-index one sum until it becomes the other. The substitution is not obvious in isolation, and the tutor said where it comes from: part (b) pairs the value k with the value n minus k, so any sum over k on one side should become a sum over n minus k on the other.

  1. Write the left side. P(X ≤ i) = Σ over k = 0 to i of C(n, k) pk(1−p)n−k.
  2. Write the right side. P(Y ≥ n−i) = Σ over k = n−i to n of C(n, k)(1−p)k pn−k. Note that Y's success probability is 1−p, so it carries the exponent k.
  3. Substitute. Let j = n − k, which also means k = n − j.
  4. Track the limits. When k = n−i, j = i. When k = n−i+1, j = i−1. When k = n, j = 0. So j runs from i down to 0, which is the same set of terms as 0 up to i.
  5. Convert the coefficient. C(n, k) = C(n, n−k) = C(n, j).
  6. Convert the powers. (1−p)k becomes (1−p)n−j, and pn−k becomes pj.
  7. Read the result. The right side is now Σ over j = 0 to i of C(n, j) pj(1−p)n−j, which is the left side with j written where k was. A summation index is a name and nothing more, so the two sides are equal.
Checking a few numbers was raised and rejectedA student asked whether the identity could be settled by plugging in specific values instead. The tutor's answer was direct: that will not be enough. Substituting numbers confirms a case and never a claim about every n, p, i and k, and the re-indexing is the argument that covers all of them at once. He also named the one honest alternative, which is to match the two sums term by term using part (b) on each pair, and that route works because the pairing is exactly what the substitution formalises.

4. What it means

Asked whether the algebra had a picture, the tutor drew one. If X's mass function is any shape at all, Y's is that same shape flipped end for end. So the probability that X is at most i and the probability that Y is at least n−i are the same region of the same picture, seen from the two ends.

Concretely: asking for at most two heads in six tosses is the same question as asking for at least four tails in those tosses. It is one event described twice, which is why no probability had to move for the identity to hold.

Chapter 5 of Ross, A First Course in Probability carries the same pair of identities as an exercise, and treats the point version as the explanation of the tail version.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does the substitution j equals n minus k suggest itself for part (a)?
Part (b) establishes the correspondence between k on one variable and n minus k on the other, so a sum over one should become a sum over the other. The first option is a side effect rather than a motive. The third notices where the limits are but does not say why that particular substitution matches them. The fourth invents a rule that does not exist.
Six tosses of a coin that lands heads with probability 0.3. Which two events match?
With n equal to six and i equal to two, the identity pairs at most two heads with at least six minus two, that is four, tails, and the two events are literally the same outcomes. The first option pairs a lower tail with a lower tail, which is the wrong end. The second uses the right direction but the wrong threshold. The fourth replaces a tail with a single value and drops five of the outcomes.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Take n = 4 and p = 0.25, so X ~ Bin(4, 0.25) and Y ~ Bin(4, 0.75).

Compute both sides of identity (a) at i = 1, numerically, and say what the check does and does not establish.

Hint

The left side is two terms, at k equal to 0 and 1. The right side is P(Y ≥ 3), which is also two terms, at 3 and 4.

Show the worked solution
  1. Left side, k = 0. C(4,0)(0.25)0(0.75)4 = 0.3164.
  2. Left side, k = 1. C(4,1)(0.25)(0.75)3 = 4 × 0.25 × 0.4219 = 0.4219.
  3. Add. P(X ≤ 1) = 0.3164 + 0.4219 = 0.7383.
  4. Right side, k = 3. C(4,3)(0.75)3(0.25) = 4 × 0.4219 × 0.25 = 0.4219.
  5. Right side, k = 4. C(4,4)(0.75)4 = 0.3164.
  6. Add. P(Y ≥ 3) = 0.4219 + 0.3164 = 0.7383. The two sides agree.
  7. Say what it shows. It confirms this one case and nothing more. The identity is a claim about every n, p and i, and only the re-indexing covers all of them.

Sanity check. The individual terms should pair off, and they do: 0.3164 appears at k equal to 0 on the left and at k equal to 4 on the right, and 0.4219 at 1 and at 3. That term-by-term matching is part (b) showing itself in the arithmetic, which is a stronger check than the two totals agreeing.

Carry this away

  • Counting failures instead of successes gives a binomial with p replaced by one minus p.
  • At a point the identity is immediate, because choosing which succeed is choosing which fail.
  • Across a tail, substitute j equal to n minus k and track what the limits become.
  • Numerical checks confirm a case; a re-indexed sum confirms the claim.

Your teacher. When you re-index a sum, write the limit table out explicitly rather than doing it in your head: k at its bottom gives j at some value, k at its top gives j at another, and the direction of travel reverses. That table is three lines and it is where every re-indexing error lives. The tutor wrote exactly that table on the board, in the margin, before touching the terms.
