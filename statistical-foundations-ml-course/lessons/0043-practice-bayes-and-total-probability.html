Six problems, one machine - and half of them run it backwards

working 14 min Practice Set 1, sheet problems 9, 14, 25, 26, 28 and 30, each worked through to a number.

After this page

  • You can slice a two-stage experiment into cases, price the event inside each, and add - finite or infinite.
  • You can reverse the arrows with Bayes rule when the evidence is visible and its cause is not.

Before you start: The law of total probability and Bayes rule.

The one-minute version

  • All six run one machine: slice the world into cases, price the event inside each case, add. That is the law of total probability.
  • Three of them - 14, 25 and 30 - then run the machine backwards: an outcome is visible, the case that produced it is not.
  • Problems 26 and 28 slice into infinitely many cases, so the sum becomes a geometric series.
  • Every problem below is solved in full, one method each, in the practice section.
Practice, not submittedThese belong to the practice half of Practice Set 1. The four graded problems have their own pages and are due Sunday 6 September for 15 marks.

The tree, and the two directions along it

Every problem on this page is a two-level tree. Going down, you multiply a case weight by a conditional price and add the branches - that is total probability. Going up, you ask which branch produced the leaf you landed on - that is Bayes rule, and it is nothing more than one branch's share of the total.

When the slices never stop

Problems 26 and 28 hand you countably many slices instead of two or three. The total-probability sentence is unchanged; the finite sum just becomes an infinite one, and in both cases it is geometric.

Past this course so farThe course has exercised the total-probability law over finite partitions only. Problems 26 and 28 use it over an infinite one, which is what countable additivity is for. The extension is legitimate and the sheet expects it, but the formal backing has not been taught yet, so the gap is the course's and not yours.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In sheet problem 14, what decides each coin type's prior weight before the flip happens?
Priors come from the drawer's composition - two, two, one - before any evidence exists. The head probabilities are likelihoods, which only enter once the flip is observed. Labels carry no information. And the flip is evidence, so it shapes the posterior, never the prior.
In sheet problem 30, what exactly is the evidence you are updating on?
The evidence is the report - a statement whose reliability is itself uncertain - and the whole problem lives in that gap. Treating the die's six as observed is precisely the slip the problem exists to catch. The truthfulness rate is a parameter of the man, not evidence about this throw. The single throw is background setup, not the thing you learned.
Sheet problem 26 slices the space into infinitely many events. What makes slicing legal there at all?
The total-probability law needs disjoint slices that together cover everything - a partition - and problem 26 states one in so many words. Disjointness alone leaves gaps the law cannot price. Equal sizes are irrelevant; these slices shrink by halves. And C may occur in every slice - its price is given slice by slice precisely because no slice owns it.

Practice about 50 minutes, with paper

Sheet 9 - one urn, two drawsfrontier

There are n urns, numbered 1 up to n. The r-th urn holds r minus one brown balls and n minus r black balls. You pick an urn uniformly at random, then draw two balls from that urn, without replacement. First: what is the probability that the second ball drawn is black? Second: what is the conditional probability that the second ball is black, given that the first ball drawn was black?

Hint

Every urn holds exactly n minus one balls. Slice over which urn was picked, and use the identity that the numbers 0 through n minus 1 sum to n(n − 1)/2.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix the notation. Urn r holds r − 1 brown and n − r black balls, so every urn holds exactly n − 1 balls. The urn is chosen with probability 1/n each, and two balls are drawn from it without replacement, which needs n ≥ 3.
  2. Price the second ball inside one urn. Given urn r, the two draws are exchangeable: any of the n − 1 balls is equally likely to be the second one drawn. So P(second black | urn r) = (n − r)/(n − 1), the same as for the first draw.
  3. Add the slices. P(second black) = (1/n) ∑r=1n (n − r)/(n − 1). Writing m = n − r, the index m runs over 0, 1, ..., n − 1, so the sum is (1/(n(n − 1))) ∑m=0n−1 m.
  4. Evaluate. ∑m=0n−1 m = n(n − 1)/2, so P(second black) = n(n − 1) / (2n(n − 1)) = 1/2. The same computation gives P(first black) = 1/2.
  5. Price both balls black inside one urn. Given urn r, P(both black | urn r) = [(n − r)/(n − 1)] × [(n − r − 1)/(n − 2)], the second factor being the shrunken urn after one black ball has left.
  6. Add those slices. P(both black) = (1/n) · (1/((n − 1)(n − 2))) ∑m=0n−1 m(m − 1). Now ∑m=0n−1 m(m − 1) = 2 ∑m C(m, 2) = 2·C(n, 3) = n(n − 1)(n − 2)/3.
  7. Evaluate. P(both black) = [n(n − 1)(n − 2)/3] / [n(n − 1)(n − 2)] = 1/3.
  8. Divide, as the conditional definition says. P(second black | first black) = P(both black) / P(first black) = (1/3)/(1/2) = 2/3.

Sanity check. The 1/2 is forced by symmetry: relabelling urn r as urn n + 1 − r swaps the two colours and leaves the setup unchanged, so black and brown must be equally likely. The 2/3 exceeds 1/2, as it must - a black first ball is evidence that the urn is a black-rich one, and that evidence carries into the second draw. Test n = 3 by hand: the urns hold (0 brown, 2 black), (1, 1), (2, 0), and P(both black) = (1/3)(1) + 0 + 0 = 1/3, matching step 7.

Sheet 14 - one flip, three suspectsfoundation

Type A coins land heads with probability 0.5, type B with 0.7, type C with 0.9. A drawer holds five such coins - two of type A, two of type B, and one of type C. You pick one at random without seeing its type, and flip it once. The flip shows heads. What is the probability that the coin is type A? Type B? Type C?

Hint

Price the denominator P(heads) once, then take each type's share of it.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the hypotheses and their priors. The drawer's counts are 2, 2, 1 out of 5, so P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.4, P(C) = 0.2. These are the weights before any flip.
  2. Write the likelihoods. P(H | A) = 0.5, P(H | B) = 0.7, P(H | C) = 0.9, straight from the coin definitions.
  3. Price the three joint paths. P(A ∩ H) = 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.20; P(B ∩ H) = 0.4 × 0.7 = 0.28; P(C ∩ H) = 0.2 × 0.9 = 0.18.
  4. Add for the denominator. The three types partition the possibilities, so P(H) = 0.20 + 0.28 + 0.18 = 0.66.
  5. Take each share. P(A | H) = 0.20/0.66 = 20/66 = 10/33 ≈ 0.303. P(B | H) = 0.28/0.66 = 14/33 ≈ 0.424. P(C | H) = 0.18/0.66 = 9/33 = 3/11 ≈ 0.273.

Sanity check. The three posteriors sum to 10/33 + 14/33 + 9/33 = 1, as they must. Type A's weight fell from 0.4 to 0.303 and type C's rose from 0.2 to 0.273, which is the right direction: a head is weak evidence against the fair coin and for the biased ones. Type C still loses to type B because its prior is half the size, which is the lesson - a strong likelihood does not beat a weak prior on its own.

Sheet 25 - hot day, rainy dayfoundation

In a certain city X, a day is hot with probability 0.4 and cold with probability 0.6. When a day is hot, it rains with probability 0.2. When a day is cold, it rains with probability 0.7. You are told that a particular day turned out rainy. Given that, what is the probability the day was hot?

Hint

The two day types partition the world, so the denominator is a two-branch total.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the slice and the evidence. The slice is the day type - hot or cold, exactly one holds. The evidence is R, the day is rainy.
  2. Price the two rainy paths. P(hot ∩ R) = 0.4 × 0.2 = 0.08. P(cold ∩ R) = 0.6 × 0.7 = 0.42.
  3. Add for the denominator. P(R) = 0.08 + 0.42 = 0.50.
  4. Divide. P(hot | R) = 0.08 / 0.50 = 0.16, that is 4/25.

Sanity check. The answer fell from the prior 0.4 to 0.16, which is correct: rain is much more common on cold days, so seeing rain is evidence against heat. The complement P(cold | R) = 0.42/0.50 = 0.84, and 0.16 + 0.84 = 1. Note 0.16 is nothing like the given 0.2 - that number is P(rain | hot), the arrow pointing the other way.

Sheet 26 - the slice cut infinitely thinworking

There is a sequence of events Ai, one for every natural number i, where the i-th event has probability 1 over 2 to the power i. These events partition the sample space. There is also an event C, whose probability inside each slice is given: P(C | Ai) = 1 over 3 to the power i. Find P(C).

P(Ai) = 1/2i   for every i,   the Ai partition the space,   P(C | Ai) = 1/3i   ⇒   P(C) = ? Read as: Ai is the i-th slice, and its probability is one over 2 to the i. The slices are disjoint and cover the whole space - the word partition - so exactly one of them contains every outcome. P(C | Ai) is the conditional probability of C inside slice i.
Hint

Multiply the two schedules term by term and read off the common ratio.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the law for this partition. The Ai are disjoint and cover everything, so P(C) = ∑i=1∞ P(Ai) P(C | Ai). Countable additivity is what licenses the infinite sum.
  2. Fill in the general term. P(Ai) P(C | Ai) = (1/2i)(1/3i) = 1/6i.
  3. Recognise the series. P(C) = ∑i=1∞ (1/6)i, a geometric series with first term 1/6 and ratio 1/6, which is less than 1 so it converges.
  4. Sum it. A geometric series with first term a and ratio x sums to a/(1 − x), so P(C) = (1/6)/(1 − 1/6) = (1/6)/(5/6) = 1/5.

Sanity check. First confirm the slices really are a partition of the whole space: ∑ 1/2i = (1/2)/(1 − 1/2) = 1, so they account for all the probability. Then bound the answer two ways: it exceeds the first term alone, 1/6 ≈ 0.167, and it is below maxi P(C | Ai) = 1/3, since a weighted average never beats its largest input. And 0.2 sits between the two.

Sheet 28 - the bias set by the first stageworking

A positive integer I is chosen at random, where the probability that I equals n is 1 over 2 to the power n, for n running 1, 2, and so on. Once the value of I is fixed at n, a coin is tossed once - and this coin lands heads with probability e to the power minus n. What is the probability that the toss is a head?

P(I = n) = 1/2n,   P(heads | I = n) = e−n   ⇒   P(heads) = ? Read as: I is the randomly chosen integer, and the chance it equals n is one over 2 to the n. Given I = n, the coin tossed is one whose head probability is e raised to minus n, a small bias for large n. There is no single coin until the first stage lands.
Hint

Same shape as problem 26. The common ratio is 1/(2e).

Show the worked solution
  1. Slice over the value of I. The events {I = n}, for n = 1, 2, 3, ..., are disjoint and cover everything, so P(H) = ∑n=1∞ P(I = n) P(H | I = n).
  2. Fill in the general term. P(I = n) P(H | I = n) = (1/2n) e−n = (1/(2e))n.
  3. Check convergence. The ratio is 1/(2e) ≈ 0.1839, comfortably below 1, so the geometric series converges.
  4. Sum it. P(H) = (1/(2e)) / (1 − 1/(2e)). Multiply top and bottom by 2e: P(H) = 1/(2e − 1) ≈ 1/4.4366 ≈ 0.2254.

Sanity check. The answer must exceed its first term (1/(2e) ≈ 0.1839) and stay below the largest likelihood weighted over all slices: since e−n ≤ e−1 for every n ≥ 1, P(H) ≤ e−1 ≈ 0.368. The value 0.2254 sits between them. Reading the superscript wrong would show up here at once: e times n grows without bound and is not a probability at all.

Sheet 30 - the report is not the dieworking

A man is known to speak the truth three times out of four. He throws a die - one ordinary throw, the outcome hidden from you - and then reports that the throw was a six. What is the probability that the throw actually was a six?

Hint

Two hypotheses only: the throw was a six, or it was not. State what a lie means before pricing it.

Show the worked solution
  1. State the two modelling assumptions in words. The die is fair, so P(six) = 1/6 and P(not six) = 5/6. The man's truthfulness rate applies to the statement he makes: he reports what the die shows with probability 3/4, and reports something else with probability 1/4.
  2. Name the evidence. S is the event "he says six". It is not the event "the die shows six", and the whole problem lives in that gap.
  3. Price the likelihood under each hypothesis. If the throw really was a six, he says six exactly when he tells the truth: P(S | six) = 3/4. If the throw was not a six, he says six exactly when he lies in that direction: P(S | not six) = 1/4.
  4. Price the two paths. P(six ∩ S) = (1/6)(3/4) = 3/24 = 1/8. P(not six ∩ S) = (5/6)(1/4) = 5/24.
  5. Add for the denominator. P(S) = 3/24 + 5/24 = 8/24 = 1/3.
  6. Divide. P(six | S) = (3/24)/(8/24) = 3/8 = 0.375.

Sanity check. The report raised the chance from 1/6 ≈ 0.167 to 3/8 = 0.375, so it is genuine evidence - but it stays well under a half, because five faces get to produce a false "six" against one face producing a true one. Test the extremes: a perfectly truthful man gives 1·(1/6) / (1/6) = 1, and a man who is right half the time gives (1/6)(1/2) / [(1/6)(1/2) + (5/6)(1/2)] = 1/6, back to the prior, as an uninformative witness should.

Carry this away

  • Every answer here came from the same two lines: price each path as weight times conditional price, then add the paths.
  • Bayes rule is not a fifth formula - it is one path divided by the total, so the denominator is priced once and reused.
  • An infinite partition changes only the summation, and both series on this page were geometric.
  • A posterior that moved the wrong way, or a set of posteriors that does not sum to 1, is an arithmetic error you can catch on the page.
