Six problems, one machine - and half of them run it backwards

working 14 min Practice Set 1, sheet problems 9, 14, 25, 26, 28 and 30: the law of total probability and Bayes rule, exercised six ways. Every problem is restated, placed and aimed - and none is solved.

The one-minute version

  • These are practice problems: solutions are not submitted and not graded. The submitted work is the four graded problems elsewhere in this chapter, due Sunday 6 September for 15 marks.
  • All six run one machine: slice the world into cases, price the event inside each case, add. That is the law of total probability.
  • Three of them - 14, 25 and 30 - then run the machine backwards: an outcome is visible, the case that produced it is not, and Bayes rule reverses the arrows.
  • Problem 9 is the same machine with two draws from one urn; problems 26 and 28 are the machine wearing infinite clothing. Nothing on this page is solved - each entry stops at the first move.

Sheet problem 9 - one urn, two draws, two different questions

The question, in plain English. There are n urns, numbered 1 up to n. The r-th urn holds brown and black balls in sizes that depend on r: it contains r minus one brown balls and n minus r black balls. So the first urn is all black, the last is all brown, and the ones between slide from one extreme to the other. You pick an urn uniformly at random - every urn equally likely - and then draw two balls at random from that urn, without replacement. Two questions follow. First: what is the probability that the second ball drawn is black? Second: what is the conditional probability that the second ball is black, given that the first ball drawn was black?

What it is really testing. Whether the slicing machine assembles itself when the cases are handed to you implicitly. The cases are the n urns, added up by The law of total probability; the two-draw structure is priced by The multiplication rule; and the second question is the conditional definition itself, from Conditional probability. A single-draw cousin - an urn picked by a die - is worked in full on Conditioning in practice; this sheet problem adds the second draw and the reversal.

Where the wording bitesThe two questions look like twins and are not: the second is the same quantity measured inside the shrunken world where the first draw is known black, and answering one when the sheet asked the other is the main slip. Second, "without replacement" edits the second draw - the urn the second ball leaves is not the urn the first ball met. Third, the urn is picked once for the whole experiment; re-picking between draws answers a different problem. Fourth, "at random" for the urn means uniformly over the n urns - worth one sentence in any write-up.

How to start. Define the experiment in two stages: choose the urn, then draw twice from it. For the first question, slice over which urn was picked; inside urn r, price the event "second ball black" with the two-draw structure; then add the slices with their weights. For the second question, run the same slicing inside the world where the first ball came out black - or, equivalently, price the full path "first black, then black" and divide the way the conditional definition tells you. Stop once both setups are written; the counting inside urn r is the work the sheet is testing.

Weight. No marks are printed against this problem - it is practice. Difficulty: moderate; the machinery is second nature by now, and the care goes into keeping the two questions separate.

Sheet problem 14 - one flip, three suspects

The question, in plain English. Coins come in three types. Type A coins are fair: they land heads with probability 0.5. Type B coins are biased towards heads: probability 0.7. Type C coins are biased harder still: probability 0.9. Your drawer holds five such coins - two of type A, two of type B, and one of type C. You reach in and pick one coin at random, without seeing which type it is, and flip it once. The flip shows heads. What is the probability that the coin you hold is type A? Type B? Type C? Three separate questions, one flip of evidence.

What it is really testing. Whether Bayes rule fires correctly when the priors are counts rather than stated probabilities, and whether three posteriors get three separate turns. The rule and its assembly from slices live on Bayes rule, and the tree-first discipline - draw the two-level picture before writing algebra - is the whole method of Conditioning in practice. This is the sheet's cleanest Bayes drill: nothing hidden, everything priced, three answers to give.

Where the wording bitesThe prior for each type comes from how many coins of that type the drawer holds - two, two, one - and not from an equal three-way split. Type C has the biggest head probability and the smallest prior; priors and likelihoods pull different ways, which is the lesson. Second, the sheet asks three questions and wants three answers - one Bayes turn per type, sharing a denominator worth pricing once. Third, the evidence is exactly one flip; nothing licenses a second, and the posterior after one head is not the type's probability "in general".

How to start. Name the three hypotheses - the coin is type A, type B, type C - and write each one's prior from the drawer counts. Write each one's likelihood: the chance of heads given that type, straight from the list. Price the denominator once, the same way for all three questions. Then it is one Bayes turn per type. Stop there - the arithmetic is deliberately easy, and the discipline of naming hypothesis, prior and likelihood is the point of the problem.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: routine; three mechanical turns of the same handle, with the only thought going into the priors.

Sheet problem 25 - the fastest Bayes rep on the sheet

The question, in plain English. In a certain city X, a day is hot with probability 0.4 and cold with probability 0.6. When a day is hot, it rains with probability 0.2. When a day is cold, it rains with probability 0.7. You are told that a particular day turned out rainy. Given that, what is the probability the day was hot?

What it is really testing. Direction. The sheet hands you P(rain given hot) and P(rain given cold) and asks for P(hot given rain) - the exact swap Bayes rule performs, with the denominator assembled by The law of total probability. After problem 14 it is the same machine with the numbers pre-priced; its value is speed, and the confirmation that the shape, not the story, carries the method.

Where the wording bitesThe reversal is the trap: 0.2 is not "the chance a rainy day was hot" - it is the chance a hot day rains, which is a different object pointing the other way. Second, the priors 0.4 and 0.6 are for the day's type before any rain is seen; they are inputs, not answers. Third, the answer is a conditional probability, so a correct write-up shows a numerator, a denominator and the division - not a bare number.

How to start. Name the slice - hot or cold, exactly one holds - and the evidence - rain. The priors and both likelihoods are printed in the problem, so the only work is assembling the reversal. Write the Bayes shape with words in the slots first, numbers second. Stop before dividing; that division is the rep the sheet wants you to do.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: routine; if this one takes more than a few minutes, reread Bayes rule before pressing on, because 30 leans on the same turn.

Sheet problem 26 - the slice, cut infinitely thin

The question, in plain English. There is a sequence of events Ai, one for every natural number i, where the i-th event has probability 1 over 2 to the power i. These events partition the sample space: they are mutually disjoint, and together they cover everything, so exactly one of them happens. There is also another event C, and you are told its probability inside each slice: given Ai, the event C has probability 1 over 3 to the power i. Find P(C), the overall probability of C.

P(Ai) = 1/2i   for every i,   the Ai partition the space,   P(C | Ai) = 1/3i   ⇒   P(C) = ? Read as: Ai is the i-th slice, and its probability is one over 2 to the i. The slices are disjoint and cover the whole space - the word partition - so exactly one of them contains every outcome. P(C | Ai) is the conditional probability of C inside slice i, which is one over 3 to the i. The question asks for C's probability before any slice is specified.

What it is really testing. Whether the total-probability law is attached to the word partition rather than to "two or three cases". The law and its partition picture are on The law of total probability, which works finite slices; this problem hands you countably many and expects the same sentence to run unbroken. The two schedules - halves for the slices, thirds for the prices - must also stay separate; swapping them is the tempting slip.

Past this course so farThe course has exercised the total-probability law over finite partitions only. This problem uses it over an infinite one, which is exactly what countable additivity is for - the extension is legitimate and the sheet expects it to be used without ceremony - but the formal backing has not been taught yet, so the gap is the course's, not yours. The sum itself is the geometric-series kind school taught; setting it up term by term and summing it is the work.

How to start. Write the total-probability sentence for this partition in words first: C's probability is the sum over all slices of "the slice's weight times C's price inside it". Then fill in the i-th term from the two given schedules. At that point the problem is a series - set it up, sum it. Stop before summing; the setup is the probability, the sum is school algebra, and you will not need this page for either half of the checking.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: moderate; the probability content is one sentence, and the rest is a series you have summed before.

Sheet problem 28 - the coin's bias is decided by the first stage

The question, in plain English. A positive integer I is chosen at random, where the probability that I equals n is 1 over 2 to the power n, for n running 1, 2, and so on. Once the value of I is fixed at n, a coin is tossed once - and this coin lands heads with probability e to the power minus n, a bias that depends on the integer drawn. What is the probability that the resulting toss is a head?

P(I = n) = 1/2n,   P(heads | I = n) = e−n   ⇒   P(heads) = ? Read as: I is the randomly chosen integer, and the chance it equals n is one over 2 to the n. Given that I took the value n, the coin tossed is one whose head probability is e to the minus n - the number e raised to minus n, a small bias for large n. The question asks for the overall head probability, before I is revealed.

What it is really testing. The same two-stage shape as problem 26, one page later and in different clothes: an infinite first stage whose outcomes are integers, and a second stage whose behaviour is conditional on the first. The tool is again The law of total probability. The trap to refuse is treating the coin as having one fixed bias - the bias is e to the minus n, and n is random, so there is no single coin until the first stage lands.

Where the wording bitesThe head probability is e to the minus n, not e times n and not 1 over e - read the superscript slowly. Second, the stages are sequential: the integer is drawn first, and only then does the coin's identity settle. Third, the stage-one weights run over the positive integers - they start at n = 1, not n = 0 - and a write-up should say so rather than leave the range implicit.

How to start. Slice over the value of I. Slice n has weight 1 over 2 to the n and carries a conditional head chance of e to the minus n. The total-probability sentence assembles the whole thing; the resulting series is the same species as problem 26's. Stop once the series is written with its general term - summing it is algebra, and the sheet's testing stops at the setup being right.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: routine once 26 is done; it is deliberately the same problem wearing a different first stage.

Sheet problem 30 - the report is not the die

The question, in plain English. A man is known to speak the truth three times out of four. He throws a die - one ordinary throw, the outcome hidden from you - and then reports that the throw was a six. What is the probability that the throw actually was a six?

What it is really testing. Whether evidence and fact stay separated in your head when the story makes them sound the same. The reversal from the report back to the throw is Bayes rule once more, and the two hypotheses - the throw really was a six, or it was not - play the role the coin types played in problem 14. The problem's fame outlives its difficulty: it is the classic witness setup, and the sheet wants the classic discipline - name the hypotheses, price the likelihoods, turn the handle once.

Where the wording bitesThe evidence is the statement "it is a six", not the throw itself - a write-up that conditions on the die showing six has skipped the only interesting step. Second, the truthfulness rate applies to whatever the die actually showed: there is a likelihood of him saying "six" when it truly is a six, and a different one when it is not, and both must come from the three-out-of-four rate rather than from intuition. Third, the sheet says "a die" and nothing more; the natural reading is a fair die, and a careful write-up states that assumption in words rather than swallowing it silently.

How to start. Hypotheses: the throw was a six; the throw was not a six. The prior for each comes from the die itself, not from the man. The likelihoods come from his truthfulness - one for speaking truly given each hypothesis. One Bayes turn assembles the answer. Stop before the division: identifying the two likelihoods correctly is the problem, and the rest is a fraction you have turned twice already on this page.

Weight. No marks printed - practice. Difficulty: routine in execution, famous in kind; the only real work is refusing to treat the report as the outcome.

Sheet problem 9 asks for the second ball's chances twice, plainly and then given the first was black. What makes the second question different?
A condition shrinks the world: given the first ball was black, every probability is now measured inside that shrunken world, urn weights included. No new urn is drawn - both balls leave the one urn. Nothing goes back - the sheet says without replacement. And colours never move between balls; the urn's contents only change by losing a ball.
In sheet problem 14, what decides each coin type's prior weight before the flip happens?
Priors come from the drawer's composition - two, two, one - before any evidence exists. The head probabilities are likelihoods, which only enter once the flip is observed. Labels carry no information. And the flip is evidence, so it shapes the posterior, never the prior.
In sheet problem 30, what exactly is the evidence you are updating on?
The evidence is the report - a statement whose reliability is itself uncertain - and the whole problem lives in that gap. Treating the die's six as observed is precisely the slip the problem exists to catch. The truthfulness rate is a parameter of the man, not evidence about this throw. The single throw is background setup, not the thing you learned.
Sheet problem 26 slices the space into infinitely many events. What makes slicing legal there at all?
The total-probability law needs disjoint slices that together cover everything - a partition - and problem 26 states one in so many words. Disjointness alone leaves gaps the law cannot price. Equal sizes are irrelevant; these slices shrink by halves. And C may occur in every slice - its price is given slice by slice precisely because no slice owns it.
