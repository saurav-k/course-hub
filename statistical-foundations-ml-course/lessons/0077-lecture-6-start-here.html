Lecture 6: the random variable, and the two curves that describe it

foundation 9 min Eight handwritten pages of the lecturer's pre-class notes. The idea that turns an outcome into a number, the two functions that describe that number completely, and the named shapes the rest of the course will keep reaching for. Fourteen sittings.

The one-minute version

  • Everything so far has priced events: subsets of a sample space. This lecture introduces the object that lets you price a number instead, which is what every later idea in the course is built on.
  • A random variable is a function from the sample space to the real line. The notes say outright that the name is a misnomer: the function is deterministic, and the randomness sits entirely in which outcome the experiment produced.
  • Two functions describe it completely. The PMF says how much weight sits on each point. The CDF says how much weight has accumulated by each point, and it is the one that works for every random variable, discrete or not.
  • Lecture 5 met the binomial as a counting result. This lecture is where it stops being a formula and becomes a distribution: one member of a small family of named shapes that also includes Bernoulli, discrete uniform, geometric and Poisson.
  • Then mass becomes density, the Gaussian arrives with a CDF that has no closed form at all, and the lecture closes by putting a heavy tail beside a light one.

What is new here, and what is not

Every lecture up to this point has answered questions of the form "how likely is this event". An event is a set of outcomes, and the machinery for pricing one is now complete: the axioms from Lecture 3, conditional probability and independence from Lecture 4, and the counting rules that Lecture 5 put to work.

That machinery has a limit, and the limit is why this lecture exists. You cannot add two events together. You cannot ask for the average of an event, or its spread, or whether one event tends to move with another. Those questions all need numbers, and the sample space does not necessarily contain any: HHT is not a number, and neither is a pair of dice faces.

So the first move of this lecture is to attach a number to every outcome. Once that is done, the whole of the rest of the course becomes available: expectation, variance, covariance, tail inequalities and the limit theorems are all statements about numbers attached to outcomes, and none of them can be stated before this page.

How this lecture is arranged

The notes run in a straight line and these pages keep that order, with one deliberate exception. The notes define the CDF before they define the PMF, because the CDF is the more general object. These pages introduce the PMF first, because the PMF is the easier picture and the CDF is then visibly a running total of it. Nothing is added or removed by that swap, and Part 4 says so on the page.

The fourteen parts

  1. Why a number instead of an outcome. Toss a coin three times and count the heads. Roll two dice and add them. The two examples the notes open with, and what they have in common.
  2. The function with the wrong name. A random variable is a function from the sample space to the real line. The notes call the name a misnomer and this page says exactly why that matters.
  3. The support, and the PMF. A discrete random variable is one whose range is countable, and the PMF is the weight it puts on each point of that range.
  4. The CDF is a running sum. The staircase, drawn directly beneath the PMF it accumulates, and the one description that works for every random variable.
  5. Four things every CDF does. Monotone, starts at zero, ends at one, right continuous. The notes prove all four, and two of the proofs are the continuity of probability doing real work.
  6. Reading probability off the staircase. Four computations the CDF makes available, ending with the one that matters most: the size of a jump is exactly the probability of landing on that point.
  7. Bernoulli and the discrete uniform. The two simplest named shapes. One switch, and one fair choice among a run of integers.
  8. The binomial, now a distribution. The formula Lecture 5 built by counting, restated as a PMF with a support and a pair of parameters. The change is in the language, and the language is the point.
  9. The geometric, and memorylessness. The first support that never ends, and the property the notes prove it has and no other discrete shape does.
  10. The Poisson, and the shape of N. A PMF built from a single positive number, and the honest question of where its support starts.
  11. When mass becomes density. The definition that crosses over to the continuous case, and the four consequences the notes list, including the one that unsettles every reader: every single point has probability zero.
  12. The uniform and the exponential. Two densities drawn beside their CDFs, one with corners the CDF cannot smooth and one that decays forever.
  13. The Gaussian, and its missing formula. The bell, its two parameters, the standard normal, and the honest fact that its CDF cannot be written down in closed form and is computed numerically.
  14. Light tails against heavy tails. Everything above is light tailed. The Cauchy density is not, and the difference is drawn on one pair of axes.

The honesty notes, named in advance

These are pre-class notes rather than a delivered lecture, and they are terser than the class notes this course has worked from before. Five things need saying before you meet them.

The source carries almost no numbersAcross eight pages the notes state exactly one worked set of figures: the two-coin example, where each of the four outcomes carries 1/4, the PMF is 1/4, 1/2, 1/4 and the CDF climbs through 1/4 and 3/4 to 1. They also state X(HHH) = 3, X(HTH) = 2 and X((1,6)) = 7. Everything else is symbolic. So every decimal on these pages, and every curve drawn on them, is this course's own evaluation of the notes' own formulas, carried in a callout marked as such and never given a key-number mark. The policy is recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md.
Four results are stated without proof, on purposeThe notes prove the four CDF properties in full and they prove memorylessness for the geometric in full. Four other claims are asserted and left: that the geometric is the only memoryless discrete shape, that the exponential is the only memoryless continuous one (the notes write "prove it" in the margin and move on), that a continuous random variable assigns probability zero to any countable set, and that the Gaussian CDF has no closed form. These pages teach all four as the notes state them and say plainly that no proof is given here. Nothing is imported from another source to fill the gap.
The geometric's words and its formula disagreeThe notes describe the geometric as "number of coin tosses before first heads" and then write the PMF as p(1 − p)k−1. Those two do not describe the same quantity: the formula counts the toss on which the first head lands, so its smallest value is 1, while "before" would make the smallest value 0. Part 9 shows why the formula is the half to trust, and does not repair the sentence.
The letter N means two different sets on one pageThe notes give the geometric the support N and the Poisson the support N as well, and the two cannot be the same set: the geometric's masses add to one only if N starts at 1, and the Poisson's add to one only if N starts at 0. Part 10 works both sums and shows which reading each shape forces. This is a notation collision rather than an error in either formula.
The date on the source does not fit this course's timelineThe first page carries a handwritten date reading 11/8/25. This course records the delivered sessions as running from 11 August 2026 through 30 August 2026, and it states a weekday for two of them. A date on a document titled as pre-class notes is a preparation date rather than a session date, and there is no source here that reconciles the two. These pages therefore claim no delivery date for this lecture, and the divergence is recorded in PLOT.md rather than resolved.

The numbering, which is genuinely confusing

Two numbering schemes run through this course and they no longer agree. It costs one paragraph to say so, and saying so is much cheaper than the alternative.

The source document for this lecture is titled as the lecturer's Lecture 4. It is not this course's Lecture 4. This course's Lecture 4 is lessons 0045 to 0057, the applied conditional-probability pass with counting and sampling, and it is on the site already. The lecturer's own numbering and the sequence of sessions this course has published have drifted apart by one topic, in the same direction and for the same reason that the course map's roadmap already runs one lecture behind slide 20's plan.

Nothing is renumbered to make the two schemes agree. Published URLs do not move, and a lecture that has been read under one number does not get another. This session takes the next free number in this course's own sequence, which makes it Lecture 6, and PLOT.md carries a sentence recording the drift so that the next person to add a lecture is not caught by it.

What to carry out of it

How to read the pages. Every page leads with a picture, then names the mechanism, then works the numbers. Nothing is compressed: if a step is missing, that is a defect in the page and not in you. The glossary holds one-sentence definitions for every term, and the Lecture 6 formula sheet holds every expression in this lecture on one printable page, with the six named distributions in one table.
Your teacher. Carry one question through all fourteen pages: where has the randomness gone? It is the question the misnomer on Part 2 is protecting you against. The function X is a lookup table you could print out in advance; nothing about it changes when you run the experiment. What the experiment does is choose which row of that table you read. Every symbol in this lecture is on one side of that line or the other, and readers who keep the line sharp find the rest of the course much easier than readers who let X become "a number that wobbles".
