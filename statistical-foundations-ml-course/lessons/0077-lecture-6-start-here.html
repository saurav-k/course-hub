Lecture 6: the random variable, and the two curves that describe it

foundation 9 min Two sources now: eight handwritten pages of the lecturer's pre-class notes, and the delivered session of Tuesday 1 September 2026 with its own eight pages of board work. The idea that turns an outcome into a number, the two functions that describe that number completely, and the named shapes the rest of the course keeps reaching for. Fifteen sittings.

The one-minute version

  • Everything so far has priced events: subsets of a sample space. This lecture introduces the object that lets you price a number instead, which is what every later idea in the course is built on.
  • A random variable is a function from the sample space to the real line. The notes say outright that the name is a misnomer: the function is deterministic, and the randomness sits entirely in which outcome the experiment produced.
  • Two functions describe it completely. The PMF says how much weight sits on each point. The CDF says how much weight has accumulated by each point, and it is the one that works for every random variable, discrete or not.
  • Lecture 5 met the binomial as a counting result. This lecture is where it stops being a formula and becomes a distribution: one member of a small family of named shapes that also includes Bernoulli, discrete uniform, geometric and Poisson.
  • Then mass becomes density, the Gaussian arrives with a CDF that has no closed form at all, and the lecture closes by putting a heavy tail beside a light one. The room never got that far: the delivered session stopped after the Poisson and handed the continuous half to the next session, and Parts 11 to 14 carry the notes alone.
  • The delivered session also adds what a pre-class note cannot: a full walk of the staircase from minus five thousand upward, three interval computations off the die, the binomial masses evaluated at n = 3, and the room's own corrections. Part 15 carries the modelling question the class spent its longest answer on.

What is new here, and what is not

Every lecture up to this point has answered questions of the form "how likely is this event". An event is a set of outcomes, and the machinery for pricing one is now complete: the axioms from Lecture 3, conditional probability and independence from Lecture 4, and the counting rules that Lecture 5 put to work.

That machinery has a limit, and the limit is why this lecture exists. You cannot add two events together. You cannot ask for the average of an event, or its spread, or whether one event tends to move with another. Those questions all need numbers, and the sample space does not necessarily contain any: HHT is not a number, and neither is a pair of dice faces.

So the first move of this lecture is to attach a number to every outcome. Once that is done, the whole of the rest of the course becomes available: expectation, variance, covariance, tail inequalities and the limit theorems are all statements about numbers attached to outcomes, and none of them can be stated before this page.

Two sources, and exactly where they part

These pages were first written from the lecturer's handwritten pre-class notes. They now also carry the delivered session: the full transcript of the class, and the eight pages of board work written during it. Both sources are on these pages, and every page says which of the two it is quoting.

The two agree on everything they share, and they do not cover the same ground. The delivered session ran from the definition of a random variable through the CDF, its properties, the interval rules, the PMF, and the four named discrete shapes, and then stopped. Its closing words were that the continuous case comes next class. So Parts 1 to 10 now rest on two independent sources, and Parts 11 to 14 still rest on the pre-class notes alone.

How this lecture is arranged

The notes run in a straight line and these pages keep that order, with one deliberate exception. Both sources define the CDF before they define the PMF, because the CDF is the more general object, and the delivered session spent its first forty minutes on the CDF with the PMF not yet named. These pages introduce the PMF first, because the PMF is the easier picture and the CDF is then visibly a running total of it. Nothing is added or removed by that swap, and Part 4 says so on the page.

The fifteen parts

  1. Why a number instead of an outcome. Toss a coin three times and count the heads. Roll two dice and add them. The two examples the notes open with, and what they have in common.
  2. The function with the wrong name. A random variable is a function from the sample space to the real line. The notes call the name a misnomer and this page says exactly why that matters.
  3. The support, and the PMF. A discrete random variable is one whose range is countable, and the PMF is the weight it puts on each point of that range.
  4. The CDF is a running sum. The staircase, drawn directly beneath the PMF it accumulates, and the one description that works for every random variable.
  5. Four things every CDF does. Monotone, starts at zero, ends at one, right continuous. The notes prove all four, and two of the proofs are the continuity of probability doing real work.
  6. Reading probability off the staircase. Four computations the CDF makes available, ending with the one that matters most: the size of a jump is exactly the probability of landing on that point.
  7. Bernoulli and the discrete uniform. The two simplest named shapes. One switch, and one fair choice among a run of integers.
  8. The binomial, now a distribution. The formula Lecture 5 built by counting, restated as a PMF with a support and a pair of parameters. The change is in the language, and the language is the point.
  9. The geometric, and memorylessness. The first support that never ends, and the property the notes prove it has and no other discrete shape does.
  10. The Poisson, and the shape of N. A PMF built from a single positive number, and the honest question of where its support starts.
  11. When mass becomes density. The definition that crosses over to the continuous case, and the four consequences the notes list, including the one that unsettles every reader: every single point has probability zero.
  12. The uniform and the exponential. Two densities drawn beside their CDFs, one with corners the CDF cannot smooth and one that decays forever.
  13. The Gaussian, and its missing formula. The bell, its two parameters, the standard normal, and the honest fact that its CDF cannot be written down in closed form and is computed numerically.
  14. Light tails against heavy tails. Everything above is light tailed. The Cauchy density is not, and the difference is drawn on one pair of axes.
  15. Where the model came from. From the delivered session alone. A student asked why we assume a fair die when the point of an experiment is to find out whether it is fair. The answer ran through fraud scoring, fitting a parameter to history, and checking whether the fit holds.

The honesty notes, named in advance

These pages carry a terse set of pre-class notes and a delivered session that ran shorter than those notes. Seven things need saying before you meet them.

Four parts were never delivered in the roomThe session ran out of material to deliver at the Poisson and said so: the last technical words were that discrete random variables are done, and the continuous case was named as next class's work. Parts 11 to 14 - density, the uniform and exponential, the Gaussian, and the tails - come from the pre-class notes and from nowhere else in this lecture. The room's own pass over that same ground is Lecture 7, and each of those four pages links to its counterpart there. Nothing was moved or renumbered to tidy that up.
The source carries almost no numbersAcross eight pages the pre-class notes state exactly one worked set of figures: the two-coin example, where each of the four outcomes carries 1/4, the PMF is 1/4, 1/2, 1/4 and the CDF climbs through 1/4 and 3/4 to 1. They also state X(HHH) = 3, X(HTH) = 2 and X((1,6)) = 7. The delivered session adds four more worked sets, all of them stated aloud and written on the board: the die staircase in sixths, P(1 < X ≤ 3) = 2/6, P(2 < X < 5) = 2/6, the biased coin at 0.2 and 0.8, and the binomial at n = 3 and p = 1/2 giving 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8. Anything beyond those two lists is this course's own evaluation, carried in a callout marked as such and never given a key-number mark. The policy is recorded in learning-records/0002-finishing-an-arithmetic-the-lecture-leaves-open.md.
Memorylessness was asserted, not proved, in classThe pre-class notes prove the geometric's memorylessness in full. The lecturer, reaching the same property in the room, said outright that he was not going to prove it there and that the proof is in the notes. So Part 9 carries a proof the class never saw, and it labels it as the notes' proof rather than the session's. Both sources also assert, without argument, that no other discrete shape is memoryless, and neither proves it.
The geometric's wording was settled liveBoth sources describe the geometric as the number of tosses before the first head and then write the mass as (1 − p)k−1p, which counts the toss the head lands on. A student asked in class which of the two k is, and the lecturer answered plainly: k is how many tosses it took to see the first success, so a success on the first toss makes k = 1. The formula wins and the word "before" is loose. Ross settles it the same way in section 4.8.1, calling the geometric "the trial number of the first success". Part 9 carries the exchange.
One spoken figure did not match the boardIntroducing the email campaign, the lecturer wrote P(email opened) = 0.1 on the board and then said aloud "1 in 100 people actually even open the email". Those are not the same number: 0.1 is one in ten. The board figure is the one every page uses, and Part 8 names the slip rather than quietly printing the right version.
The discrete uniform is the notes' shape, not the room'sThe pre-class notes name five discrete shapes. The delivered session named four - Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson - and used the fair die throughout without ever calling it a distribution with a name. Part 7 keeps the discrete uniform because the notes define it, and says on the page that the room never named it.
The letter N means two different sets on one pageThe notes give the geometric the support N and the Poisson the support N as well, and the two cannot be the same set: the geometric's masses add to one only if N starts at 1, and the Poisson's add to one only if N starts at 0. The board makes the same split explicit and unremarked, writing k ∈ {1, 2, 3, ...} for the geometric and k ∈ {0, 1, 2, ...} for the Poisson two lines apart. Part 10 works both sums and shows which reading each shape forces. This is a notation collision rather than an error in either formula.

The numbering, which the delivered session settles

This lecture was published before its session was available, and at that point two numbering schemes were in play. The pre-class document these pages were first written from is titled as the lecturer's Lecture 4, which is not this course's Lecture 4: that is lessons 0045 to 0057, the applied conditional-probability pass with counting and sampling. The hub page used to record that mismatch as an open drift.

The delivered session closes it. The board written during the class is headed "Lecture 6" in the lecturer's own hand, and dated Tuesday 1 September 2026 at 7:28 PM. The lecturer's live numbering and this course's sequence agree, and they agree on this number. What is left is one stale title on one pre-class document, which is a filing artefact rather than a disagreement about which session this is.

Nothing was renumbered at any point. Published URLs do not move, and a lecture that has been read under one number does not get another. This session is Lecture 6 in both schemes, and the pre-class document's own title is the only thing that still says otherwise.

What to carry out of it

How to read the pages. Every page leads with a picture, then names the mechanism, then works the numbers. Nothing is compressed: if a step is missing, that is a defect in the page and not in you. The glossary holds one-sentence definitions for every term, and the Lecture 6 formula sheet holds every expression in this lecture on one printable page, with the six named distributions in one table.
Your teacher. Carry one question through all fifteen pages: where has the randomness gone? It is the question the misnomer on Part 2 is protecting you against. The function X is a lookup table you could print out in advance; nothing about it changes when you run the experiment. What the experiment does is choose which row of that table you read. Every symbol in this lecture is on one side of that line or the other, and readers who keep the line sharp find the rest of the course much easier than readers who let X become "a number that wobbles".
