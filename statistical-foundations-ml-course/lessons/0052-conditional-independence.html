Lecture 4 · Part 7 of 12 · the conditional-independence definition

Two things can be unrelated inside every world and related across all of them

working 10 min One line of definition from the board, and one worked example built here, because the board gives none.

The one-minute version

  • The definition, exactly as the notes give it: A and B are conditionally independent given C if and only if P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C) × P(B | C).
  • It is the ordinary product test, run inside the world where C is known to have happened, rather than in the whole sample space.
  • Conditional independence does not imply independence. Two events can satisfy the product test inside C and inside Cc, and fail it once the two worlds are pooled.
  • The notes state the definition and stop. The two-replica example on this page is this course's own construction, built so the claim above has something concrete behind it.

1. The definition, symbol by symbol

A ⊥ B given C  ⇔  P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C) × P(B | C) A, B and C are events, and C must have positive probability so that conditioning on it is legal. P(· | C) is the conditional probability measure: the world has shrunk to C, and every probability is now measured as a share of C rather than a share of the whole sample space. The equation is the ordinary independence product from Part 5, with every P replaced by a P-given-C. The perpendicular symbol ⊥ is the usual shorthand for "is independent of".

Nothing here is new machinery. TA Session 1, Part 5 proved that conditioning on a fixed event with positive probability produces a genuine probability space in its own right, with its own sigma-field and its own measure. Conditional independence is just ordinary independence, evaluated in that space.

The lecture defines it and gives no exampleThe class notes state the one-line definition above and move straight on to counting. There is no worked instance, no numbers, and no stated figure to quote. Everything numerical below is this course's own construction, built to give the definition something to bite on, and none of it should be attributed to the board.

2. Why the idea is worth a page at all

The interesting content of conditional independence is what it does not say. Independence given C and independence outright are different claims, and neither implies the other.

The reason is easy to state in words. Suppose two events both respond to some third thing. Inside a world where that third thing is pinned down, they have nothing left to share, and they look unrelated. Across worlds, they move together, because they are both following the same driver. Watching them without knowing the driver, you see a correlation that is entirely inherited.

3. The example, in full

This course's constructionEverything from here to the end of section 5 is invented for this page, with round numbers chosen so that every step divides exactly. It illustrates the lecture's definition; it is not the lecture's example.

A service runs two identical API replicas behind a load balancer. Both read from one shared database. In any given minute:

  • C is the event "the shared database is degraded", with P(C) = 0.1. So P(Cc) = 0.9.
  • A is the event "replica 1 returns an error in that minute".
  • B is the event "replica 2 returns an error in that minute".

The two replicas do not talk to each other. They share only the database. So the model is: given the database state, whatever each replica does is its own business. Written as numbers:

Given thatP(A | ·)P(B | ·)P(A ∩ B | ·)ProductVerdict
C, database degraded0.50.50.250.5 × 0.5 = 0.25independent inside C
Cc, database healthy0.10.10.010.1 × 0.1 = 0.01independent inside Cc

Both rows satisfy the definition, so A and B are conditionally independent given the database state. That is the model, and it is a reasonable one: nothing couples the two replicas except the thing being conditioned on.

4. Pooling the two worlds

Now ask the question an outside observer actually asks, watching error rates on a dashboard with no visibility into the database: how often does replica 1 error, and how often do both error together? Each answer is a law of total probability calculation over the partition {C, Cc}.

  1. Replica 1's overall error rate. P(A) = P(C) × P(A | C) + P(Cc) × P(A | Cc) = 0.1 × 0.5 + 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.05 + 0.09 = 0.14.
  2. Replica 2's overall error rate. Identical arithmetic, since the two replicas have the same conditional rates: P(B) = 0.14.
  3. Both together. P(A ∩ B) = P(C) × P(A ∩ B | C) + P(Cc) × P(A ∩ B | Cc) = 0.1 × 0.25 + 0.9 × 0.01 = 0.025 + 0.009 = 0.034.
  4. The product test, run outright. P(A) × P(B) = 0.14 × 0.14 = 0.0196.
  5. Compare. 0.034 against 0.0196. They are not equal, so A and B are not independent, even though they are conditionally independent given C. The joint failure is about 1.7 times as likely as the independence assumption would predict.

6. What this means for the engineering

The practical versionA reliability estimate that multiplies component failure rates is asserting independence. Almost always, the honest model is conditional independence given some shared thing: a database, a rack, a network segment, a deploy, a certificate expiry, a cloud region. The multiplication is then correct inside each state of that shared thing and wrong once the states are mixed. The repair is not to abandon the multiplication - it is to condition first, multiply inside each world, and reassemble with the law of total probability, which is exactly the four-step calculation in section 4. That is also why removing a shared dependency raises availability so much more than adding another replica does.

7. Check yourself

A and B are conditionally independent given C. What does that let you conclude about P(A ∩ B)?
Conditional independence gives the joint inside each world, and the law of total probability reassembles them: P(C) times P(A and B given C), plus the same for C-complement. Equating it to P(A) times P(B) is the mistake this whole page exists to break. It is not always larger either - the direction depends on whether the conditional rates move together or in opposition. And it is perfectly computable: section 4 computes it in four lines.
What would have to be true for the two replicas to be independent outright, not merely given C?
If P(A given C) equals P(A given C-complement), the database state carries no information about either replica, the two worlds are the same table, and mixing them changes nothing - so conditional independence becomes plain independence. Making the database more reliable shrinks the gap but never closes it: any positive P(C) with different conditional rates leaves a dependence. The overall rate is 0.14 by construction and is not a lever. And separate regions would help by removing the shared driver, which is a real fix but a change to the system rather than a condition on these numbers.
Your teacher. Say the two sentences aloud until they stop sounding contradictory: "given the database state, the replicas are unrelated" and "watching the dashboard, the replicas are clearly related". Both are true of the same system, and the second is entirely inherited from the driver named in the first. Every time somebody says "we controlled for that", the equation they are claiming is the one at the top of this page - and it is worth asking whether they checked it or assumed it.
