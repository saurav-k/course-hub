Lecture 8 · Worked example · the delivered session

You cannot maximise a random profit, so you maximise its average instead

13 min The last thirty minutes of the delivered session, worked end to end. A retailer picks a stock level, the demand refuses to be known in advance, and one expectation turns an unanswerable question into a quadratic with a single answer.

After this page

  • You can write a decision's payoff as a function of a random variable, and say why that payoff is itself random.
  • You can take the expectation of a function of a variable by integrating the function against the variable's own density, without deriving a new distribution for the payoff.
  • You can split an integral at the point where a min changes form, evaluate both halves, and finish with the two derivative tests the class insisted on.

Before you start: The indicator trick, and the expected-value rule from Part 5, which is the single tool this page spends.

The one-minute version

  • A retailer stocks Q units for tomorrow. Demand D is uniform on [0, 100], each unit costs 60 and sells for 100.
  • Units sold is min(D, Q), never more than the shelf holds and never more than the day asks for, so profit is 100 min(D, Q) − 60Q.
  • That profit is a random variable, because D is. There is nothing to maximise until you replace it with a number, and the expectation is that number.
  • The min changes form at D = Q, so the integral splits there. The two halves give −10Q2 and 40Q(100 − Q), and together 40Q − 0.5Q².
  • Setting the derivative 40 − Q to zero gives Q* = 40. A student stopped the class to demand the second-derivative test, which is −1, so the point is a maximum.
  • The best expected profit is 800, and the optimal stock is below the average demand of 50. That gap is the whole lesson.

1. The picture: a number you must commit to before you know anything

The retailer must decide tonight how much to put on the shelf for tomorrow. Two things can go wrong, and they pull in opposite directions. Stock too little and customers arrive who cannot be served, so the margin on those units is lost. Stock too much and the surplus is bought at cost and thrown away, so the cost of those units is lost outright.

The lecturer's framing in the session was deliberately domestic: soak the rice and the dal tonight, decide how much dosa batter to prepare, and find out tomorrow whether the guess was right. Today the demand was 50.5 kilograms, tomorrow is a weekend and it might be 65, the day after it might be 75.

Yesterday's numbers do not settle tomorrow's, and no single stock level is right on every day. What can be settled is which stock level is best on average over many days, and an expectation is exactly the machine for turning a whole distribution of outcomes into one number worth optimising.

2. The mechanism: profit as a function of the demand

Name the two quantities before writing anything down, because the whole example turns on keeping them apart.

Π(Q, D) = 100 · min(D, Q) − 60Q Q is the stock level, a number the retailer chooses tonight and then holds fixed. It is not random. D is tomorrow's demand, the only random quantity anywhere on this page, distributed uniformly on [0, 100]. min(D, Q) is the number of units actually sold: the day cannot buy more than it wants, and the shelf cannot supply more than it holds. 100 is the sale price of one unit, so 100 min(D, Q) is the day's revenue. 60 is the cost of one unit, and all Q units are bought whether or not they sell, so 60Q is the day's cost and it does not depend on D at all. Π is the resulting profit, and because D is random, Π is random too.

Two objections from the room are worth carrying, because both are the natural first reaction.

"Shouldn't the demand run from 0 to Q rather than 0 to 100?" No. Customers do not know what is on the shelf, so demand arrives from its own distribution regardless of Q. What the shelf limits is not the demand but the sales, and that limit is already expressed by the min. The lecturer's answer was direct: if demand exceeds the stock, sales are simply lost, and the model shows that loss rather than hiding it by shrinking the demand.
"If demand is above Q, surely you earn nothing on the excess, so why is the revenue 100Q?" The excess earns nothing, and 100Q is exactly the statement that it earns nothing: revenue stops at the Q units you had. The lecturer worked it in kilograms live. Stock 10, demand 30, and you sell 10: revenue 100 × 10, cost 60 × 10, profit 40 × 10. Profit on a sold-out day is 40Q, not zero.

3. The tool: expectation of a function, not of the demand

A student in the session asked the sharpest question of the hour: the mean of a uniform is (a + b)/2, which is 50 here, so why not use it? The lecturer's answer is the reason this example sits in an expectation lecture at all.

The mean of the demand is not the answer to anything here. E[D] = 50 is true and is not what is wanted. The quantity being averaged is the profit, which is a function of the demand, and the average of a function is not the function of the average. Substituting 50 into the profit expression answers a different question: what profit a perfectly typical day would bring, which is not the same as what an average day brings once the good and bad days are weighed against their own probabilities. The rule that keeps this straight is the expected-value rule from Part 5: put the function in the value slot and leave the density exactly as it was.
E[g(D)] = ∫−∞∞ g(x) · fD(x) dx g is any real-valued function of the variable; here it is the profit, g(x, Q) = 100 min(x, Q) − 60Q, with Q held fixed. fD is the density of the demand, unchanged and unaffected by g. x ranges over the values the demand can take. Read it as before: the value of the payoff at x, times the weight the demand attaches to x, accumulated across every x. No distribution for the profit is ever derived, which is the entire point; the lecturer said plainly that he did not know the density of the profit and did not need it.

4. The numbers, worked in full

The density of the demand is 1/100 on [0, 100] and zero elsewhere, so the infinite limits collapse to that interval and the constant comes straight out.

  1. Write the expectation. E[Π] = ∫0100 g(x, Q) · (1/100) dx = (1/100) ∫0100 g(x, Q) dx. The 1/100 is a constant because the demand is uniform, and nothing else about the density is used anywhere below.
  2. Cut the integral where the function changes. min(x, Q) equals x below Q and equals Q above it, so E[Π] = (1/100)[∫0Q g dx + ∫Q100 g dx]. A single antiderivative for the whole range does not exist, which is the only reason the split happens.
  3. Substitute the low-demand form. For 0 ≤ x ≤ Q the profit is 100x − 60Q, so the first piece is ∫0Q (100x − 60Q) dx. Note that Q is a constant inside this integral, not the variable being integrated.
  4. Integrate the first piece term by term. 100 ∫0Q x dx = 100 [x2/2]0Q = 50Q2, and 60Q ∫0Q 1 dx = 60Q · Q = 60Q2.
  5. Collect the first piece. 50Q2 − 60Q2 = −10Q2. It is negative, which is correct and worth pausing on: on the days when demand falls short you have paid for stock you could not sell.
  6. Substitute the high-demand form. For Q ≤ x ≤ 100 the profit is 100Q − 60Q = 40Q, a constant with no x in it at all.
  7. Integrate the second piece. A constant integrated over an interval is the constant times the width: ∫Q100 40Q dx = 40Q(100 − Q).
  8. Expand and combine. 40Q(100 − Q) = 4000Q − 40Q2, so E[Π] = (1/100)[−10Q2 + 4000Q − 40Q2] = (1/100)[4000Q − 50Q2].
  9. Divide through. 4000/100 = 40 and 50/100 = 0.5, giving E[Π(Q)] = 40Q − 0.5Q². This step was asked for twice from the floor and worked twice on the board.
  10. Read what you have. The randomness is gone. What is left is an ordinary downward-opening parabola in the one thing the retailer controls.

5. The optimum, and the test the class insisted on

  1. Differentiate. d/dQ (40Q − 0.5Q2) = 40 − 2(0.5)Q = 40 − Q. The factor 2 comes down from the square, and it cancels the half exactly.
  2. Set the derivative to zero. 40 − Q = 0, so Q* = 40. This is a stationary point, and so far nothing says whether it is a peak or a trough.
  3. Apply the second-derivative test. d2/dQ2 (40Q − 0.5Q2) = −1. It is −1 everywhere, and negative means the slope is falling through the stationary point, so the point is a maximum.
  4. Evaluate the maximum. E[Π(40)] = 40(40) − 0.5(402) = 1600 − 800 = 800 per day, on average.
The lecturer stopped at the first derivative and a student corrected himThe board went straight from 40 − Q = 0 to Q* = 40. A student pointed out that the second derivative has to be checked for a maximum, and the lecturer agreed at once: "You are absolutely right. I was a little bit sloppy here." He then worked the test, and a second student asked what the test means, which produced the sketch of the two parabolas that both have a zero derivative at their stationary point. The typeset handout for the same example carries the same admission in its own remark, that ideally one should do the second-derivative test as well. The correction is part of the material, not an embarrassment attached to it, and it is the reason this page works both tests rather than one.
This course's arithmetic. The lecture stops at Q* = 40 and never compares it to the mean demand. The comparison is worth one line: the margin gained on a sold unit is 100 − 60 = 40 and the cost sunk into an unsold one is 60, so the fraction 40 / (40 + 60) = 0.4 is the share of the demand distribution you should be prepared to serve. On a uniform over [0, 100] that fraction of the range is 0.4 × 100 = 40, which reproduces the answer without any calculus. None of this ratio appears in the source; it is this course's own reading of the lecture's own numbers, and it is the standard newsvendor criterion.

6. What changes if the demand is not uniform

A student asked whether the demand could be skewed instead, and the answer settles what is general here and what is not. The uniform assumption enters at exactly one place, step 1, where the density came out of the integral as the constant 1/100. Everything before that is general and everything after that is arithmetic on a particular density.

With any other density the same procedure runs unchanged: write the payoff as a function of the demand, split where the min changes form, integrate each piece against that density, then optimise. The optimal Q will be different, and, as the lecturer said, it will definitely be different. He also gave the practical route for a real shelf: track the demand historically, build an empirical distribution from what you saw, and put that in the density slot.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the expectation integral split into two pieces at x = Q?
Below Q every extra unit of demand is sold, so profit reads 100x − 60Q; above Q the shelf is empty and profit is stuck at 40Q. Two forms need two integrals. The first option is wrong because a uniform density is the same constant across the whole interval, and the split has nothing to do with the density. The third is the objection the room raised and the lecturer refused: demand is not capped by stock, only sales are. The fourth invents a price change that is nowhere in the model, where both prices are fixed.
Why does the optimal stock of 40 sit below the mean demand of 50?
A unit that does not sell burns the full cost of 60, while a sale you could not serve forgoes only the margin of 40, so the penalties are lopsided and the optimum leans toward stocking less. The first option is false: the mean of a uniform is exactly its midpoint, which is where the 50 came from. The second is false, since the expected profit is a genuine quadratic and that is why it has a peak. The third contradicts the model, where unsold units have no salvage value at all.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1the same machine, new numbers

The same retailer, the same uniform demand on [0, 100], but a premium item: cost 20 per unit and sale price 100 per unit.

Find the expected profit as a function of Q, the optimal stock, and the best expected profit. Then say in one sentence why the answer moved the way it did.

Hint

Nothing about the structure changes. Redo steps 3 to 9 with 20 in place of 60, and watch what happens to the constant that multiplies Q in the second piece.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the profit. g(x, Q) = 100 min(x, Q) − 20Q, so below Q it is 100x − 20Q and above Q it is 100Q − 20Q = 80Q.
  2. First piece. ∫0Q (100x − 20Q) dx = 50Q2 − 20Q2 = 30Q2. It is positive this time, because at a cost of 20 even a half-empty day pays.
  3. Second piece. ∫Q100 80Q dx = 80Q(100 − Q) = 8000Q − 80Q2.
  4. Combine and divide by 100. (1/100)[30Q2 + 8000Q − 80Q2] = (1/100)[8000Q − 50Q2] = 80Q − 0.5Q2.
  5. Differentiate and solve. 80 − Q = 0, so Q* = 80. The second derivative is again −1, negative, so it is a maximum.
  6. Evaluate. 80(80) − 0.5(6400) = 6400 − 3200 = 3200 per day.
  7. Say why it moved. The margin rose to 80 and the cost of a wasted unit fell to 20, so understocking now hurts four times as much as overstocking, and the optimum jumps above the mean demand instead of sitting below it.

Sanity check. The newsvendor ratio predicts it without the calculus: 80 / (80 + 20) = 0.8, and 0.8 of the range [0, 100] is 80, which matches. A second check: Q* must stay inside [0, 100], because stocking beyond the largest possible demand only adds cost, and 80 does. Every number in this practice is this course's own.

Carry this away

  • A decision whose payoff depends on a random quantity has a random payoff, and the expectation is what makes it optimisable at all.
  • The expected value of a function of a variable is computed against the variable's own density, with no new distribution derived for the function.
  • A min or a max inside a payoff means the integral splits at the point where the form changes.
  • A stationary point is not a maximum until the second derivative says so, which the class had to supply from the floor.
  • The optimum leans away from the more expensive mistake, which is why 40 rather than the average demand of 50.

Your teacher. The move worth stealing from this page is not the integral, it is the sentence before it: the only randomness here is in the demand. Say that out loud about any decision problem and the rest usually writes itself, because everything that is not random comes out of the expectation as a constant and everything that is random stays in the value slot. When you meet a payoff with a min, a max or a threshold in it, look for the point where its form changes before you look for an antiderivative. Then ask an agent to simulate ten thousand days of this shelf at Q = 30, 40 and 50 and average the profits, and watch the three averages come out near 750, 800 and 750: the parabola is genuinely flat near its top, which is why a real retailer stocking 45 loses very little.
