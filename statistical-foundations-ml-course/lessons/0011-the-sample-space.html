The sample space is the set of everything that could happen

foundation 8 min The first letter of the triple (Omega, F, P): one set that lists every outcome an experiment can produce, and three examples that get progressively less obvious.

The one-minute version

  • A random experiment is any process whose result is not known in advance; an outcome, written omega, is one result of it.
  • The sample space, written Ω ("capital omega"), is the set of all possible outcomes, with nothing missing and nothing extra.
  • The deck's three examples are a coin (two outcomes), the speed of a coin (the positive reals), and tosses until the first head (the positive integers).
  • The third example is the load-bearing one: a sample space need not be finite, and that fact forces the machinery of Parts 4 and 5.

1. The definition, in one line

Ω = the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment Read as: "capital Omega is the sample space". Ω is a set in the Part 1 sense, its elements are the outcomes, and each outcome is written ω, "little omega". One run of the experiment produces exactly one ω.

Two demands hide inside the word "all". The list must be mutually exclusive, meaning no outcome appears twice, so that the experiment never lands on two list entries at once. And it must be collectively exhaustive, meaning nothing that could happen is missing, so that the experiment never lands off the list. Both are ordinary set hygiene: the outcomes are distinct elements, and together they are the whole set.

2. Three examples, each stranger than the last

The deck makes three choices of Ω for three different things you might observe about tossing a coin. They are worth working in order, because each one breaks an assumption the previous one quietly made.

The first is the obvious one. The second asks the coin's speed as it spins, and a speed can be any non-negative real number, so Ω is R+, the positive reals: not a list but a continuum. The third asks how many tosses until the first head, and that is a whole number with no upper bound: Ω = {1, 2, 3, ...}, the positive integers. Ten heads in a row is allowed, and so is ten thousand, so the list never ends.

3. Why the third example runs the rest of the lecture

Here is the consequence in one concrete sentence. On the third space, the deck asks about "the number of tosses is odd", which is the set {1, 3, 5, 7, ...}. No finite list of coin tosses covers that question: it is a union of infinitely many single-element sets, and Part 4's axioms are built so that such a set is still an event with a probability. Had the deck stopped at the two-outcome coin, a short list would have done, and most of this lecture would be unnecessary. The infinite sample space is not a curiosity; it is the reason the machinery exists.

Same coin, different spacesNothing physical changed across the three examples: the same coin was tossed each time. What changed is which quantity you promised to record. Choosing the sample space is a modelling decision, and the deck's advice is to record enough to answer the questions you care about, and nothing more.

Check yourself

An app records how many seconds a user spends on a page, and any non-negative value is possible. What is the sample space?
Seconds spent can be any non-negative value, 2.5 included, so the space is the non-negative reals and not a countable list. A list of whole seconds loses the fractional visits. The positive integers would be right for "how many whole seconds", which is a different promise. Two outcomes answers a different question entirely, staying or leaving.
Why does the deck bother with the "toss until the first head" example at all?
Its sample space is {1, 2, 3, ...}, endless, so questions like "the count is odd" are unions of infinitely many outcomes and the sigma-field of Part 4 has to exist. The coin is just a convenient prop, and the deck uses three different spaces on it. The space exists fine, and long runs are permitted in it, which is precisely the point.
Your teacher. Before turning the page, look at any metric your own systems report and try to state its sample space in one sentence. Latency, queue depth, retry count: each one forces you to decide finite, countable, or continuous, and that decision is exactly the skill this page teaches. If you cannot state the space, you do not yet know what the metric promises to record.
