The exponential tilts the odds towards small values, and its tail decides the loan

working 13 min The second named density, delivered live, and the moment the loan question from Lecture 1 becomes one subtraction.

After this page

  • You can turn "the chance of exceeding a threshold" into one minus a CDF, and evaluate it for the exponential.
  • You can fit a rate to data by matching the sample average, and say what that fit assumes.

Before you start: The uniform and the bus you just missed, for a density and its CDF worked end to end.

The one-minute version

  • The exponential with rate λ is the shape for a quantity where small values are likelier than large ones, and it lives only on the non-negative numbers.
  • Its density is λe−λx for x ≥ 0 and 0 otherwise. That brace is two cases, not a fraction.
  • At zero the density is exactly λ. It falls towards the axis for ever and never touches it, reaching zero only in the limit.
  • Its CDF is 1 − e−λx, so the chance of exceeding x is the clean tail e−λx.
  • The loan rule from Lecture 1 is that tail: approve when e−2000λ falls under 5%. What it costs: the whole answer rests on the shape being right.

1. The density, and the brace that is not a fraction

The lecturer set the exponential against the uniform directly. A uniform mimics a choice where all values are equally likely; an exponential is one where smaller values are more likely than higher ones. Only one parameter carries that, a rate the notes write as λ, required to be strictly positive.

fX(x) = λe−λx for x ≥ 0, and 0 otherwise X is the random quantity. fX is its density, the height of the curve at each point. λ, lambda, is the single parameter, a strictly positive number called the rate. e is the base of natural logarithms, about 2.718, and e−λx shrinks as x grows, faster for larger λ. The whole line reads as two cases: on the non-negative numbers the height is that decaying curve, and on the negative numbers the height is flat zero.

A student read the large brace as a division sign and asked whether something was being divided by zero. The lecturer's answer is worth carrying, because the notation appears on every piecewise definition in the course: the brace is a case list. It says "in this regime the density is this, and in the other regime it is that", and the 0 on the second line is the value the density takes for negative x, not a denominator. A quantity modelled as exponential cannot be negative, so there is no density there to write.

Two more student questions pinned down the shape. First, the height at the very start: put x = 0 into λe−λx and the exponential factor is e0 = 1, so the density at zero is exactly λ. Hajek states the same fact as fT(0) = λ in Section 3.4 of Probability with Engineering Applications. Second, whether the curve ever lands on the axis: it does not. It approaches zero and gets to zero only as x tends to infinity, which the lecturer confirmed twice.

2. The CDF, and the tail worked out

FX(x) = 1 − e−λx for x ≥ 0, and 0 for x < 0 FX(x) is the cumulative distribution function at threshold x: the probability that X lands at or below x. λ is the same rate as in the density. The subtraction from 1 is what turns a decay into a climb: as x grows, e−λx shrinks and the whole expression rises towards 1 without arriving.

The lecturer wrote this line straight down and did not evaluate the integral on the board. Two checks make it trustworthy anyway: at x = 0 it reads 1 − 1 = 0, and as x grows it climbs towards 1 without exceeding it. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis derive the same expression in Example 3.6 of Introduction to Probability.

  1. Name the event. "The spend exceeds 2000" is the event X > 2000.
  2. Turn it into a CDF question. Every outcome either exceeds 2000 or does not, so P(X > 2000) = 1 − P(X ≤ 2000) = 1 − FX(2000).
  3. Substitute the CDF. 1 − (1 − e−2000λ).
  4. Cancel the two ones. P(X > 2000) = e−2000λ. The tail of an exponential is the exponential factor on its own, with no leading λ and no subtraction left.

3. The loan decision, closed

Lecture 1 opened this course with a lender deciding whether to approve an applicant, and stated the rule without the machinery to evaluate it: model the daily spend, and reject if the chance of a day above 2000 rupees is 5% or more. Seven lectures later the model has a name.

approve when e−2000λ < 0.05 λ is the rate fitted to this one applicant's spending history. 2000 is the rupee threshold the lender chose, and 0.05 is the risk the lender is willing to carry. The left-hand side is the share of days on which this applicant spends more than 2000.
  1. Fit the rate to a careful spender. Their daily spend averages 500 rupees. The mean of an exponential is 1/λ, so set 1/λ = 500 and get λ = 0.002.
  2. Evaluate the tail. e−2000 × 0.002 = e−4 = 0.0183, or 1.83% of days.
  3. Apply the rule. 1.83% is below 5%, so approve.
  4. Fit the rate to a looser spender. Their daily spend averages 1000 rupees, so λ = 0.001.
  5. Evaluate that tail. e−2000 × 0.001 = e−2 = 0.1353, or 13.53% of days.
  6. Apply the rule again. 13.53% is well above 5%, so reject.
  7. Find the exact boundary. The two verdicts meet where e−2000λ = 0.05. Taking natural logs, 2000λ = ln 20 = 2.9957, so λ = 0.0014979 and the mean spend at the boundary is 1/λ = 667.6 rupees a day.

4. What the rate means, and how it is fitted

A student asked what λ represents, having been told that the Gaussian's parameters mean something. The lecturer answered that the expected value of an exponential is 1/λ: a large rate pushes the quantity towards small values, a small rate lets it spread further out. Hajek and Bertsekas both derive that mean by parts.

The follow-up question was the practical one, how you choose λ at all. The lecturer's method: take the average of your dataset and set it equal to 1/λ-hat, because that reciprocal is the mean the model claims. Solve, and the fitted rate is one over the sample average. The hat marks it as an estimate rather than a known truth.

The live session added what a written note has no room for: the three applications named aloud, the loan rule from Lecture 1 closed with the tail identity, the two student questions that pinned the value at zero and the behaviour at infinity, and the recipe for fitting the rate from a sample average.

5. Where the ground is soft

The other two applications the lecturer named are the classical ones: service times in a queue, such as how long a supermarket till takes over one customer, and the lifetime of a component or a piece of hardware.

Every number here rests on an unchecked shapeThe tail identity is exact, and the arithmetic above is this course's own. What is not established anywhere is that daily spend really is exponential. Choosing that shape fixes a great deal at once: the most likely spend becomes zero, the standard deviation is forced to equal the mean, and the whole risk of the applicant collapses into one number. The lecture states the model and moves on, saying only that the rate had been estimated earlier and never giving its value, so no loan figure in this lecture is quotable and every one on this page was computed here. In practice a lender would check the fitted shape against the spending history before trusting a 1.83% against a 5% bar.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A component's lifetime is Exp(0.01) in hours. What is the chance it is still running after 100 hours?
Still running after 100 hours is the event X exceeds 100, and its probability is the tail e to the minus lambda x, here e to the minus one. The first option is the CDF, which answers the opposite question of failing by 100 hours. The second confuses the rate, a density height per hour, with a probability. The fourth mistakes a curve that never reaches zero for a probability that never falls below one.
Fitting an exponential to spending data whose daily average is 250, what rate does the lecturer's recipe give?
The model's mean is one over lambda, so setting one over lambda equal to 250 gives lambda equal to 1/250, which is 0.004. The second option skips the reciprocal and would put the rate in rupees. The third takes a number from the decision rule, which has nothing to do with fitting the model. The fourth invents a halving rule: the rate is inversely proportional to the mean, so halving the mean doubles the rate.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A checkout till is modelled as taking Exp(λ) minutes per customer, and its logged service times average 2.5 minutes. The store wants no more than 10% of customers to take longer than 5 minutes.

Find the fitted rate, the share of customers who exceed 5 minutes, and whether the target is met.

Hint

Fit the rate from the average first, then use the tail identity rather than integrating anything. You need e to the minus two, which is about 0.135.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fit the rate. The model's mean is one over lambda, so 1/λ = 2.5 and λ = 0.4 per minute.
  2. Write the tail. The chance of exceeding 5 minutes is e to the minus lambda times 5, which is e to the minus 0.4 times 5.
  3. Evaluate the exponent. 0.4 times 5 = 2, so the tail is e to the minus 2 = 0.1353.
  4. Compare with the target. 13.53% exceeds the 10% ceiling, so the target is not met.
  5. Say what would meet it. A 10% tail needs an exponent of ln 10 = 2.3026, so λ must reach 2.3026/5 = 0.4605, which is a mean service time of 2.17 minutes.

Sanity check. Five minutes is exactly twice the average, and the exponential gives a tail of about 13.5% at twice its mean whatever the units, so the answer should not depend on the numbers being minutes. It does not: the exponent is 2 either way.

Carry this away

  • The exponential density is a two-case definition: a decay on the non-negative numbers, flat zero below them.
  • It starts at height λ and approaches the axis without ever touching it, reaching zero only in the limit.
  • Its tail is clean: the chance of exceeding x is e to the minus lambda x, with nothing left over.
  • The rate is fitted by matching the sample average to one over lambda, and that fit is the weakest link in the loan decision.
