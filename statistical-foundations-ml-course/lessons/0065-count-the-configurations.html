Lecture 5 · Part 7 of 10 · the four-image enumeration

Counting is still the right move, as long as every counted thing comes with its price attached

10 min the repair The bridge from counting to the binomial, checked by hand on a case small enough to write out in full.

The one-minute version

  • Part 6 killed the shortcut, not the counting. The general rule is that P(A) is the sum of the weights of the outcomes inside A.
  • When those outcomes all happen to carry the same weight w, the sum is just |A| × w. Counting still does the heavy work; it simply hands its answer to a multiplication instead of to a division.
  • The board checks this on four images with two errors, small enough to list. The configurations are CCEE, ECCE, EECC, ECEC, CECE and one more, six in all, and every one of them is worth (0.8)2(0.2)2.
  • So Pr(2 errors in 4 images) = |A| · (0.8)2(0.2)2, exactly as the notes write it.
  • That is the binomial. Put |A| = C(n, k) and w = pk(1 − p)n−k and Part 5's formula is this page with letters in it.

1. The rule, in three steps

The class notes write the argument as a chain of three equalities. Read left to right it says: the probability of getting two errors is the probability that the configuration produced lands somewhere in A, which is the sum over the members of A of the probability of producing that particular member, which is the size of A times the shared value.

Pr(2 errors) = Pr(configuration lies in A) = ∑i=1..|A| Pr(configuration is Ai) = |A| · (0.8)2(0.2)2 A is the set of all configurations with exactly two errors, and Ai is the i-th of them. |A| is how many there are. The middle step is the third axiom: the configurations are disjoint, since one evaluation produces exactly one of them, so the probability of the union is the sum of the parts. The last step is the only one that needs a condition, and the condition is that every term in the sum is the same number.

Step 1 is the one people skip, and it is the one Part 6 exists to make unskippable. Here it passes: every configuration with exactly two errors contributes two factors of 0.2 and two of 0.8, so all of them are worth the same, and it does not matter where in the string the errors sit.

2. Four images and two errors, listed in full

The board shrinks the problem until it can be checked by hand. Four images instead of ten, two errors instead of three, and the same classifier with an error probability of 0.2.

A configuration is now a four-letter string. There are 24 = 16 of them in total, and A is the set with exactly two E's in it.

One row of the board cannot be readFive of the six configurations are clearly legible on the class notes. The sixth row's first letter is overwritten and will not resolve into a C or an E. This course does not guess at the ink. Instead the missing row is fixed by the mathematics: exactly two of the four positions must hold an E, there are C(4, 2) = 6 such strings, and the five legible rows account for CCEE, ECCE, EECC, ECEC and CECE. The only string left is CEEC, so that is the sixth row and it is listed above as A6. The value of the argument does not depend on the reading either way, because the count of six is what the formula uses and the count is forced.

3. Working it out

  1. Check the weights. Every string in A has two E's and two C's, so each contributes 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.8 × 0.8 in some order. All six are worth the same. Step 1 passes.
  2. Price one of them. (0.8)2(0.2)2 = 0.64 × 0.04 = 0.0256. That decimal is this course's arithmetic; the notes leave the powers standing.
  3. Count them. |A| = 6, and the list above shows all six. The same count comes from the formula: choosing which two of the four positions hold an error is C(4, 2) = 6.
  4. Multiply. |A| · (0.8)2(0.2)2 = 6 × 0.0256 = 0.1536, again this course's arithmetic.

The reason for shrinking the problem is that at this size the answer can be checked exhaustively. There are only sixteen configurations in total, and every one of them can be assigned to a group by its error count. Grouping them and adding up the groups must give exactly one, and it does.

Both checks are this course's own arithmetic and both are worth running whenever this method is used on a problem too big to list.

4. Why this page is the binomial

Put the small case and the general formula side by side and there is nothing left to prove.

On this pageIn the general formulaWhat it is
|A| = 6C(n, k)A plain count of configurations, from case 3
(0.2)2pkThe k errors, priced by independence
(0.8)2(1 − p)n−kThe other n − k results, all correct
|A| · (0.8)2(0.2)2C(n, k) pk(1 − p)n−kCount times shared weight

The binomial formula is not a separate result to be memorised alongside the counting rules. It is one line of counting and one line of independence, joined by the observation that every configuration in the group costs the same. That observation is the whole content of Part 6 and this page together, and it is why the two pages sit next to each other.

5. Check yourself

What condition makes "count them, then multiply by one weight" a legal step?
The sum of the weights collapses into a count times a single weight only when every term in the sum is that same weight, which here holds because all six configurations contain two errors and two correct results. Nonzero probability is not enough: the sixteen configurations here all have nonzero weight and they still fall into five different values. Outcomes are never independent of each other in any useful sense, since they are mutually exclusive rather than independent. And the size of the event relative to the space is irrelevant to whether the multiplication is valid.
For four images with exactly two errors, how many configurations are there and why?
Fixing which two of the four positions hold an E fixes the whole configuration, and there are C(4, 2) = 6 such choices, which is exactly the list on this page. Four is the number of images, not the number of two-error configurations, and it happens to be the count for the one-error and three-error groups instead. Sixteen counts every configuration of four letters, across all five error counts, and only six of those have exactly two errors. And two is the number of errors itself, which the question has already fixed.
Your teacher. Do this one on paper before you move on. Take four images, write out all sixteen strings of C and E in a column, put the weight beside each, and add the column up. It takes about eight minutes and it is the only time in this lecture you will see the entire sample space at once with its weights visible. Everything on the previous page and this one becomes obvious from that column, and it stays obvious afterwards in a way that reading about it does not.
