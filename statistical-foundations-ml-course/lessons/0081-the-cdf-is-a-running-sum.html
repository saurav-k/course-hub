Lecture 6 · Part 4 of 14 · the CDF

Stop asking how much mass is here and start asking how much has accumulated so far

11 min the second description The other function that describes a random variable, and the one that survives everything later in the lecture.

The one-minute version

  • The cumulative distribution function of X is FX(x) = P(X ≤ x): the probability that the value comes out at x or anywhere below it.
  • For a discrete random variable it is a running sum of the PMF, FX(x) = ∑ pX(i) taken over the points i of the support with i ≤ x.
  • Drawn, it is a staircase. It is flat between the points of the support and jumps at each one, and the notes say it is monotone, starts at 0, goes to 1, and can have jumps.
  • Every jump is exactly as tall as the mass that caused it. The two pictures on this page share a vertical scale so that you can measure this rather than take it on faith.
  • The CDF is defined for every random variable. The PMF is not, and Part 11 is where that difference starts to matter.
A note on the order of these pages. The notes define the CDF two pages before they define the PMF, because the CDF is the more general object and needs no discreteness. These pages take the PMF first, because it is the easier picture and the CDF is then visibly a total of it. No content is added or dropped by the swap. If you read the source alongside, expect the order to differ and nothing else to.

1. The definition, symbol by symbol

The notes set up the same three things they always set up: a probability space (Ω, F, P), a random variable X on it, and then the new object, written FX(·) with a capital F.

FX(x) = P(X ≤ x) FX is the name of the function, capital F for cumulative and a subscript X saying which random variable it belongs to. x is a real number you choose, the threshold. X ≤ x is an event, and unpacking the shorthand of Part 2 it is the set of outcomes {ω : X(ω) ≤ x}. P prices that event. Read the whole line as "the probability that the value lands at or below the threshold".

Note the direction. The CDF always looks leftwards and downwards from the threshold, and it always includes the threshold itself. Both of those are choices, and both are load-bearing: the inclusion is why the staircase is right continuous in Part 5, and the direction is why Part 6 has to subtract to get anything else.

2. For a discrete variable it is a running sum

When the support is countable, the event X ≤ x breaks into disjoint pieces, one per point of the support that is at or below x. Adding disjoint probabilities is the axiom from Lecture 2, Part 5, so the notes write

FX(x) = P(X ≤ x) = ∑ pX(i), over i in SX with i ≤ x SX is the support, the set of values X actually takes, defined in Part 3. i runs over the members of that set. The condition i ≤ x keeps only the ones at or below the threshold. pX(i) is the mass at each of those, and the sigma adds them. Points off the support contribute nothing, which is why the sum can be written over the support rather than over the whole line.

So the recipe is: pick a threshold, walk left along the support collecting every mass you meet, and report the total. Nothing more.

3. The two-toss example, worked at four thresholds

Take the same random variable as Part 3: two tosses, X counts the heads, and the masses are 1/4 at 0, 1/2 at 1 and 1/4 at 2. Evaluating the running sum at four thresholds gives the whole picture.

  1. At x = −0.5. No point of the support is at or below −0.5, so the sum is empty and FX(−0.5) = 0. The same holds at every threshold below 0.
  2. At x = 0. One point qualifies, the value 0 itself, because the definition includes the threshold. So FX(0) = 1/4. The same total holds all the way up to but not including 1.
  3. At x = 1.4. Two points qualify, 0 and 1. So FX(1.4) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4. The notes mark exactly this value on their vertical axis.
  4. At x = 2 and beyond. All three points qualify, so FX(2) = 1/4 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 1, and it stays at 1 for ever after, because there is no more mass to collect.

4. The staircase, drawn under the masses that built it

Do that sweep at every threshold at once and the answer is a curve. The notes draw it as a staircase with three levels, and here it is directly beneath the PMF it accumulates, on the same horizontal axis and the same vertical scale, so that the jumps and the masses can be compared with a ruler rather than an argument.

5. Reading the filled and hollow dots

The dots at the ends of each tread are not decoration, and the notes draw them deliberately. They say which of the two levels applies at the exact instant the staircase changes.

  • A filled dot means the value at that point is the level the dot sits on. At x = 1 the filled dot is on the upper tread, so FX(1) = 3/4, not 1/4.
  • A hollow dot means the tread approaches that point but does not include it. The tread at height 1/4 runs up to but not including x = 1.

That convention is forced by the definition. FX(1) = P(X ≤ 1) includes the case X = 1, which carries a mass of 1/2, so the total at exactly 1 is already 3/4. The jump has happened by the time you arrive. Part 5 gives this a name, right continuity, and proves it.

6. Why this is the description that lasts

The notes list three properties beside their drawing: FX is monotone, starts at 0, goes to 1, and can have jumps. Part 5 proves all of them. What is worth saying now is why this function is the one the lecture keeps.

7. Check yourself

For the two-toss head count, what is the value of the CDF at exactly x = 1?
The definition is P(X at most x), so the threshold itself counts, and the masses at 0 and 1 both fall in, giving one quarter plus one half. One quarter would be the answer to P(X strictly below 1), which is a different question and the subject of Part 6. One half is the mass at the point rather than a running total, so it is the PMF's answer and not the CDF's. And one is the total over the whole support, which is not reached until the threshold gets to 2.
Why does the staircase stay flat between the values 1 and 2?
Moving the threshold from 1.1 to 1.9 sweeps past no point of the support, so the running total collects nothing new and the value cannot change. The CDF is defined on the whole real line, exactly like the PMF, which is why there is a curve to draw between the support points at all. No rule limits how fast a probability may change, and the jumps in the same figure change it instantly. And the mass at 1 was collected the moment the threshold reached 1, which is what the jump at 1 is.
Your teacher. If you have ever written a cumulative sum over a sorted array, you have written this function. The PMF is the array of values and the CDF is the prefix-sum array, and the jump at each index is the original value at that index, recoverable by subtracting neighbours. That is not an analogy, it is the same computation, and Part 6 is essentially the list of things a prefix-sum array lets you answer in constant time.
