"Not a sigma-field" should be a demonstration, never a verdict

foundation 14 min Tutorial problems 1 and 2. Three candidate collections, two closure proofs, and the smallest counterexample you can build.

The one-minute version

  • A sigma-field is a collection of events closed under complements and countable unions, containing the whole sample space.
  • Of the three candidates in problem 1, only F3 survives, and each failure has a named witness set.
  • The intersection of two sigma-fields is always a sigma-field; the union need not be.
  • The union counterexample is worth memorising: two fields on {1,2,3}, whose union loses the set {1,2}.

Problem 1, restated

The sample space is Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}, the set of four possible outcomes. Three collections of subsets are proposed, and the question asks which of them is a sigma-field of subsets of Ω, justified by checking the sigma-field axioms:

F1 = { ∅, {1,2}, {3,4} } Read as: the first candidate is a collection of three sets - the empty set ∅ (no outcomes at all), the event {1,2} (outcome 1 or 2 occurred), and the event {3,4}.
F2 = { ∅, Ω, {1}, {2,3,4}, {1,2}, {3,4} }     F3 = { ∅, Ω, {1}, {2}, {1,2}, {3,4}, {2,3,4}, {1,3,4} } The second candidate holds six sets and does contain the whole space Ω. The third holds eight sets. Both look respectable at a glance, which is exactly why the sheet chose them.

Which theorem is in play, and why

A collection F of subsets of Ω is a sigma-field when three things hold: the whole space Ω belongs to F; whenever a set A belongs to F, its complement Ac (everything in Ω outside A) also belongs to F; and the union of any countable list of members again belongs to F. Because Ω here is finite, the third condition quietly weakens to finite unions: any endless list of subsets of a four-point space repeats itself, so its union is really a finite union. The tutorial notes say exactly this, and it halves the work. What the condition does not become is optional, and that is where the trap of this problem lives.

Auditing F1: it cannot even see the whole space

  1. Does F1 contain Ω? Scan the three members: ∅, {1,2}, {3,4}. None of them is {1,2,3,4}. Fail.
  2. See the same failure through the complement axiom. The empty set ∅ is a member, and its complement is ∅c = Ω, which is not. One member with a missing complement is enough, so the complement axiom fails as well.
  3. Check unions anyway, to see how deep the damage goes. Take {1,2} and {3,4}, both members. Their union is {1,2} ∪ {3,4} = {1,2,3,4} = Ω, which is not a member. The union axiom fails with the same witness.
  4. Conclude. F1 is not a sigma-field. Every road leads back to one root cause: the collection forgot the full space.

Auditing F2: the convenient axiom passes, and that is the trap

  1. Does F2 contain Ω? Yes, explicitly.
  2. Check every complement. The six members pair off perfectly: ∅ ↔ Ω, {1} ↔ {2,3,4}, and {1,2} ↔ {3,4}. Every member's complement is again a member. Pass.
  3. Stop here and you get the wrong answer. This is the moment the sheet was built around. Everything checked so far is green, and a reader in a hurry concludes. One more axiom remains.
  4. Test unions, hunting for a witness. Try {1} ∪ {3,4}. Both are members. Their union is {1,3,4}. Scan the six members: ∅, Ω, {1}, {2,3,4}, {1,2}, {3,4}. The set {1,3,4} is not among them. Fail, with witness {1} ∪ {3,4} = {1,3,4}.
  5. Conclude. F2 is not closed under unions, so it is not a sigma-field, despite passing the complement check completely.

Auditing F3: read its structure before grinding

The eight members of F3 are not arbitrary. Split Ω into the three blocks {1}, {2}, {3,4} - a partition, meaning non-empty pieces that are disjoint (no shared outcomes) and together cover Ω. Every member of F3 is a union of whole blocks, and all eight possible unions of the 23 = 8 subfamilies of blocks appear:

Where this goes wrongThe failing cell of F2 is the whole problem. Its complements close perfectly, so checking complements alone produces a confident wrong yes. The sheet picked F2 precisely so that a partial audit succeeds. The fix is mechanical, not clever: write the three axioms down as rows, and fill every cell with either a tick or an explicit witness set before saying anything. Problems 3 and 12 re-run this exact trap with measures and independence.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

On a finite sample space, a candidate collection passes the complement check completely. What remains before it can be called a sigma-field?
Unions are exactly what F2 failed, which is why that check can never be skipped. Singletons need not be present: F3 omits {3} and {4} yet qualifies. Ordering members is cosmetic and proves nothing. Disjointness is not required either - F3 holds overlapping-free blocks but the power set of any space is a sigma-field full of overlapping sets.

Problem 2, restated

Let F1 and F2 be two sigma-fields of subsets of the same sample space Ω. Part (i) asks you to prove that their intersection F1 ∩ F2 - the collection of sets belonging to both fields - is always itself a sigma-field. Part (ii) asks whether their union F1 ∪ F2 - the collection belonging to at least one - is necessarily a sigma-field, justified by a counterexample if not.

F1 ∩ F2 = { A : A belongs to F1 and A belongs to F2 } Read as: the intersection collects exactly those events A that both fields agree on. The whole proof runs on that double membership.

Part (i): intersection, proved axiom by axiom

The theorem in play is the definition of a sigma-field itself, applied twice: once to each parent. Nothing else is needed, because every property of the intersection is inherited from the two parents through the double membership above.

  1. Whole space. Ω belongs to F1 because F1 is a sigma-field, and to F2 for the same reason. Belonging to both, Ω belongs to the intersection.
  2. Complements. Take A in the intersection. Then A belongs to F1, so Ac belongs to F1; and A belongs to F2, so Ac belongs to F2. Again both at once, so Ac is in the intersection.
  3. Countable unions. Take members A1, A2, ... of the intersection. Each An sits in both fields. Since F1 is closed under countable unions, the union ∪n An sits in F1; likewise in F2. So the union is in the intersection.
  4. Conclude. All three axioms hold, so F1 ∩ F2 is always a sigma-field. Notice the pattern, because it is reusable: membership in an intersection is double membership, and every inheritance argument goes through both channels.

Part (ii): union, and the smallest counterexample

Here the governing object flips: there is no theorem making unions of sigma-fields safe, so the honest move is to hunt for the smallest concrete failure rather than argue abstractly. Shrink the space until nothing can hide.

  1. Take the smallest interesting space, Ω = {1, 2, 3}, and the two smallest sigma-fields beyond the trivial one: F1 = {∅, Ω, {1}, {2,3}} and F2 = {∅, Ω, {2}, {1,3}}.
  2. Verify both parents really are sigma-fields. Each holds four sets forming two complement pairs (∅ with Ω; the singleton with its two-element complement), and every union of members lands back inside: {1} ∪ {2,3} = Ω closes F1, {2} ∪ {1,3} = Ω closes F2. Pass.
  3. Form the union. F1 ∪ F2 = {∅, Ω, {1}, {2}, {2,3}, {1,3}} - six sets.
  4. Break it. {1} and {2} both belong to the union, one through each parent. Their union {1} ∪ {2} = {1,2} belongs to neither parent, so it is missing from F1 ∪ F2. Not closed under finite unions, therefore not a sigma-field.
One claim above goes beyond the tutorial notes, and it is flagged rather than smuggled in: the notes give the three-point counterexample but never say it is the smallest one. The minimality argument is this page's own work, and it is short. A two-point space carries only two sigma-fields, the trivial one {∅, Ω} and the full power set, and the union of any two of those is again one of them - so nothing can leak until the space has at least three points. Check it if you distrust it; that instinct is the habit the session is building.
Where this goes wrongTwo classic slips. First, proving the intersection case by taking a set in F1 alone: the argument dies immediately, because the hypothesis gives you membership in both fields and you must use both. Second, answering part (ii) with words like "not necessarily" and no example - the question demands the counterexample, and the one above is the smallest one there is.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the counterexample, why does {1,2} fail to belong to F1 ∪ F2?
Membership in a union needs just one parent holding the set whole: F1 offers {1} and {2,3}, F2 offers {2} and {1,3}, and nobody offers {1,2}. Assembling it from one piece of each parent is exactly what closure forbids. Union closures fail only sometimes, which is why the question says need not. And {3} being absent from the parents is true but beside the point: the union fails on {1,2} directly.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1 - a flipped version of F1foundation

On Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}, consider the collection G = { ∅, Ω, {1,2}, {3,4} }. Problem 1 rejected F1 = {∅, {1,2}, {3,4}}. This candidate keeps the same two working sets but adds back the two that were missing. Is G a sigma-field?

Hint

Pair the members off as complements, then test every union you can form from four sets.

Show the worked solution
  1. Complements. ∅c = Ω and Ωc = ∅; {1,2}c = {3,4} and {3,4}c = {1,2}. Every member's complement is a member.
  2. Unions. Any union involving ∅ changes nothing. {1,2} ∪ {3,4} = Ω, a member. Every other pair is one of these. Closed.
  3. Intersections come free once complements and unions close, since A ∩ B = (Ac ∪ Bc)c.
  4. Conclude. G is a sigma-field: it is all unions of the partition {{1,2}, {3,4}}, exactly the F3 pattern of problem 1 with two blocks.

Sanity check. A partition into 2 blocks yields 22 = 4 unions, and G has exactly 4 sets - the counting agrees, unlike F1, whose 3 members could never be a union-closed family here.

Practice 2 - build your own leaking unionworking

Construct two sigma-fields on Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4} whose union is not a sigma-field, and name the exact set that goes missing.

Hint

Copy the three-point counterexample and let the fourth point ride along inside a block.

Show the worked solution
  1. Choose the fields. F1 = {∅, Ω, {1}, {2,3,4}} and F2 = {∅, Ω, {2}, {1,3,4}}. Each is all unions of a partition - {{1}, {2,3,4}} and {{2}, {1,3,4}} - so each is genuinely a sigma-field.
  2. Union them. F1 ∪ F2 = {∅, Ω, {1}, {2}, {2,3,4}, {1,3,4}}, six sets again.
  3. Find the leak. {1} and {2} are members, one from each parent. Their union {1,2} is a member of neither parent, hence missing from the union.
  4. Conclude. The union is not closed under unions, so it is not a sigma-field, and the witness set is {1,2}.

Sanity check. The leaked set is always the union of one distinguished piece from each parent - here the singletons - because that is the only way a set can be assembled from members without being one itself.

Your teacher. If the F2 failure still feels unfair, ask yourself which axiom could have caught it earliest - then notice the answer is "none individually": only the discipline of finishing the checklist catches it. That discipline, not set theory, is the content of this problem.
