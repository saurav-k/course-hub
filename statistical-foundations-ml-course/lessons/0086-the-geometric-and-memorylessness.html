Lecture 6 · Part 9 of 14 · the geometric

A hundred tosses with no head tell you nothing about how long the wait still has to run

frontier 13 min The first endless support in this lecture, and the one property the notes prove for a named shape.

The one-minute version

  • The geometric distribution counts tosses until a head appears. Its support is the whole numbers with no largest value, so it is the first countably infinite support in this lecture.
  • Its mass is pX(k) = p(1 − p)k−1: a run of k − 1 failures, then one success.
  • Its tail is remarkably simple: P(X > s) = (1 − p)s, which is just "the first s tosses were all tails".
  • Memorylessness is the property P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s). The notes prove it in full and gloss it in the margin: having seen no head in a hundred tosses, the chance of at least ten more is the chance of more than ten tosses from a fresh start.
  • The notes then assert, without proof, that the geometric is the only discrete shape with this property, and name queueing theory as where it is used.

1. The definition, and a wording that does not match it

The notes write the heading "Geometric dist", then a description in quotation marks, then the support, then the mass function.

pX(k) = p(1 − p)k−1 p is the parameter, the probability that any single toss comes up heads, the same on every toss and independent of the others. k is the value being priced. (1 − p)k−1 prices k − 1 tosses all coming up tails, by independence. p in front prices the next one coming up heads. So the whole expression prices the sequence "k minus one tails, then a head".

Read the formula and the description side by side and they do not quite agree, and this is worth stopping on rather than sliding past.

The words say "before" and the formula says "including"The notes describe the geometric as the number of coin tosses before first heads. Under that reading the smallest value is 0, since the first toss can be a head with no tosses before it. But the formula gives pX(1) = p(1 − p)0 = p, which prices a head on toss one as the value 1, not 0, and at k = 0 the formula would read p(1 − p)−1, which is bigger than 1 whenever p is and is not a probability at all. The formula pins the reading: X is the toss on which the first head lands, counting from one. This page follows the formula and leaves the sentence as the notes wrote it. It is a loose phrase in a set of pre-class notes, not a mathematical error, and repairing the words silently would hide the fact that the two readings differ by exactly one.

Under the reading the formula forces, the support is {1, 2, 3, ...}. The notes write it as N, and Part 10 shows that this same letter has to mean a different set one paragraph later.

This course's instanceThe notes state the geometric symbolically and draw nothing. p = 0.3 is this course's own choice so the shape can be seen, and the heights are this course's own evaluation of the notes' own formula: 0.3, 0.21, 0.147, 0.1029, 0.072, 0.0504 and onward, each 0.7 times its predecessor. The twelve drawn bars account for 0.9862 of the total, and the remaining 0.0138 is spread over the infinitely many values past twelve. None of this is the lecturer's.

2. The tail, which is the only thing the proof needs

Before the property, work out the tail probability, because everything afterwards is a ratio of two of them. The notes write these sums and this course fills in the one substitution they leave implicit.

  1. Write out the tail. P(X > s) means the first head lands strictly after toss s, so it collects the masses at s + 1, s + 2, ..., giving ∑k = s+1∞ p(1 − p)k−1.
  2. Reindex. Put ℓ = k − 1. As k runs from s + 1 upwards, ℓ runs from s upwards, and the sum becomes ∑ℓ = s∞ p(1 − p)ℓ. This is the form the notes write, and they do not show the substitution. Read their sums as being over the exponent rather than over the value.
  3. Factor out the common part. Every term carries at least (1 − p)s, so pull it out: (1 − p)s ∑m = 0∞ p(1 − p)m. The notes write exactly this factorisation at the bottom of the page.
  4. Recognise the leftover sum. What is left is every mass of the whole distribution added up, so it is 1. That is the PMF total from Part 3, and no separate geometric-series formula is needed.
  5. Read the answer. P(X > s) = (1 − p)s.

That answer has a one-line reading that makes it obvious in hindsight. "The first head lands after toss s" is the same event as "the first s tosses were all tails", and by independence that has probability (1 − p)s. The sum was never really necessary; it is the check that the formula agrees with the story.

3. Memorylessness, stated

P(X > s + t | X > t) = P(X > s) t is how long you have already waited with no success. s is how much longer you are asking about. The left-hand side is a conditional probability: given that the wait has already exceeded t, how likely is it to exceed s + t in total, which is to say to run at least s longer. The right-hand side is the same question asked from a standing start. The equation says the two are equal.

The notes gloss it in the margin with a concrete case, and it is the sentence to hold on to: you have not seen a head in a hundred tosses, and you ask how likely it is to take at least ten more. The answer is exactly the probability of needing more than ten tosses from scratch. The hundred tosses have bought you nothing.

4. Memorylessness, proved

The notes prove it in five steps and every one of them is on the page here.

  1. Expand the conditional. By the definition from Lecture 3, Part 4, the left side is P(X > s+t and X > t) / P(X > t).
  2. Collapse the intersection. Since s is not negative, any X above s + t is automatically above t, so the event X > s+t is contained in X > t and their intersection is just X > s+t. The numerator becomes P(X > s+t).
  3. Put in the sums. The notes write the ratio out as ∑j = s+t∞ p(1 − p)j over ∑ℓ = t∞ p(1 − p)ℓ, both in the reindexed form of step 2 above.
  4. Shift the top sum. With r = j − s, the numerator becomes ∑r = t∞ p(1 − p)r+s, which is (1 − p)s times the denominator, term by term. The notes write this substitution explicitly.
  5. Cancel. The two sums divide out and the left side is (1 − p)s. Section 2 already showed the right side is (1 − p)s as well, so the two are equal and the proof closes.

5. What memorylessness looks like

The property has a picture, and the picture is more convincing than the algebra. Take the distribution above, wait four tosses with no head, and draw what is left. Rescaled so it adds to one again, it is the original shape exactly.

This course's arithmeticCheck one bar by hand. The mass at 5 is 0.3 × 0.74 = 0.07203 and the surviving event has probability 0.74 = 0.2401, so the conditional mass at 5 is 0.07203 / 0.2401 = 0.3, which is exactly the original mass at 1. The 0.74 cancels, which is the algebra of section 4 happening on one bar. Both figures, both panels and all of these numbers are this course's own.

6. What the notes assert and do not prove

Two claims are stated flat, with no argumentThe notes write that among discrete random variables, the geometric is the only one with the memorylessness property, and that it has applications in queueing theory. Neither is argued and neither is illustrated. This course teaches both as stated and adds nothing: no proof of uniqueness is given here, and no queueing example is constructed, because inventing one would be putting words in the lecturer's mouth. Note that the claim is worth its weight: it means that if a waiting time really is memoryless and really is counted in whole steps, the geometric is not one option among several but the only one. Part 12 meets the continuous counterpart of exactly the same statement, where the notes write "prove it" in the margin and move on.

7. Check yourself

A geometric variable has p = 0.3. What is the probability that the first head lands after toss 4?
The tail formula gives one minus p to the power s, and the story behind it is that the first four tosses were all tails, each with probability nought point seven. Raising p itself to the fourth prices four heads in a row, which is the opposite event. Multiplying probabilities by a count is never legal and here would exceed one at thirteen tosses. And one minus p is the tail at s equal to one only, so it answers a different question about a single toss.
Which step of the proof is the one that actually discards the waiting already done?
Once the intersection collapses, the numerator no longer mentions t as a separate condition at all, and everything after that is arithmetic confirming what has already happened. The final cancellation produces the answer but it is bookkeeping on an expression whose structure was already fixed. The reindexing is a change of variable that alters no probability. And the series adding to one is a check on the total mass, which would hold whether or not the shape were memoryless.
Your teacher. Memorylessness is genuinely counterintuitive, and the reason it feels wrong is that most waits in life are not memoryless. A bus that is ten minutes late is more likely to arrive soon, because buses have schedules; a machine running for ten years is more likely to fail soon, because parts wear. A coin has neither a schedule nor wear, and that is the whole difference. When you meet a waiting time in practice, the useful question is not whether it is geometric but whether the thing you are waiting on has any state at all. If it does, memorylessness is the wrong model and you will over-trust the wait you have already put in.
