Two product decisions come off one staircase, read at different points

working 10 min The same six masses as the previous part, now accumulated, with the board's own off-by-one carried rather than tidied away.

After this page

  • You can turn a product question about recommendation quality into one evaluation of the CDF.
  • You can write P(X > k) as 1 - F(k) without landing on the wrong k.
  • You can say why two quality questions about the same list are usually not complements.

Before you start: A recommendation list is a PMF, for the six masses this page accumulates.

The one-minute version

  • The masses stay exactly as they were. What changes is that you add them up from the left, which is the CDF.
  • "How many users find something in the top three" is F(3) = 0.30 + 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.70. One evaluation, one answer.
  • "How many are left unsatisfied" is P(X > 4) = 1 - F(4) = 1 - 0.80 = 0.20. The board first wrote 1 - F(3), a student caught it, and it was fixed live.
  • 0.70 and 0.20 do not add to 1, and they are not meant to: they are two different quality bars, with position 4 belonging to neither.
  • A stricter team would move the bar again and read F(2) = 0.55 instead. The bar is a product decision; the CDF just answers it.

1. The running total, drawn

The cumulative distribution function collects mass from the left. At a threshold it reports the probability that X came in at or below it, which for a discrete variable is a sum of masses.

FX(k) = P(X ≤ k) = ∑i=1k pX(i) Read as: X is the position of the user's click, a whole number from 1 to 6. k is the threshold you are asking about, the last position that still counts. FX(k), the cumulative distribution function of X at k, is the probability that the click landed at position k or anywhere above it in the list. pX(i) is the mass at position i, and the sigma adds those masses for every i from 1 up to k. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis give exactly this form in section 3.2 and note that for a discrete variable the result has a staircase shape.

2. Question one: relevance in the top three

A team's first instinct is a quality of service number: if users click near the top, the ranking is working. The lecturer picks the top three as the bar and asks what fraction of users find a relevant link there.

  1. Turn the question into an event. "Found something in the top three" is the event X ≤ 3.
  2. Recognise it as a CDF value. P(X ≤ 3) is FX(3) by definition, so no new machinery is needed.
  3. Sum the masses up to three. 0.30 + 0.25 = 0.55, then 0.55 + 0.15 = 0.70.
  4. Read it back in the team's words. 70% of users find a relevant answer within the first three choices they are shown.

3. Question two: the users left unsatisfied

The second question mirrors the first. Some users click position five, so the top four were all wrong, and some press "show more", so all five were. Both groups are evidence against the ranking, and the team wants their combined share.

Two routes reach it and they agree. The direct route adds the two masses at the far end. The complement route notices that "not in the top four" is the negation of "in the top four", which is one subtraction.

P(X > k) = 1 - P(X ≤ k) = 1 - FX(k) Read as: k is the quality bar, the last position that still counts as a success. P(X > k) is the probability the click landed strictly past that bar. FX(k) is the probability it landed at the bar or above it. The two events cover every possible click and share none, so their probabilities add to one, and each is one minus the other. The NIST e-Handbook names 1 - F(x) the survival function and defines it as exactly Pr[X > x].
  1. Add the two far masses directly. P(X = 5) + P(X = 6) = 0.05 + 0.15 = 0.20.
  2. Now take the complement route. The negation of X > 4 is X ≤ 4, so P(X > 4) = 1 - FX(4).
  3. Evaluate F(4). 0.30 + 0.25 + 0.15 + 0.10 = 0.80, which the staircase above shows as the fourth tread.
  4. Subtract. 1 - 0.80 = 0.20, the same answer the direct route gave.
  5. Read it back in the team's words. One user in five finds nothing useful in the first four links and has to reach the last one or ask for more.
Honesty note: the board's off-by-one, caught liveThe first version written on the board was P(X > 4) = 1 - FX(3). A student interrupted with "is that not less than or equal to four?" and the lecturer answered correct, yeah, I made an error here, at least some people are awake, then rewrote it as 1 - FX(4). The class notes carry the corrected form only, which is why this page names the slip: it is the single most likely error a reader will repeat. The rule that fixes it is mechanical. The complement of X > k is X ≤ k, with the same k on both sides. Writing 1 - F(3) would answer P(X > 3) = 0.30, which is a different and larger group of users.

4. Move the bar and watch both numbers move

5. Two bars, not one bar and its complement

A student pushed back on this. If P(X ≤ 3) is the good outcome, should the bad outcome not be its complement, P(X > 3)? The lecturer answered that it could be, and that it was not what he had asked.

He had defined a bad recommendation as one that sends the user to the last link or to "show more", which is X > 4. That is a product choice, not a fact about the distribution: we are just looking at two different events which are not complement of each other.

A stricter team moves the bar rather than the arithmetic. The lecturer offered exactly that: a team wanting 90% of clicks in the first two positions reads F(2) = 0.55. Same masses, same curve, harder bar, worse verdict.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is P(X > 4) equal to 1 - F(4) rather than 1 - F(3)?
A complement keeps the same threshold and only flips the direction, so "past four" pairs with "four or below", which is F(4) = 0.80 and gives 0.20. The first option is simply false: F(3) excludes positions four, five and six, not the sixth alone. The second states a rule that does not exist, and applying it would give 0.30, the answer to a different question. The fourth confuses two thresholds: F(4) minus F(3) is the mass at position four, 0.10, not at position five.
A stricter team wants most clicks in the top two. Which number do they read?
Moving the bar to position two means asking for P(X ≤ 2), which is F(2) = 0.30 + 0.25 = 0.55, and it says the ranking is nowhere near a 90% target. F(3) answers the lecturer's original question and uses a looser bar than the team asked for. The mass at position two alone answers "how many click exactly the second link", which is not a cumulative question at all. And 1 - F(4) is a real number about this list, but it is the unsatisfied share under the second bar, so it neither rises nor falls when the team tightens the first one.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A second surface ships the same six-position layout, and its measured click masses are below.

0.20, 0.20, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.15 at positions 1 to 6 Read as: Y is the click position on this second surface, a whole number from 1 to 6. The six numbers are pY(1) through pY(6) in order, so the click lands at position 1 with probability 0.20, at position 2 with probability 0.20, and so on to position 6 with probability 0.15.

Find FY(3) and P(Y > 4). Then say which surface a team should ship if its stated bar is "at least 70% of clicks in the top three", and whether that verdict would change under the bar "at most 15% of users left unsatisfied".

Hint

Do the sum-to-one check first: a wrong table makes every later number wrong in a way that is hard to see. Then note that the two bars are different questions, so nothing forces the two verdicts to agree, and the interesting part of this problem is the case where they do not.

Show the worked solution
  1. Check the table. 0.20 + 0.20 + 0.20 = 0.60, plus 0.15 is 0.75, plus 0.10 is 0.85, plus 0.15 is 1.00. Legal PMF.
  2. Accumulate to three. FY(3) = 0.20 + 0.20 + 0.20 = 0.60.
  3. Accumulate to four. FY(4) = 0.60 + 0.15 = 0.75.
  4. Take the complement at the same threshold. P(Y > 4) = 1 - 0.75 = 0.25. Checking directly: 0.10 + 0.15 = 0.25.
  5. Apply the first bar. The page's surface gives 0.70 and clears "at least 70% in the top three"; this one gives 0.60 and fails it. Ship the page's surface.
  6. Apply the second bar. Unsatisfied shares are 0.20 and 0.25, so both fail "at most 15% left unsatisfied", and the page's surface is still the better of the two.
  7. State the verdict. Both bars pick the same surface here, but only the first bar is actually met, so the honest report is "better, and still not good enough".

Sanity check. FY(3) = 0.60 must sit between FY(2) = 0.40 and FY(4) = 0.75, and it does, because a CDF never decreases. FY(3) + P(Y > 4) = 0.60 + 0.25 = 0.85, which is below 1 rather than equal to it, exactly as on this page: position 4 with its 0.15 is counted by neither.

Carry this away

  • A product question about ranking quality becomes one evaluation of the CDF, and the only real decision is where to put the bar.
  • F(3) = 0.70 and 1 - F(4) = 0.20 are the lecture's two answers, and the second one is the complement of F at the same threshold, never at the one below.
  • Two questions asked at two different bars leave a middle group counted by neither, which is why their answers need not add to one.
  • A stricter bar is a business decision applied to an unchanged distribution: F(2) = 0.55 is the same list judged harder.

Your teacher. If the off-by-one still feels slippery, do not memorise a rule. Ask an agent to tabulate all six values of F(k) for this list beside all six values of 1 - F(k), then point at the row where the two questions on this page live and say out loud which k each one uses. The pattern you want to end up with is physical rather than symbolic: a bar sits between two positions, everything above it is F, everything below it is 1 - F, and both readings use the same bar. Every off-by-one in this material is somebody quietly using two different bars in one line.
