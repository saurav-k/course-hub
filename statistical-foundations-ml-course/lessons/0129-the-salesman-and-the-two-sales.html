Two independent variables become one by drawing every pair and then adding the cells that agree

working 13 min Problem 4.13 from the course's own textbook, worked on the slow Sunday sitting. The first problem in this course where two random variables have to be combined.

After this page

  • You can build the mass function of a single appointment when a sale splits two ways.
  • You can lay two independent variables on a grid, fill it by multiplication, and collapse it onto a sum.
  • You can check the result by summing the cells and by summing the recovered mass function.

Before you start: Part 1, for what a mass function is and what it must total.

The one-minute version

  • Two appointments. The first sells with probability 0.3, the second with 0.6, independently. Any sale is deluxe at $1000 or standard at $500, equally likely.
  • Each appointment is therefore a three-valued variable: 0, 500 or 1000. The equally likely clause splits each sale probability in half.
  • First appointment: 0.7, 0.15, 0.15. Second: 0.4, 0.3, 0.3.
  • X is the total, so its support is 0, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, and its mass comes from a three by three grid of independent products.
  • Collapse the grid onto the totals: 0.28, 0.27, 0.315, 0.09, 0.045.

1. The question, restated in full

A salesman has scheduled two appointments to sell encyclopedias. His first appointment will lead to a sale with probability 0.3, and his second will lead independently to a sale with probability 0.6. Any sale made is equally likely to be either for the deluxe model, which costs $1000, or the standard model, which costs $500. Determine the probability mass function of X, the total dollar value of all sales. The source is A First Course in Probability, Problem 4.13, and it was worked on the slow Sunday sitting.

Two clauses carry all the work. Independently licenses multiplying across the grid. Equally likely means each sale probability is halved between the two models, and forgetting that halving is the only real trap in the problem.

2. Each appointment on its own

P(S1 = 500) = (1/2) × P(a sale at appointment 1) S1 is the money taken at the first appointment, a random variable with three possible values: 0, 500 or 1000. S2 is the same for the second appointment. The factor 1/2 is the equally likely split between deluxe and standard, applied only once a sale has happened. So a sale probability is shared between two price points rather than assigned to either.
  1. No sale at appointment 1. P(S1 = 0) = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7.
  2. Standard at appointment 1. A sale, then the standard model: (1/2)(0.3) = 0.15.
  3. Deluxe at appointment 1. By the same split, 0.15.
  4. Appointment 2, all three. P(S2 = 0) = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4, and each of the two models gets (1/2)(0.6) = 0.3.
  5. Check each one. 0.7 + 0.15 + 0.15 = 1 and 0.4 + 0.3 + 0.3 = 1, so each appointment is a proper mass function before anything is combined.

3. The grid, and collapsing it

X is S1 + S2, so its support is every reachable total: 0, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000. Note that 1000 is reachable two ways, from a deluxe alone or from two standards, which is why the grid is needed rather than a list.

Every cell is the product of its row and column probability, which is exactly what independence licenses. The tutor wrote the totals grid first and the probability grid second, and the order matters: the totals tell you which cells will have to be added together.

  1. X = 0. One cell only, the top left: 0.28.
  2. X = 500. Two cells, 0.21 + 0.06 = 0.27.
  3. X = 1000. Three cells, 0.21 + 0.045 + 0.06 = 0.315. This is the value the grid earns its place on.
  4. X = 1500. Two cells, 0.045 + 0.045 = 0.09.
  5. X = 2000. One cell, 0.045.
  6. Check. 0.28 + 0.27 + 0.315 + 0.09 + 0.045 = 1 exactly.
The support was listed before any of it was justifiedThe notes write the five values 0, 500, 1000, 1500 and 2000 with the bracketed remark "different ways of adding values of S1 and S2", and then compute. That is the right list, but the phrase hides the fact that 1000 arises from three different pairs while 2000 arises from one, and that unevenness is the whole shape of the answer. This page draws the grid before the list for that reason.

The problem is 4.13 of Ross, A First Course in Probability, which sets it immediately after the mass function is defined and before any named distribution is available.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is P(the first appointment yields 500 dollars) equal to 0.15 and not 0.3?
A sale happens with probability 0.3 and is then equally likely to be either model, so each model collects half of it. The first option invents an interaction between two appointments the question calls independent. The second gives a fact about price with no bearing on probability. The third is loosely true but names no mechanism, and would equally describe the deluxe outcome.
Why does the total of 1000 dollars carry more mass than any other total here?
A deluxe from either appointment alone and two standards together all give 1000, so three cells contribute, more than any other total gets. The first option describes the position and not the cause. The second is false: the two models are equally likely. The fourth is true but would push mass toward 500 and 1000 without explaining the peak.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Same salesman, but the second appointment is cancelled and replaced by a third whose sale probability is 0.5, again splitting equally between the two models.

Find the mass function of the new total, then compare its value at 1000 with the page's 0.315 and explain the difference.

Hint

Only the second column changes: the new appointment gives 0 with 0.5 and each model with 0.25. The first appointment is untouched.

Show the worked solution
  1. New second variable. P(0) = 0.5, P(500) = P(1000) = 0.25, and those sum to 1.
  2. X = 0. One cell: 0.7 × 0.5 = 0.35.
  3. X = 500. (0.7)(0.25) + (0.15)(0.5) = 0.175 + 0.075 = 0.25.
  4. X = 1000. (0.7)(0.25) + (0.15)(0.25) + (0.15)(0.5) = 0.175 + 0.0375 + 0.075 = 0.2875.
  5. X = 1500. (0.15)(0.25) + (0.15)(0.25) = 0.075.
  6. X = 2000. (0.15)(0.25) = 0.0375.
  7. Explain. 0.2875 is below 0.315 because the weaker second appointment moves mass toward the low totals; the whole distribution has shifted left rather than changed shape.

Sanity check. The five masses add to 0.35 + 0.25 + 0.2875 + 0.075 + 0.0375 = 1 exactly. The mass at zero must also have risen, since the second appointment now fails more often, and 0.35 beats the page's 0.28. The mass at 2000 must have fallen for the same reason, and 0.0375 is below 0.045.

Carry this away

  • A sale that splits equally between two prices halves its probability across them, once the sale has happened.
  • Two independent variables go on a grid, and every cell is the product of its row and column.
  • Draw the totals grid first, because it shows which cells will have to be added together.
  • Cells sharing a total lie on one anti-diagonal, and the longest one carries the peak.

Your teacher. Draw the totals grid on paper before you compute a single probability. Nine cells take fifteen seconds and they turn the hard part of the problem, which is knowing what to add to what, into something you can point at. The same grid is how a sum of two independent variables is handled for the rest of this subject, and it scales: with more values the cells grow but the anti-diagonal rule does not change.

Where this came from

Problem 4.13 of A First Course in Probability, worked on the slow Sunday sitting from the sheet's own page headed with that book title. The notes define S1 and S2 as the takings at the two appointments, A as the deluxe model and B as the standard, list each variable's three values, then give P(S1 = 0) = 0.7, P(S1 = 500) = (1/2)P(sale) = 0.15 and the same for the deluxe with the annotation "since A and B are equally likely", then 0.4, 0.3, 0.3 for the second appointment. They carry the three by three totals table with the caption that it shows which combination of the two gives each value of X, the statement that independence licenses P(S1 = 500 ∩ S2 = 500) = 0.045 and so on for all combinations, the second table of the nine cell probabilities, and then the mass function assembled twice, once as sums of cells and once as the five numbers 0.28, 0.27, 0.315, 0.09 and 0.045. The grid drawing, the anti-diagonal reading, the totals check and the practice variant are this course's own.
