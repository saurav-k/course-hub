TA Session 3 · Part 3 of 10 · Problem 1

Rule out the impossible ranks first, and the rest of the problem is one sliding product

working 12 min Problem 1 on the sheet, from Ross, Chapter 4, end-of-chapter Problem 1, p. 142. The question asks for ten values and only six of them exist.

After this page

  • You can build the PMF of a "first success in an ordered list" variable by a sliding product of sampling probabilities.
  • You can rule an impossible value out by an argument about the population rather than by grinding to a zero.

Before you start: The support and the PMF, for what it means to give every value its mass.

The one-minute version

  • Ten people, five men and five women, ranked with no ties. X is the best rank any woman achieves.
  • The question asks for P(X = i) at i = 1 through 10, and X can never exceed 6: only five men exist, so at worst they take ranks 1 to 5 and a woman is sixth.
  • Each surviving value is one pattern: some men, then the first woman. P(X = i) is the chance of drawing i minus 1 men in a row and then a woman.
  • The six answers are 1/2, 5/18, 5/36, 5/84, 5/252 and 1/252.
  • The check is free: those six must add to exactly 1, and they do.

1. The question, restated in full

Five men and five women are ranked according to their scores on an examination. No two scores are alike, and all 10! possible rankings are equally likely. Let X denote the highest ranking achieved by a woman, where rank 1 is the best. The sheet's own worked instance: X equals 2 if the top-ranked person was male and the next-ranked person was female. Find P(X = i) for i = 1, 2, 3, …, 10.

Two readings of the sentence caused trouble in the room and are worth settling before any arithmetic. The bracketed instance is an example, not an assumption: the question does not say the first rank is male. And "highest ranking" means the best rank, the smallest number, not the largest.

2. Ruling out before computing

The tutor's first move was not to compute anything. He asked whether P(X = 10) could be anything other than zero, and let the room reason it out.

For X to be 10, ranks 1 through 9 would all have to be men. There are only five men. So P(X = 10) = 0, and the same argument kills 9, 8 and 7. The worst case the population allows is all five men taking ranks 1 to 5, which puts the first woman at rank 6, so X cannot exceed 6.

His stated reason for doing this first is the one to take away: the mechanical route gets to P(X = 7) and stalls, because the product needs a sixth man to place and there is none left. You would eventually multiply by zero and get the right answer, and you would have spent the time being confused about what to multiply by.

3. The sliding product

For X to equal i, the first i − 1 ranks must all be men and rank i must be a woman. What happens after rank i is irrelevant, which is what makes this one product rather than a count over 10! orderings.

P(X = i) = (5/10) × (4/9) × … × (women left / people left) i is the rank being asked about. Each factor is the chance that the next rank goes the way the pattern needs, given everything already placed. The numerator counts the people of the required kind still unplaced, and the denominator counts everyone still unplaced. The final factor is always the woman, so its numerator is 5, the number of women, which never decreases before the first woman appears.
  1. X = 1. The first rank is a woman. Five women out of ten people: 5/10 = 1/2.
  2. X = 2. First a man, then a woman. The man is 5/10; then nine people remain, four men and all five women, so the woman is 5/9. The product is 25/90 = 5/18, about 0.278.
  3. X = 3. Man, man, woman: (5/10)(4/9)(5/8) = 100/720 = 5/36, about 0.139.
  4. X = 4. (5/10)(4/9)(3/8)(5/7) = 300/5040 = 5/84, about 0.0595.
  5. X = 5. (5/10)(4/9)(3/8)(2/7)(5/6) = 600/30240 = 5/252, about 0.0198.
  6. X = 6. All five men first, then a woman with certainty: (5×4×3×2×1)/(10×9×8×7×6) × 1 = 120/30240 = 1/252, about 0.00397.

The last factor at X = 6 is exactly 1, and that is worth pausing on. Once all five men have taken the first five ranks, everyone left is a woman, so rank 6 is a woman with no uncertainty at all. The pattern that has been sliding down the list finally runs out of men and terminates itself.

Two routes were shown and only one was recommendedThe fast sitting worked this problem by computing each value in turn, and reached the same six numbers. The slow sitting ruled out 7 through 10 before computing anything, and the tutor said explicitly why: the mechanical route arrives at P(X = 7) with no man left to place, and you get stuck asking what to multiply by rather than seeing that the answer is zero. That is the route this page takes. The other route reaches the same answers and simply costs you the confusion.

The problem is Chapter 4 of Ross, A First Course in Probability in its companion volume, and the sequential-product route is the one that text uses for ordered-sampling questions of this shape.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the last factor in P(X = 6) equal to one rather than five ninths or similar?
After five men have taken ranks one to five, every one of the five remaining people is a woman, so rank six is female with certainty. The first option confuses the range of the question with the state of the pool. The second is true but does not give a probability of one on its own. The fourth invents a conditioning step that the sliding product never performs.
Suppose there were seven men and three women instead. What changes about the support?
The largest X can be is one more than the number of men, so seven men push the top of the support to eight. The first option gets the direction backwards: more men means women can be pushed further down. The third treats the support as fixed by the head count rather than by the split. The fourth would need nine men to be possible.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Six candidates are interviewed in a random order: two are internal and four are external. Let Y be the position of the first internal candidate.

Give the support of Y, then the full PMF, and verify it sums to 1.

Hint

Y equals k means the first k minus 1 are external and the kth is internal. Four external candidates cap how far the first internal one can be pushed.

Show the worked solution
  1. Rule out first. At worst all four external candidates go before both internal ones, putting the first internal one fifth. So the support is 1 through 5, and P(Y = 6) = 0.
  2. Y = 1. Two internal out of six: 2/6 = 1/3.
  3. Y = 2. (4/6)(2/5) = 8/30 = 4/15.
  4. Y = 3. (4/6)(3/5)(2/4) = 24/120 = 1/5.
  5. Y = 4. (4/6)(3/5)(2/4)(2/3) = 48/360 = 2/15.
  6. Y = 5. All four external first, then an internal with certainty: (4/6)(3/5)(2/4)(1/3) × 1 = 24/360 = 1/15.

Sanity check. Put everything over fifteen: 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15, so the five masses sum to exactly 1. The masses must also fall as k grows, since each extra external candidate is another factor below one, and they do.

Carry this away

  • Ask what the population makes impossible before you compute anything: it costs one sentence and saves a stall.
  • A first-success-in-a-list variable gives one prefix pattern per value, so one product settles each value.
  • Each factor is what is left of the required kind over what is left in total, and the final factor may be exactly 1.
  • The six masses add to 1, which is a free check on a page of fractions.

Your teacher. Write the pattern before the fractions. For each value of X put down MMF or MMMF as a string, count its letters, and only then attach the shrinking fractions one letter at a time. Almost every error here is a mismatch between the number of men in the pattern and the number of factors in the product, and a written string makes that mismatch visible instead of arithmetic.
