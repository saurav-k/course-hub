Lecture 8 · Part 6 of 6 · the balls-and-bins example

Count with indicators, and the dependence you were about to worry about does not matter

working 11 min The notes drop m balls into n bins, ask for the expected number of empty bins, and reach the answer in three lines. Then they box a question in the margin and leave it.

After this page

  • You can turn "how many of these things happen" into a sum of indicators and read its mean straight off linearity.
  • You can show that two empty-bin indicators are not independent, and say which way the dependence runs.
  • You can say exactly why that dependence leaves the answer untouched, and name the property that would have broken.

Before you start: LOTUS and linearity, for the addition rule this whole page spends.

The one-minute version

  • Drop m balls into n bins, each ball landing in a bin picked uniformly at random. How many bins end up empty?
  • Counting the arrangements is painful. Instead define one indicator per bin: Xi = 1 if bin i is empty, 0 otherwise.
  • An indicator's mean is just the probability it is 1, and a bin stays empty only if every ball misses it: E[Xi] = ((n − 1)/n)m.
  • The count of empty bins is S = X1 + … + Xn, so linearity gives E[S] = n((n − 1)/n)m. Three lines, no arrangements counted.
  • The indicators are not independent. Linearity never asked whether they were, so the answer stands exactly as written.

1. The question, and why the direct route is unpleasant

There are m balls and n bins. Each ball is dropped into a bin chosen uniformly at random, independently of the other balls. Let S be the number of bins that end up with nothing in them. Find E[S].

The direct route asks: what is the probability that exactly k bins are empty? That needs an inclusion-exclusion argument over which bins are empty, and it produces a sum you would then have to weight by k and add up. The answer would be correct and nobody would enjoy getting it.

2. The trick: one indicator per bin

An indicator is a random variable that is 1 when some event happens and 0 when it does not. It is the smallest useful random variable there is, and it has one property that makes it worth defining.

Xi = 1 if bin i is empty, 0 otherwise  ⇒  E[Xi] = 1 · P(bin i empty) + 0 · P(bin i not empty) = P(bin i empty) i is which bin we are looking at, running from 1 to n. Xi is that bin's indicator, taking only the values 1 and 0. E[Xi] is its expected value, the weighted average of its two values against their probabilities. Because one value is 1 and the other is 0, the average collapses to a single probability: the mean of an indicator is the probability of the event it indicates. That sentence is the entire trick.

Now stack them. The number of empty bins is the number of indicators that came up 1, which is their sum.

S = Σi=1n Xi S is the count of empty bins, the quantity the question asks about. The capital sigma means "add up", and the range under and over it says to add the indicators for bins 1 through n. Since each term contributes 1 exactly when its bin is empty and 0 otherwise, the total is the number of empty bins. The count and the sum are the same random variable written two ways.

3. The three lines

  1. Find the probability one named bin stays empty. Bin i survives ball 1 only if that ball lands in one of the other n − 1 bins, which happens with probability (n − 1)/n. The balls are dropped independently of each other, so the m misses multiply.
  2. Write the indicator's mean. By the collapse above, E[Xi] = P(bin i empty) = ((n − 1)/n)m. Note the shape: as m grows the base sits below 1, so the power shrinks, and a bin gets harder to leave empty as more balls fall.
  3. Take the expectation of the sum. E[S] = E[Σ Xi] = Σ E[Xi]. This is the step linearity licenses, and it is the only place in the derivation where anything could have gone wrong.
  4. Add n identical terms. Every bin looks the same to the balls, so every E[Xi] is the same number, and the sum of n copies of it is E[S] = n((n − 1)/n)m.

Notice what never happened. No arrangement was counted, no distribution of S was derived, and the joint behaviour of the bins was never written down. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis and Hajek both take this route; Hajek's Section 2.2 derives the addition rule from the joint pmf and states E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] with no independence condition attached, and Section 4.5 leans on that same line before it goes anywhere near a joint distribution.

4. The boxed question: are the indicators independent?

Page 7 of the notes draws a box in the margin beside this calculation and writes Are Xi, Xj ind? The question is never answered. It is a good question, and worth answering, because the honest answer is no and the calculation survives anyway.

Take the smallest case that settles it: two bins and one ball. Exactly one bin gets the ball, so exactly one bin is empty. If bin 1 is empty then bin 2 is certainly not, and the two indicators can never both be 1.

  1. Write the joint probability in general. Bins i and j are both empty exactly when every one of the m balls lands in one of the other n − 2 bins, so P(Xi = 1, Xj = 1) = ((n − 2)/n)m.
  2. Write what independence would demand. Independence would need that joint probability to equal the product ((n − 1)/n)m × ((n − 1)/n)m = ((n − 1)2/n2)m.
  3. Compare the two bases. Independence would need (n − 2)/n to equal (n − 1)2/n2. Multiplying out, the left is (n2 − 2n)/n2 and the right is (n2 − 2n + 1)/n2. They differ by 1/n2, for every n.
  4. Read the direction. The true joint base is the smaller one, so for m ≥ 1 the joint probability sits below the product. Two bins are less likely to be empty together than independence predicts, which is exactly the crowding story: emptying bin i hands its share of the balls to everyone else.
  5. Put numbers on it. With n = 3 and m = 2 the joint is (1/3)2 = 1/9 = 0.1111 and the product is (2/3)4 = 16/81 = 0.1975. Not equal, so not independent.

5. Why the answer does not change

Step 3 of the derivation was the only load-bearing step, and it used linearity: the expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations. That rule was proved on the previous page by marginalising a joint pmf, and the proof never assumed the variables were independent. The notes write the point in the margin themselves: always true, doesn't need X independent of Y.

So dependence is allowed to be as strong as it likes. Even the two-bin case, where the indicators are perfectly opposed, gives E[S] = 2 × (1/2)1 = 1, which is right: with one ball, exactly one bin is empty, every time.

A full check by hand, three bins and two ballsThe formula gives E[S] = 3 × (2/3)2 = 12/9 = 1.3333. Now enumerate all 9 equally likely outcomes. In 3 of them both balls land in the same bin, leaving 2 empty bins. In the other 6 the balls land in different bins, leaving 1. The average is (3 × 2 + 6 × 1)/9 = 12/9 = 1.3333. Identical, and the enumeration made no independence assumption because none was available to make. This arithmetic is this course's own.
The notes box the question and never answer itPage 7 writes Are Xi, Xj ind? in a box beside the empty-bin line and moves straight on to the product rule. Section 4 above is this course's answer, not the lecturer's: they are not independent, and the dependence is negative. Two things follow, and only one of them is on this page. The mean of S is untouched, because linearity was the only tool used. The variance of S is not untouched: variance does not add across dependent variables, and computing Var(S) here would need every covariance term, each of them slightly negative. That calculation is nowhere in the source notes and is not attempted here. Treat the boxed question as answered for the mean and open for the spread.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is E[S] = n((n − 1)/n)m correct even though the bin indicators are dependent?
Linearity of expectation was proved by marginalising a joint pmf, so it holds for any variables on the same probability space, dependent or not, and that is the only step the derivation leans on. The second option is false: the covariance between two empty-bin indicators is negative, not zero. The third is true and is why n identical terms can be added, but it is a shortcut in the arithmetic and not the reason the sum rule applies. The fourth describes the model that produces the probability ((n − 1)/n)m; it says nothing about why expectations may be added.
With two bins and one ball, how does P(both bins empty) compare with the product of the two indicator means?
The single ball must land somewhere, so one bin is always occupied and both being empty is impossible: the joint probability is 0, while each mean is 0.5 and their product is 0.25. The second option has the two values swapped, and a product of two halves cannot be zero. The third quotes 0.50, which is the chance that one named bin is empty, not the chance that both are. The fourth would make the joint equal the product, which is precisely the independence this example fails.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Ten requests are hashed into ten shards, each request landing on a shard uniformly at random and independently of the others.

Find the expected number of idle shards. Then show that the indicators for shard 1 and shard 2 are not independent, and say which way the dependence runs.

Hint

This is n = 10, m = 10. For the second half, write the probability that two named shards are both idle directly, then compare it against the product of two single-shard probabilities. You will need 0.9 to the tenth power, about 0.3487, and 0.8 to the tenth, about 0.1074.

Show the worked solution
  1. One shard's indicator. Shard i is idle only if all ten requests miss it, so E[Xi] = (9/10)10 = 0.3487.
  2. Sum the ten of them. By linearity, E[S] = 10 × 0.3487 = 3.4868 idle shards. So about 3.5 of the ten shards see no traffic at all, and about 6.5 carry everything.
  3. Write the joint. Shards 1 and 2 are both idle only if all ten requests land in the other eight, so P(X1 = 1, X2 = 1) = (8/10)10 = 0.1074.
  4. Write the product. Independence would require 0.3487 × 0.3487 = 0.1216.
  5. Compare. 0.1074 is not 0.1216, so the two indicators are not independent. The covariance is 0.1074 − 0.1216 = −0.0142.
  6. Say which way. The joint sits below the product, so the dependence is negative: knowing shard 1 is idle crowds all ten requests into the remaining nine shards and makes shard 2 less likely to be idle.

Sanity check. The expected idle count must lie between 0 and 10, and 3.4868 does. It must also fall as m rises: at m = 5 the formula gives 10 × 0.95 = 5.90, comfortably above 3.49. And the covariance must be negative here rather than positive, because emptying one shard can only add load elsewhere. All figures on this page are this course's own arithmetic.

Carry this away

  • The mean of an indicator is the probability of the event it indicates. That single collapse is what makes the trick work.
  • Any "how many of these happen" question becomes a sum of indicators, and its mean is the sum of the individual probabilities.
  • The empty-bin indicators are not independent, and the dependence is negative: emptying one bin crowds the others.
  • Linearity does not care, so E[S] = n((n − 1)/n)m stands. The variance of S would care, and is not computed anywhere here.

Then take the indicator idea somewhere else: ask an agent to compute the expected number of fixed points in a random permutation of n items by defining an indicator for "item i stays put", and notice that those indicators are dependent too and the answer is still exactly 1 for every n.
