One exponent decides whether the Pareto has a mean at all

working 11 min The previous page had a mean that was undefined. This one has a mean that is genuinely infinite, and the notes name the reason in a single sentence about how the tail decays.

After this page

  • You can run the Pareto expectation integral and read off the two cases the antiderivative produces.
  • You can say why a polynomial tail permits an infinite mean while an exponential tail never can.
  • You can spot the engineering measurements where this matters, and say what a sample average is doing there.

Before you start: The mean that does not exist, for the difference between an undefined mean and an infinite one.

The one-minute version

  • The Pareto has two parameters: a floor x0 below which nothing happens, and a shape α.
  • Its tail is a power of x, P(X > x) = (x/x0)−α, not an exponential of x.
  • The expectation integral leaves x1−α to evaluate at infinity. That is the whole decision.
  • For α > 1 it vanishes and E[X] = αx0/(α − 1). For α ≤ 1 it runs away and the mean is infinite.
  • The notes' own conclusion: this happens because the tail decays polynomially, not exponentially.

1. The shape, before the algebra

A Pareto variable never goes below a floor, and above that floor its chance of being large falls off like a power of x. That is the entire picture. The notes give the tail first and the density second, which is the right order: the tail is what makes the distribution interesting.

P(X > x) = (x / x0)−α for x ≥ x0, and 1 for x < x0 X is the random quantity. x0 is the floor, the smallest value X can take, and it is strictly positive. α is the shape or tail exponent, also strictly positive; a large α means the tail dies quickly and a small α means it lingers. The left side reads "the probability that X exceeds x". Below the floor that probability is 1, because X is certain to exceed anything smaller than its own minimum.
fX(x) = α x0α x−(α+1) for x ≥ x0, and 0 otherwise fX is the density, the thing you integrate to get a probability. The notes write the leading constant as α / x0−α, which is the same number written with the negative exponent in the denominator. The exponent on x is one larger than the exponent in the tail, which is what differentiating the tail does to it.

2. The integral, worked

Expectation for a continuous variable is the value weighted by the density, integrated over everything the variable can be. Here "everything" starts at the floor.

  1. Write the definition on this support. E[X] = ∫x0∞ x · α x0α x−(α+1) dx. Nothing below the floor contributes, because the density is zero there.
  2. Collect the powers of x. Multiplying x by x−(α+1) adds the exponents: 1 − (α + 1) = −α. The integral becomes α x0α ∫x0∞ x−α dx.
  3. Antidifferentiate. The power rule raises the exponent by one and divides by the new exponent, giving x1−α / (1 − α). This step needs α ≠ 1, or the divisor is zero.
  4. Set up the evaluation. E[X] = α x0α [ x1−α / (1 − α) ]x0∞. Everything now depends on one question: what does x1−α do as x grows without bound?
  5. Case α > 1. Then 1 − α is negative, so x1−α is one over a growing power and goes to zero. Only the lower limit survives, with a minus sign: − x01−α / (1 − α) = x01−α / (α − 1).
  6. Simplify that case. Multiply by the constant out front: α x0α · x01−α / (α − 1). The two powers of x0 add to α + 1 − α = 1, leaving E[X] = α x0 / (α − 1).
  7. Case α < 1. Now 1 − α is positive, so x1−α grows without bound and the upper limit is infinite. The mean is infinite.
  8. Case α = 1. The power rule does not apply, and the integral of 1/x is ln x, which also grows without bound. Infinite again, by a different route.
E[X] = α x0 / (α − 1) for α > 1, and ∞ for α ≤ 1 E[X] is the mean, the long-run average of X. x0 is the floor and α the tail exponent. Read the finite case as "the mean is the floor, multiplied by a factor that depends only on the shape". That factor α/(α − 1) is close to 1 for a large α and blows up as α approaches 1 from above.

3. Why polynomial decay is what allows this

The notes end page 4 with one written sentence: the expectation is infinite for a small exponent because tail probabilities do not decay exponentially, only polynomially. That sentence is the transferable idea, and it is worth unpacking once.

An expectation is a sum of value times weight. Large values carry a lot of weight if they are not rare enough. The exponential tail e−μx shrinks faster than any power of x grows, so multiplying it by x still leaves something that integrates. A power-law tail is itself only a power of x, so multiplying by x can leave a power that does not integrate. Whether it does is exactly the comparison in step 4 above.

The notes bracket the antiderivative for alpha not equal to one, then answer for alpha at most onePage 4 writes the evaluated bracket [x1−α/(1 − α)] with the condition α ≠ 1 attached, and the very next line states the conclusion as infinite for α ≤ 1, which includes the value just excluded. The gap is real but small, and it is closed by a separate integral rather than by the same one: at α = 1 the integrand is 1/x, whose antiderivative is ln x, and ln x also grows without bound. Step 8 above is this course's, not the lecture's. The stated conclusion is correct; the working printed beneath it covers every case except the boundary.

4. Where an engineer meets this

This example is the course's own, not the lecture's. Object sizes in a content store are a standard heavy-tailed measurement: almost every object is small, a few are enormous, and the largest one you have seen keeps being beaten. Take a floor of 4 KB, the smallest stored object, and a fitted exponent of 1.2.

This course's arithmetic on the lecture's formulaWith x0 = 4 KB and α = 1.2, the mean is αx0/(α − 1) = (1.2 × 4) / 0.2 = 24 KB, six times the floor. Re-fit the same data next quarter and get α = 0.9 instead, and the same formula returns nothing at all: the mean is infinite. The measured average object size in your dashboard did not become infinite. It became meaningless, in the precise sense that it will not settle as you collect more objects, and it will jump every time a single very large one arrives.

That is the operational content of an infinite mean. A capacity plan built on "average object size times object count" is not conservative-but-rough under an exponent at or below one; it is a number with no limit to converge to. The same warning applies to request latencies with a long tail and to loss amounts in a risk model. The repair is not a bigger sample. It is to plan against a quantile, such as the 99th percentile, which stays finite for every positive exponent.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the Pareto mean infinite when the exponent α is at most 1?
Multiplying x by the density leaves x−α, whose integral to infinity diverges once α is at most 1. The first option is false: the density integrates to exactly one for every positive α, which is why the distribution exists at all. The third is false: a tail probability is a probability and never goes negative. The fourth is false: the floor x0 is part of the definition and does not depend on α.
Why can no exponential tail ever produce an infinite mean?
Whatever power of x the value contributes, the exponential factor eventually overwhelms it, so the product always integrates. The second option is false: the exponential runs to infinity just as the Pareto does. The third is invented, and any positive rate gives a finite mean of one over the rate. The fourth states two true facts that have nothing to do with whether an integral converges.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Object sizes in a store are modelled as Pareto with a floor of 4 KB.

Find the mean size at α = 2.5 and at α = 1.1, then state the mean at α = 0.8, and say what changes about a capacity plan between the three.

Hint

Everything comes from one factor, α/(α − 1), applied to the floor. Check which side of 1 each exponent falls on before you divide.

Show the worked solution
  1. Check the case for α = 2.5. It is above 1, so the finite formula applies.
  2. Evaluate it. α/(α − 1) = 2.5/1.5 = 1.667, and times the 4 KB floor that is 6.67 KB.
  3. Check the case for α = 1.1. Also above 1, so the same formula applies.
  4. Evaluate it. 1.1/0.1 = 11, and times 4 KB that is 44 KB. Dropping the exponent by 1.4 multiplied the mean by more than six.
  5. Check the case for α = 0.8. It is below 1, so the integral diverges and the mean is infinite. There is no number to report.
  6. Say what changes. At 2.5 an average-based capacity plan is sound. At 1.1 it is finite but fragile, because a small error in the fitted exponent moves the answer a long way. At 0.8 the plan has no basis at all and must be written against a quantile instead.

Sanity check. Every finite answer must exceed the 4 KB floor, since a mean cannot sit below the minimum possible value. The factor must also fall as α rises, and 1.667 against 11 does. If your two finite answers came out in the other order, you divided by α rather than by α − 1.

Carry this away

  • The Pareto tail is a power of x, and the density is that power with the exponent raised by one.
  • The expectation integral collapses to evaluating x1−α at infinity, and that single limit decides everything.
  • Above one the mean is the floor times α/(α − 1); at one and below it is infinite.
  • Polynomial decay is what makes an infinite mean possible, because an exponential tail beats every power of x.

Your teacher. If the two-case split feels arbitrary, ignore the constants for a moment and integrate x−0.5, then x−1, then x−2 from 1 to infinity by hand. Only the third converges, and you will see the boundary sitting exactly at the exponent 1 rather than being placed there by the Pareto. Then ask an agent to draw the running average of a few thousand samples at α = 0.8 and at α = 2.5: one line settles and the other keeps jumping upward, which is what an infinite mean looks like in data rather than in an integral.
