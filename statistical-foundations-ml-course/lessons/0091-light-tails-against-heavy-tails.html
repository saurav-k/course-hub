Lecture 6 · Part 14 of 14 · the closing remark

Two curves that look almost identical in the middle and differ by a factor of a million in the tail

11 min the last page Four lines in the notes, and the one comparison that makes them mean something.

The one-minute version

  • The notes close by saying that everything above is an example of a light tailed distribution.
  • A heavy tailed distribution, they say, has significant mass away from the mean. That is the whole of the definition given.
  • The example offered is the Cauchy distribution, with density fX(x) = 1/(π(1 + x2)) on the whole real line.
  • Side by side with a standard normal the two look similar near the centre. At four units out the Cauchy density is about 140 times the normal's, and at six units out about a million times.
  • The difference in shape is one line of algebra: the normal decays like e to minus a square, and the Cauchy decays like one over a square. Everything else follows from that.

1. What the notes say, and what they leave open

The source gives this section four handwritten lines. Everything above is light tailed. Heavy tailed distributions have significant mass away from the mean. For example the Cauchy distribution, whose density is written out. That is all.

fX(x) = 1 / (π(1 + x2)), for x in R x2 makes the density symmetric about zero, exactly as the squared gap did for the Gaussian in Part 13. 1 + x2 is never zero, so the density is defined and strictly positive everywhere. Dividing by it makes the density fall away as x grows. π is the constant that makes the total area one, doing the same job that the leading lambda did for the exponential and that one over sigma root two pi does for the Gaussian. The support is the whole real line, and this version of the shape carries no parameters at all.
The definition given is informal, and one of its words is not yet definedTwo things are worth naming rather than glossing over. First, "significant mass away from the mean" is a description rather than a criterion: it does not say how much mass, or how far away, and the notes offer no formal threshold. This course does not supply one, because supplying one would be choosing between definitions the source did not choose between. Second, the phrase leans on the word mean, which this lecture has not defined: expectation is the subject of the next lecture, and whether every distribution on this page even has one is a question that belongs there rather than here. Everything on this page is therefore a direct comparison of densities and of tail probabilities, which needs no mean at all.

2. The two curves on one pair of axes

The notes draw nothing here and compare the Cauchy to nothing. Comparing it to a standard normal is the fastest way to make the phrase concrete, so that is what this section does, and both curves come from formulas the notes themselves state.

4. Why the two behave so differently

The whole difference is visible in one comparison of the two formulas, and it needs no calculation.

  1. The normal's tail. Its density carries exp(−x2/2). As x grows, the exponent grows like a square and the exponential of a large negative number collapses extremely quickly.
  2. The Cauchy's tail. Its density is 1/(π(1 + x2)). As x grows this behaves like 1/(πx2), so doubling x divides the density by about four and nothing more.
  3. Compare the two rates. Dividing by four each time you double is slow. Exponential collapse is not slow. That gap is what widens from a factor of 1.2 at two units out to a factor of over a million at six.
  4. Name the pattern. A tail that dies exponentially is light. A tail that dies like a power of x is not, and the notes' phrase "significant mass away from the mean" is a description of the second case.

5. What this lecture leaves you holding

This is the last page, so here is the whole of Lecture 6 in one list.

  • A random variable is a function from the sample space to the real line, and nothing about it is random. Part 2.
  • A PMF says how much weight sits on each point of a countable support. Part 3.
  • A CDF says how much weight has accumulated, works for every random variable, and obeys four properties proved once and for all. Parts 4, 5 and 6.
  • Five named discrete shapes, from the one-switch Bernoulli to the endless Poisson. Parts 7 to 10.
  • A density replaces mass when the support becomes an interval, area replaces height, and every single point weighs nothing. Part 11.
  • Four named continuous shapes, ending with the Gaussian and its uncomputable-by-formula CDF. Parts 12 and 13.
  • And the reminder that all of them except the last are light tailed, so none of them prepares you for a world where the far values keep arriving.

What is missing is any notion of a typical value or a spread. Every shape on these pages has been described entirely by where its weight sits, and never by a single summary number. That is the next lecture.

6. Check yourself

Why does the first figure make the Cauchy look like the milder of the two shapes?
At the scale of the peak both tails are pressed against the axis, so the one difference the eye can pick up is the peak height, where the Cauchy is the lower of the two. It is not milder: it puts more mass far out, which is the entire point of the section. The curves do cross, but the crossing is not what makes the picture misleading; the loss of resolution in the tails is. And the first figure is drawn at true scale, with the magnification applied only in the second.
What structural feature of the Cauchy density makes its tail heavy?
Doubling x divides a one over x squared tail by about four, which is slow, while the normal's exponent grows like a square and collapses the density instead. The lower peak is a consequence of the heavy tail rather than its cause, since more mass far out leaves less in the middle. Carrying no parameters is a fact about this version of the shape and has nothing to do with decay. And the normal is also symmetric and also defined everywhere, so neither feature can be what separates them.
Your teacher. The engineering reading of this page is worth stating plainly. If you have ever sized a system by assuming that requests take about the average time, plus or minus a bit, you assumed a light tail. If the real latency distribution is heavy tailed, the occasional request that takes a hundred times the median is not an incident, it is the distribution behaving normally, and no amount of retrying will make it stop happening. That is the difference the second figure is showing, and it is why the last four lines of a set of pre-class notes are worth a whole page.
