Every page so far was handed a model, and a student asked where it came from

working 11 min The longest answer of the delivered session, given to a question the pre-class notes never raise. It has no counterpart in the notes and no page before this one.

After this page

  • You can say where the numbers in a probability model come from when nobody hands you a distribution.
  • You can fit one parameter to a month of counts and say what the fit assumed before it fitted anything.
  • You can name the check that tells you whether the family you chose matches the data you have.

Before you start: The Poisson and the shape of n, which is the family fitted here.

The one-minute version

  • Every example in this lecture starts by assuming a probability measure: the coin is fair, the die is fair, the emails open with probability 0.1. A student pointed out that the reason you run an experiment is usually to find out whether the coin is fair, so where does the assumed model come from?
  • The lecturer took the question seriously and called it a question about probabilistic modelling rather than about this lecture. The answer is a loop: history builds a model, the model answers questions history cannot.
  • The example he chose is one no coin can carry: a lending decision. Whether a borrower will default is a question about an event that has never happened to them, so no amount of staring at their history answers it directly.
  • The concrete version is a Poisson fitted to website traffic. One parameter, estimated as an average over thirty days, then a check of whether the fitted shape matches what was actually seen. The check has a name: the chi-squared test.
  • And the honest closing: a named family is a first guess. If it does not fit, you try another, or you use the empirical shape itself.

1. The question, as it was asked

Near the end of the session a student put it plainly. You explained the CDF and the PMF with a fair die and a fair coin. But the reason anyone performs the experiment is to find out whether the die is fair. If it is not fair, the CDF changes and the PMF changes with it. So what are we doing?

The lecturer agreed with every part of that and then reframed it. This is not a question about cumulative distribution functions. It is a question about where a probabilistic model comes from at all, and he said so, calling it a philosophical question about how one does probabilistic modelling.

He also pointed back rather than starting from scratch: this is the cycle Lecture 1 opened with, where a probabilistic model and the data feed each other. Probability runs forward from a model to a prediction. Statistics runs backward from data to a model. Everything since Lecture 2 has run forward, with the model handed over at the start of each example.

Why the objection is a good one. If the only thing you ever wanted was the chance of a 3 on a die you already believe is fair, the whole apparatus of this lecture is overkill: the answer is one sixth and you knew it before you started. The apparatus earns its keep when the model is not obvious, when it has to be fitted, and when the question you need answered is not one the data can be asked directly. That last case is the one the lecturer went to next.

2. The answer he chose: a loan that has never defaulted

The example is worth following in his order, because the punchline is in the last step.

  1. The setting. You are deciding whether to extend a loan to a particular individual, or scoring them for fraud. You have their transaction history: everything they have done in the past.
  2. The question. What is the chance this person makes a transaction so large that they cannot repay it, or defaults on the loan?
  3. The obstruction. Look at the history and the individual has never made a large transaction. The event you are being asked to price has never occurred in the data. Its empirical frequency is zero, and zero is not the answer, because the answer being asked for is a risk rather than a count.
  4. The move. You do not read the probability off the history. You use the history to build a model, or to fit the parameters of one, and then you use the model to compute the chance of the event that never happened.
  5. Why that is legitimate. A model interpolates and extrapolates in a way a tally cannot. Once a family and its parameters are fixed, the family assigns a probability to every value in its support, including values you have not observed. That is exactly what the geometric did on Part 9: its support runs to infinity and it prices a wait of a thousand tosses without anyone ever having waited that long.

A student then offered a summary in the vocabulary of Lecture 4: you had a prior, you ran the experiment, and you updated the prior in the way Bayes' rule updates one. The lecturer half-accepted it - "sort of" - and restated it in his own terms, which are looser and cover more ground. Based on what your data is, you either fit a good model or you update the model you had, and then you use the model to compute the probabilities you actually wanted.

3. The concrete version: one parameter, fitted to a month

Asked a second question - how would you even know whether a Poisson is a good model? - the lecturer worked the whole loop on a real decision, and this is the part of the session with the most detail in it.

  1. The decision that forces the model. You are hosting a website and must choose a server or bandwidth configuration. It has to cope with however many users arrive. You do not know that number in advance, so you model it.
  2. The window. Fix a slot: the traffic arriving between nine and ten in the morning. Let X be how many users arrive in that hour.
  3. The family. Choose Poi(λ), because Part 10 is the canonical model for arrivals.
  4. The parameter. One number has to be chosen, and the lecturer chose it from history: collect the nine-to-ten count for thirty days, take the average, and use that as the estimate. He wrote it with a hat, λ̂, to mark it as an estimate from data rather than a known truth, and the model in use from then on is Poi(λ̂).
  5. Why the average. Because for a Poisson the average value is the parameter. The lecturer said this in passing and it is the one step on this page the course has not earned yet: expectation is not defined until Lecture 8, where the same fact is derived rather than asserted.
  6. The check. The fitted model states a probability for every count: no users, one user, two users, and so on. The data states a fraction for each of the same counts: on what proportion of the thirty days did no user arrive, one user, two users. Lay the two lists beside each other. If they match well, the model is a good fit. If they are very different, it is not.
  7. What the check is called. This is model fit, and the standard tool for it is the chi-squared test. The lecturer named it, said it is one of the standard tests, suggested reading about it, and said he did not know whether the course would reach hypothesis testing.
  8. What happens on a bad fit. Two options, both given. Switch to another family, or abandon the named families and use the empirical distribution itself as the model.
Three things here are named rather than taughtThe chi-squared test is named and not described, so this page names it and does not describe it either. The claim that a Poisson's average equals its parameter is used before this course defines an average, and Lecture 8 is where it is earned. And "if they match well" is doing real work with no definition of well anywhere in the session - that judgement is the whole content of a hypothesis test, and the lecturer said as much when he called the comparison a kind of error calculation. Nothing on this page proves that a fitted model is a good one; it only shows what the question looks like.

4. Which family, and the permission to use none of them

A separate student asked the neighbouring question: how do you know which distribution to use? The answer had two halves, and the second half is the one most courses leave out.

  1. Often the shape is obvious from the question. Something with two outcomes - clicking a link or not, answering the phone or not, being busy or not - is a Bernoulli. Repeat an experiment n times and count how many came out one way rather than the other, and it is a binomial. The lecturer called these choices "very natural" and said they come from practice rather than from theory.
  2. The named shapes are not a menu you must order from. This is the part worth carrying. You are not restricted to these random variables. You might have a situation that fits none of them, and it is still a perfectly good discrete random variable: it takes finitely many values, and it has its own mass function with whatever weights the situation carries.
  3. Why the named ones are named. The Poisson is canonical for traffic because people have looked at many real arrival streams and found the family fits them by varying one parameter. That is a fact about the world discovered by measurement, not a theorem. It makes the Poisson a good first thing to try, and nothing more.
  4. And you can combine them. Asked whether a situation could need two distributions at once, the lecturer said yes without hesitation: mixtures of distributions, and "all kinds of things", are available. He gave no example and this course adds none.

5. What this changes about the rest of the lecture

Nothing about the machinery, and quite a lot about how to read it. Every probability computed on Parts 1 to 14 was computed inside a model that was assumed. That assumption is not hidden and it is not a defect, but it is load-bearing, and the size of the gap between a model and the world is a separate subject with its own tools.

Two of those tools were named in this session and neither is developed: the chi-squared test for whether a fitted family matches its data, and the vaguer promise, made when a student pushed on the difference between a model and a hundred coin tosses, that there are ways to characterise the variation and that the course will get to them.

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why can a borrower's transaction history not directly give the probability of a default?
The default has never happened to this borrower, so its empirical frequency is exactly zero, and zero is not the risk anyone is asking about. Sample size is not the obstruction the lecturer named: even a very long history containing no default gives the same useless zero. Frequencies and probabilities are related perfectly well in general, and the whole fitting procedure depends on that relation holding. And dependence between transactions is a real modelling concern but it is not what makes the direct reading impossible here.
What did the lecturer say to do when a fitted Poisson does not match the observed counts?
He gave exactly two options: another family, or the empirical distribution used as the model itself. Collecting more data is not a repair for the wrong family, since a badly chosen shape stays badly chosen however much history you feed it. Adjusting the parameter is what fitting already did, and the check happens after that, so a poor fit means the family is wrong rather than the number. And stopping is not on the list at all; the empirical shape is always available as a fallback.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A service logged support tickets on thirty days: no tickets on 6 days, one on 11, two on 8, three on 4, and four on 1.

Fit a Poisson to those counts, then say what the fitted model gives for the chance of a day with five or more tickets, and name the assumption the fit made before it computed anything.

Hint

The Poisson has one parameter and the sample mean estimates it. Total the tickets, divide by the days, and only then reach for the mass function.

Show the worked solution
  1. Total the tickets. 0(6) + 1(11) + 2(8) + 3(4) + 4(1) = 0 + 11 + 16 + 12 + 4 = 43.
  2. Estimate the parameter. Thirty days carried 43 tickets, so lambda-hat = 43/30 = 1.4333 tickets per day.
  3. Write the model. P(X = k) = e to the minus 1.4333, times 1.4333 to the k, over k factorial.
  4. Work the first five masses. e to the minus 1.4333 = 0.2385, so the masses at 0 to 4 are 0.2385, 0.3418, 0.2450, 0.1170 and 0.0419.
  5. Total them and subtract. They sum to 0.9842, so P(X ≥ 5) = 1 − 0.9842 = 0.0158, about one day in sixty-three.
  6. Name the assumption. The fit chose the Poisson family before it chose lambda. Nothing in the thirty days established that family, and a day-of-week effect or a burst from one incident would break it.

Sanity check. The five masses must sit between 0 and 1 and total slightly under 1, and the fitted mean 1.4333 must land between the observed one-ticket and two-ticket days, which it does. If your P(X ≥ 5) comes out above the mass at 4, the subtraction went the wrong way.

Carry this away

  • A model is what lets you price an event your data has never shown, which is exactly why the loan that never defaulted still gets a number.
  • Fitting is two decisions, not one: the family first, then the parameter, and only the second one is arithmetic.
  • The check that the family fits is a separate step with its own test, and this session names it without developing it.
  • Using no family at all is allowed when the data is dense enough to speak for itself.

Next: Lecture 7, which spends these shapes on decisions somebody actually has to make.

Your teacher. If you build systems you have already met this loop and probably did not call it modelling. You size a queue from last quarter's traffic. You set a timeout from the observed latency spread. You pick a retry count from how often the call has failed before. Each of those is a fitted model being used to price an event that has not happened yet, and each of them silently assumes a family. The value of this page is not the chi-squared test, which you can look up. It is the habit of asking, out loud, which family you assumed and whether anyone ever checked it against the data you already had.
