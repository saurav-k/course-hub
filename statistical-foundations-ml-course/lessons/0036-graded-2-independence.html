Two tests with similar names, run in two different worlds

working 14 min Five marks, the biggest chunk on the sheet: a one-mark complement proof with a published template, then one small probability space interrogated twice, once per meaning of "independent".

The one-minute version

  • Part (a) asks you to show that if A and B are independent, then so are A and Bc, the event that B does not happen. The course has already published this exact proof - your job is to reproduce it, not cite it.
  • Part (b) builds a small uniform space of eight points and three events, then asks two questions that look alike and are not: are A and B conditionally independent given C, and are A and B independent?
  • These are two different tests in two different worlds. The plain test runs in the whole space; the conditional test runs inside C after C has been renormalised into a world of its own. A pair can pass either and fail the other.
  • This page names both tests and the traps around them. It computes neither.

Part (a), restated in full

Say A and B are two independent events in a probability space. Show that A and Bc - the complement event of B, the event that B does not happen - are also independent. [1 mark]

Every symbol, named: independent means the pair passes the product test, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B), the overlap's probability equalling what the two singles multiply to. Bc is the complement of B: every outcome of the space where B fails. The claim is that the pair A and Bc passes its own product test, P(A ∩ Bc) = P(A) × P(Bc), given that the original pair passed theirs.

What it is really testing

Whether you can run a two-line argument from the definition, and whether you know that independence is a property of the information between events, not of the particular yes-or-no wording of them. If knowing B tells you nothing about A, then knowing not-B tells you nothing about A either. The course proves exactly this claim, with exactly these two events, in Lecture 3, Independence, section 5, "Independence survives taking complements".

One warning about how to use that page, because the mark depends on it: the proof there is a template to follow, not a result to cite. Writing "by Lecture 3, complements preserve independence" earns nothing here. The sheet wants the argument in your own hand, at full length, from the product test to the conclusion. The good news is that the template is short and its moves are named.

How to start

Write down the product test you want to prove: the equation for the pair A and Bc. The only unfamiliar object on its left side is P(A ∩ Bc), so start there, and split A across B and not-B - the two pieces partition A, which is exactly what move 1 of the template does. Then stop. Assembling the three lines is the homework, and the one mark is riding on them.

Part (b), restated in full

Let Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}, and let F = 2Ω, the collection of every subset of Ω. Define the probability measure P by P(D) = |D| / 8 for every D ⊆ Ω. Define the events:

  • A = {1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8}
  • B = {1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8}
  • C = {1, 2, 3, 4}

Two questions, in the sheet's own order and numbering:

  1. Are A and B conditionally independent given C? Justify your answer. [2 marks]
  2. Are A and B independent? Justify your answer. [2 marks]

Every symbol, named: Ω is the sample space, the eight outcomes of the experiment. F = 2Ω says every subset of those eight points is an admissible event - there is no fine print about which sets are allowed. P(D) = |D| / 8 is the uniform measure: an event's probability is the number of points it contains, divided by eight, so each of the eight points carries exactly one eighth. A, B and C are three events, each naming some of the eight points. Given C is the conditioning phrase: the information that the outcome lies in C. Justify means show the two sides of whichever test applies, as numbers, and state the verdict.

The space itself is an old friend in a new costume: a small finite set, every subset admissible, probability by counting. Lecture 2, A discrete space worked carries exactly that shape of space end to end with real numbers, and it is worth a skim before you start if the setup reads as friction.

The two tests, named

Here is the point of the whole problem, stated plainly. Conditional independence given C is a different test from independence. It is not a variation, an approximation, or a corollary. It is a separate instrument, carried out inside C, after C has been shrunk into a world of its own and renormalised. The two tests, named:

Test 1 - plain independence, run in Ω: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B) Both sides are measured in the full space of eight points. The left side is the probability that the outcome sits in both A and B. The right side multiplies the two single probabilities. This is the test part (ii) asks about.
Test 2 - conditional independence, run inside C: P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C) × P(B | C) Every probability in sight is now a conditional one, measured inside the shrunken world C. P(A | C) is A's share of C, P(B | C) is B's share of C, and the left side is the share of C that lies in both traces. This is the test part (i) asks about.

Because the two tests measure different quantities in different worlds, a pair of events can pass one and fail the other, in either direction. That is not a paradox; it is the reason the sheet asks both questions about the same three sets, and it is why answering part (ii)'s test in part (i) - or the reverse - loses the marks even when every number in the working is correct.

What "renormalising" means, mechanically: told that the outcome lies in C, you keep only the points inside C and divide every probability by P(C), so that the shrunken world carries total mass one again. Ratios inside C are preserved; the ruler changes. Lecture 3, Conditional probability draws that shrinkage, and TA Session 1, Building the conditional space proves it is a genuine probability space from scratch - the division by P(C) is the whole mechanism.

The traps

  • The two sub-questions are not the same question twice. Part (i) wants test 2, run inside C with every probability conditional. Part (ii) wants test 1, run in Ω with no conditioning anywhere. Markers of this exact problem see the swap constantly.
  • "Justify" has a precise shape here. Each test is an equation between two numbers. A justification computes both numbers, compares them, and states the verdict. One side of the equation, or a verdict with no numbers under it, is half an answer on a two-mark question.
  • Nothing here is disjoint. A, B and C overlap heavily by construction. The reflex "little or no overlap means dependent, lots of overlap means independent" is exactly the instinct to switch off: the course proved in Independence that overlap says nothing either way, and the product test is the only judge.
  • C is a proper slice of Ω, not the whole space. The renormalisation is not cosmetic: dividing by P(C) genuinely changes the numbers, which is the entire reason the two tests can land in different places.
  • In part (i), every factor of the test is a conditional one. A left side of P(A ∩ B | C) against a right side mixing P(A) and P(B | C) is an equation about nothing. Keep the bar on every term, or none.

How to start

For each sub-part, write the test as an equation first, before touching the sets. For part (i): the equation is test 2, and every probability in it is a share of C. For part (ii): the equation is test 1, and every probability is a count over eight. Every number either test needs is a count of points - the measure is uniform, so probabilities are sizes over eight, and conditional probabilities are sizes of traces over C's size. Then stop. Which numbers come out equal and which do not is precisely the homework, and the four marks are riding on the comparison.

Check yourself

Which equation is the test for conditional independence of A and B given C?
The conditional test is the product test transplanted into C: the two traces' shared share of C must equal the product of their separate shares of C. The second option is test 1, the plain one, which is the answer to a different sub-question. The third is a statement about each event separately ignoring the news, which is neither test - it omits any comparison involving the pair. The fourth is an addition rule about unions, which prices a different event altogether.
A pair of events passes the plain independence test. Does that settle the conditional independence question given some event C?
The plain test prices everything in the full space; the conditional test prices everything inside C after renormalising, where the outside world is invisible. Different rulers, different numbers, no implication in either direction - which is exactly why this problem asks both questions about one triple of sets. The two escape hatches in the last two options are invented conditions that appear nowhere in either definition.
In part (a)'s template, what is the engine of the whole three-line proof?
The proof needs the one quantity the conclusion mentions, the overlap of A with not-B, and the split delivers it: A falls into two disjoint pieces, the part inside B and the part outside, so subtracting gives the outside piece from quantities the given test already prices. Bayes rule is a belief-updating tool this problem never calls for, the union bound is an inequality rather than an identity, and A and B being disjoint is false in general and would make the given independence impossible whenever both have positive probability.
Your teacher. The deepest trap on this page is finishing part (i), getting a verdict, and then letting part (ii) inherit it. Fight that with structure: write both tests down as equations first, on two separate lines, with the bar drawn on every conditional term. If the two lines end up looking identical, one of them has the wrong bar somewhere, because the two tests genuinely are different objects.
