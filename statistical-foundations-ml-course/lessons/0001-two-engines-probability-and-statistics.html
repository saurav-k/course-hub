There are only two engines, and they run in opposite directions

8 min no arithmetic on this page Probability goes model to data. Statistics goes data to model. Every question in this course is one of those two.

The one-minute version

  • Data science sits in the overlap of three fields: statistics, probability, and computation.
  • Probability is the forward engine. You are handed a model and asked what data it will produce. Written P(Data | Model).
  • Statistics is the backward engine. You are handed data and asked what model produced it. Written P(Model | Data).
  • Almost every confusion in an introductory course is running the wrong engine. Learn to tell them apart now and the rest of the lecture is easy.

1. The mental model: one overlap, two directions

Slide 3 makes a single claim, and it is worth sitting with before any notation arrives. Data science is not a fourth thing sitting beside statistics, probability, and computation. It is the region where all three are true at once. Take away the computation and you have a mathematics course. Take away the statistics and you have a simulation with nothing to check it against. Take away the probability and you have a spreadsheet that cannot say how confident it is.

2. Reading the notation before using it

Both engines are written with the same piece of notation, so name it once and it stops being an obstacle. The expression P(A | B) is read aloud as "the probability of A, given B". The vertical bar is not division and it is not "or". It means assuming B is already settled. Everything to the left of the bar is what you are uncertain about. Everything to the right of the bar is what you are treating as known.

That single reading is what makes the two engines obvious, because the two engines simply put different things on different sides of the bar.

  • P(Data | Model) puts the model on the known side. You are saying: assume the coin is fair, assume users convert at 30 percent, assume spend follows this curve. Now tell me what data to expect. This is probability, and the lecture calls it the forward engine.
  • P(Model | Data) puts the data on the known side. You are saying: here are ten days of transactions, here are a thousand clicks, here are five users. Now tell me what was generating them. This is statistics, and the lecture calls it the backward engine.

3. Telling them apart in practice

In a lecture the distinction feels obvious. In a meeting it does not, because business questions arrive with the model and the data tangled together. The reliable test takes two readings of the sentence. First, find what is being assumed. Second, find what is being asked. If the assumption is a model and the request is about data, you are running forward. If the assumption is data and the request is about a model, you are running backward.

Read the notation honestlyThe slide writes the backward engine as P(Model | Data), which is a slogan for the direction of reasoning rather than a promise that you can always compute a literal probability over models. Doing that properly needs Bayes' rule and a prior belief about the model, which is exactly what Lecture 2 sets up. Classical statistics often reaches the same destination by a different road, estimating a parameter and reporting how uncertain the estimate is, which is what Part 5 does with confidence intervals. Both are the backward engine. Neither needs you to worry about the difference yet.

4. Check yourself

A team assumes every visitor converts independently with probability 0.3, then asks how often 340 or more out of 1,000 visitors will convert. Which engine is running?
The conversion rate of 0.3 is handed over as an assumption, and the request is about counts that have not happened yet, so the model is on the known side of the bar and the data is on the unknown side. That is the forward engine. It is not statistics, because nothing is being inferred: the 0.3 was given, not estimated. It is not computation, because a simulation is one way to get the number, not the kind of reasoning being done. And it is not outside mathematics, because "how often" is precisely a probability.
Ten days of one user's transaction history sit on the table, and you are asked what that user's typical daily spend is. Which direction is the reasoning?
The ten days are the known side of the bar and "typical spend" is a property of the process that produced them, so you are reasoning from evidence to model. That is backward. It is not forward, because no model was supplied to reason out from. It is not sideways: relating two observed variables is correlation, which arrives in Part 7 and is a different question. And nothing here is circular, because the answer is not assumed anywhere in the setup.
Your teacher. If you take one habit from this page, take the two-reading test. Read the question once and underline what is assumed. Read it again and underline what is asked. It costs five seconds and it removes most of the confusion that this material generates in a first pass. Try it on the next email you get that contains a percentage.
