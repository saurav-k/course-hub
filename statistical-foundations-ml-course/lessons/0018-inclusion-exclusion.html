Every point must be counted exactly once, so the signs alternate

11 min working Two events needed one subtraction. Three events need three subtractions and one addition, and the pattern generalises.

The one-minute version

  • Part 1's addition rule subtracts the overlap of two events. With three events there are three pairwise overlaps, and subtracting all three strips the triple overlap out entirely.
  • So you add the triple overlap back. Add for odd levels, subtract for even: that is the whole pattern.
  • For n events: add n single terms, subtract C(n, 2) pair terms, add C(n, 3) triple terms, and keep alternating to the single n-way term.
  • The sign at each level is whatever makes every region of the Venn diagram count exactly once.

1. Three events, drawn first

The formula for three events is only readable once you can see the seven regions it is balancing. Here is the picture: three overlapping events, and every distinct region labelled in words. There are seven, because each event can be in or out independently, minus the one combination, none of the three, that sits outside all the circles.

2. The formula, built from the two-event rule

No new machinery is needed. Treat A ∪ B as one event, glue C to it with Part 1's addition rule, then expand both remaining unions the same way:

  1. Apply the addition rule to (A ∪ B) and C. P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A ∪ B) + P(C) − P((A ∪ B) ∩ C). The overlap of "A or B" with C is the event that C happens and at least one of A, B happens, which is (A ∩ C) ∪ (B ∩ C).
  2. Expand P(A ∪ B) itself. By Part 1 it is P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
  3. Expand the last overlap too. (A ∩ C) and (B ∩ C) are two events, so their union's probability is P(A ∩ C) + P(B ∩ C) − P(A ∩ B ∩ C). Their overlap is exactly the all-three region.
  4. Substitute and collect the terms. The singles come with a plus, the three pairs come with a minus, and the triple comes with a plus:
P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A ∩ B) − P(A ∩ C) − P(B ∩ C) + P(A ∩ B ∩ C) A, B and C are three events in the same sample space. P(A) reads "the probability of A"; P(A ∩ B) reads "the probability that A and B both happen". The signs alternate: plus at the singles level, minus at the pairs level, plus at the triple level.

3. Why these signs, checked region by region

The formula is not a convention; each sign is forced. Take any one of the seven regions and tally how often the formula counts it. A region lying in exactly one event: counted once by that single term, untouched by everything else. Net count, one. Correct. A region lying in exactly two events, say A and B: the singles level counts it twice, A and B, and the pairs level subtracts it once, for the pair A ∩ B. Net count, two minus one, which is one. Correct again.

The triple region runs the same tally across three levels. The singles count it three times. The pairs level subtracts it three times, once for each pair. Net so far, zero, which is wrong, and the triple level repairs it by adding one. Net count, one. Correct. That is the last plus sign in the three-event formula accounted for.

4. The general formula for n events

Nothing in the argument depended on there being three events. For events A1 through An, the notes write the union's probability as an alternating sum over levels. Level 1 is all the single events. Level 2 is all the pairwise intersections, every choice of two distinct events. Level 3 is all the triple intersections, and so on up to level n, which is the single term where all n events intersect.

P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An) = ∑ P(Ai) − ∑i<j P(Ai ∩ Aj) + ∑i<j<k P(Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak) − ... ± P(A1 ∩ ... ∩ An) A1, ..., An are n events. The first sum runs over the n events one at a time. The second runs over every pair with i < j, and the notes underline that it holds C(n, 2) terms, "n choose 2". Each later level sums over every choice of that many distinct events. The sign of level m is plus when m is odd and minus when m is even, and the final term's sign works out to (−1)n+1.

The region tally explains the alternation at every level at once. A point lying in exactly k of the events is counted C(k, 1) times at level 1, C(k, 2) times at level 2, and so on, with alternating signs. The alternating total works out to exactly 1 for every k from 1 to n, which is precisely the once-each count the union needs. You can watch it happen on the small cases: for a point in one event, 1; for a point in two, 2 − 1 = 1; for a point in three, 3 − 3 + 1 = 1. Section 3 checked those by hand on the picture.

5. A numeric check

Constructed for this page rather than taken from the notes: roll one fair die. Keep the two events from Part 1, A = "even face" = {2, 4, 6} and B = "multiple of three" = {3, 6}, and add C = "face is 1 or 2" = {1, 2}.

  1. Price the singles. P(A) = 3/6, P(B) = 2/6, P(C) = 2/6. Sum, 7/6. Already above 1, which is your first hint that overlaps are being counted more than once: A and C share the face 2.
  2. Price the pairs. A ∩ B = {6}, worth 1/6. A ∩ C = {2}, worth 1/6. B ∩ C = ∅, worth 0. Sum of subtractions, 2/6.
  3. Price the triple. No face is even, a multiple of three, and 1 or 2 at once, so P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 0. It adds nothing.
  4. Combine. 7/6 − 2/6 + 0 = 5/6. Counting the union directly, {2, 3, 4, 6} ∪ {1, 2} = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}, five faces out of six. The formula and the count agree.
Where the notes are looseThe notes state the general formula and mark the C(n, 2) term count at the pairs level, but they do not derive the sign pattern. The region-counting tally in sections 3 and 4 is the standard argument, and it is the reason the pattern is what it is: each level corrects what the levels above it left wrong. The identity that the alternating tally always lands on exactly 1 is a binomial-coefficient fact; this page checks it for k up to 3 and asks you to trust the pattern for larger k, which is also what the notes do.

Check yourself

In the three-event formula, why does P(A ∩ B ∩ C) arrive with a plus sign?
The all-three region is counted at every level: three times by the singles, minus three times by the pairs, so it stands at net zero and one final addition restores the once-only count. Size has nothing to do with it; the triple region is usually the smallest of the seven. There is no rule tying the sign to the number three, as the general formula's alternating pattern shows. And the triple region is the opposite of disjoint from the pairwise overlaps: it lies inside all three of them.
How many terms sit at the pairs level of inclusion-exclusion for n = 5 events?
The pairs level runs over every choice of two distinct events out of five, and there are C(5, 2) = 10 of those. Five is the count at the singles level, not the pairs level. Twenty-five counts ordered pairs, but "A and B" is the same intersection as "B and A", so order does not double anything. Twenty has no counting argument behind it at all; it is a number that merely looks near the right size.
Your teacher. Draw three overlapping circles freehand, shade the seven regions in different pressures of pencil, and tally the formula's count for each region. The five minutes of pencil work is worth more than rereading this page twice, because the tally is the derivation, and the derivation is what lets you reconstruct the formula in an exam room without memorising it. It is also the honest way to price "any of these things took the endpoint down": latency breach, error, and saturation are overlapping events, and the pairwise corrections are the shared incidents that a plain sum would count twice.
