Lecture 6 · Part 10 of 14 · the Poisson

One positive number is enough to fix a distribution over every counting number

10 min the shortest source Three lines in the notes, one honest ambiguity, and the last discrete shape before the lecture crosses over.

The one-minute version

  • The Poisson distribution has a single parameter λ > 0, and its mass is pX(k) = e−λλk / k!.
  • The notes give it in three lines and nothing else: the parameter, the support, the formula. No proof, no example, no interpretation, no drawing. This page does not invent any of those.
  • What this page can do honestly is name every symbol, check that the masses add to one, and draw the shape the formula gives.
  • The support is written N, and one paragraph earlier the geometric's support was also written N. They cannot be the same set. The Poisson's masses add to one only if N starts at 0, and the geometric's only if it starts at 1.
  • The check is not a technicality: it is the only way to tell, from the notes alone, which reading of a symbol was intended.

1. The definition, symbol by symbol

pX(k) = e−λ λk / k! λ, lambda, is the single parameter, and the notes require only that it is strictly positive. It is not itself a probability and may be any size at all. k is the value being priced, a counting number. λk is lambda multiplied by itself k times. k! is k factorial, the product of the whole numbers from 1 up to k, with 0! = 1 by the usual convention. e−λ is the constant e raised to minus lambda, and its whole job is to be the number that makes the masses add to exactly one.

Two things are worth flagging immediately. First, this is the only shape in the lecture whose formula contains a transcendental constant, and section 3 shows that the constant is not decoration but the exact normaliser. Second, the support is unbounded, like the geometric's, so this is the second countably infinite case of Part 3.

This is the whole of what the notes sayThe source gives the heading "Poisson", the constraint λ > 0, the support N, and the formula, on three handwritten lines. It offers no worked example, no derivation, no application and no drawing, in contrast to the geometric on the same page, which gets a proof and a margin gloss. This course does not fill that gap from outside the source: no interpretation of what lambda counts is asserted here, because none is in the notes and importing one would be presenting somebody else's teaching as the lecturer's. What is on this page is the formula, its symbols named, one check this course ran on it, and the shape it draws.

3. Why the masses add to one

The notes assert the formula and do not check it. The check is short and it is worth running once, because it explains what the constant in front is doing.

This course's derivationThe four steps below are this course's own. They use one fact from calculus, the series for the exponential function, which is outside the notes and is the only outside ingredient anywhere on this page. It is used to verify the notes' formula rather than to extend it.
  1. Add the masses over the support. The total is ∑k = 0∞ e−λλk/k!.
  2. Pull the constant out. The factor e−λ does not depend on k, so it comes outside: e−λ ∑k = 0∞ λk/k!.
  3. Recognise the series. The sum that is left is the standard series for eλ.
  4. Multiply. e−λ × eλ = 1. So the masses add to one, for every positive lambda.

That is what the leading constant is for. Strip it away and the remaining numbers add to eλ, which is bigger than one for every positive lambda, so they would not be probabilities. Dividing by eλ, which is the same as multiplying by e−λ, is what turns a set of positive weights into a distribution.

4. The letter N, and why it means two things

The notes give the geometric the support N and, five lines later, give the Poisson the support N. Run the sum check on each and the two cannot be the same set.

  1. Poisson starting at 0. Section 3 just showed the total is exactly 1. Correct.
  2. Poisson starting at 1. Drop the k = 0 term, which is e−λ, and the total becomes 1 − e−λ, which is strictly less than one. Not a distribution.
  3. Geometric starting at 1. Part 9 showed the total is 1, since P(X > 0) = (1 − p)0 = 1. Correct.
  4. Geometric starting at 0. The term at k = 0 would be p(1 − p)−1, which exceeds 1 for every p and is not a probability at all. Not even a candidate.

So N means the whole numbers including zero for the Poisson and excluding zero for the geometric, and the notes use one symbol for both. This is a notation collision rather than a mistake in either formula: both conventions for N are in common use, and the notes simply do not say which is meant where.

6. Check yourself

What job does the factor e to the minus lambda do in the Poisson mass function?
Stripping it away leaves a sum of e to the lambda, so dividing by that, which is multiplying by e to the minus lambda, is exactly what makes the total one. Keeping each mass below one is a side effect rather than the job, and it would be achieved by many other constants. The shrinking at large k comes from the k factorial in the denominator, which outgrows lambda to the k whatever the constant in front is. And a constant multiplier cannot move where the weight sits, since it scales every mass by the same amount.
Why can the letter N not mean the same set for both the geometric and the Poisson?
Dropping zero from the Poisson leaves a total of one minus e to the minus lambda, and adding zero to the geometric asks for a mass bigger than one, so each shape admits exactly one reading. Where the shapes appear in the notes is irrelevant to the mathematics; in fact both are on page six. No rule forces a countably infinite support to start anywhere, which is exactly why the letter has to be read from context. And both shapes have one parameter, so the count could not distinguish them even if it were relevant.
Your teacher. When a formula arrives with no story attached, the honest thing is to say so and then do what you can with the formula alone. That is what this page does. Two habits are worth taking from it. First, always run the sum check on a new PMF: it costs one line and it catches both wrong supports and wrong constants. Second, when a shape has a parameter, draw it at two settings before you decide you understand it, because the parameter is usually the part you will be asked about and it is never visible in a single picture. For the record, the standard story this shape carries in systems work: X counting arrivals in a fixed window with lambda as the arrival rate is the textbook model of requests reaching a serving endpoint, and the hub's inference course speaks of arrival rate and queueing in exactly those terms.
