8 min no new arithmetic What the credit limit, the button test, and the indicator hunt all had in common, and where the course goes from here.

The one-minute version

  • Data science is making decisions under uncertainty. Every case in this lecture ended in a decision, not in a number.
  • Statistics summarises noisy observations, finds robust patterns, and separates signal from randomness.
  • Probability models uncertainty explicitly, quantifies risk, and reasons about outcomes that have not happened.
  • Lectures 2 to 12 build the formal machinery underneath all of it, in the order the last slide of the deck lays out.

1. The same move, three times

Slide 19 gathers the lecture into a single claim: statistical thinking converts data into informed, defensible decisions. It is worth checking that claim against what actually happened, because all three cases really did follow the same shape even though they looked nothing alike.

Read the columns rather than the rows and the pattern is obvious. In every case something real was buried under something random. In every case a statistic pulled the real thing out. And in every case the number was not the deliverable: the decision was.

A few specific promissory notes from earlier pages, so you know where they get paid:

  • Part 1 waved at P(Model | Data) without saying how you would ever compute it. Lecture 2, on conditional probability, is where that becomes a real object.
  • Part 3 used C(10, 6) = 210 without explaining where 210 comes from. Lecture 3, on counting and sampling, is the answer.
  • Part 4 integrated a density and asserted that areas are probabilities. Lecture 6 makes that precise.
  • Part 5 quoted the Central Limit Theorem as a fact. Lecture 11 states and justifies it, together with the law of large numbers behind Part 6.
  • Part 7 computed a correlation from a formula on a slide. Lecture 8 derives that formula from covariance.

5. Check yourself

Across all three cases in this lecture, what was the final deliverable in each one?
Every case ended in an action: approve the limit, deploy the gold button, watch session time. The summary statistic, the fitted model, and the interval all appeared along the way, and each was necessary, but none of them was what anybody was actually asking for. That is the sentence slide 19 closes on, and it is why a number without a decision attached is unfinished work.
Which lecture in the plan on slide 20 finally makes the Central Limit Theorem, used informally in Part 5, precise?
Slide 20 names Lecture 11 as "Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem", which is exactly the pair of results that Part 5 and Part 6 leaned on. Lecture 7 supplies expectation, which the CLT needs but is not the theorem itself. Lecture 2 builds the axioms that everything rests on, several steps earlier. Lecture 12 is about conditional expectation and minimum mean square error estimation, which is a different destination entirely.
Your teacher. Before you open Lecture 2, do one thing: go back to the lecture map and try to state each of the eight parts in one sentence from memory. Whatever you cannot state is what to re-read, and re-reading two pages beats re-reading eight. Then take one number from your own work this week and ask the three questions this lecture installed: what is the noise here, what summary survives it, and what decision does it support?
