Lecture 1 · Part 4 of 8 · Slides 9 and 10

One robust number becomes a whole model, and the model makes the decision

12 min a density and one integral The median said what an ordinary day looks like. It says nothing about a bad day. This page builds the thing that does.

The one-minute version

  • A summary is not a model. 415 tells you about the middle; it cannot tell you the chance of a Rs 2,000 day.
  • The slide assumes daily spend follows an exponential distribution, whose whole shape is fixed by one number, the rate λ.
  • For an exponential, median = ln(2) / λ. Turning that around and putting the median in gives λ = 0.693 / 415 ≈ 0.00167.
  • The chance of a day above Rs 2,000 is then e−3.34 ≈ 0.035, which is 3.5 percent. The policy threshold is 5 percent, so the loan is approved.

1. Why a summary is not enough

Parts 2 and 3 ended somewhere solid. The median, Rs 415, is a defensible description of an ordinary day for this user, and we know exactly how hard it would be to fool. But now read the actual credit question again: should this person get a Rs 10,000 daily limit? That question is not about ordinary days. It is about the bad ones. And a median, by construction, has thrown away everything about the bad ones: it only knows that half the days are above it.

To answer a question about the tail you need a shape for the whole distribution, not a point on it. That means moving from a summary to a model, and moving from the backward engine to the forward one.

2. The exponential distribution, named piece by piece

The model the slide adopts is written as a probability density function:

f(x) = λ e−λx, for x ≥ 0 x is a possible daily spend, in rupees. λ ("lambda") is the rate: one number that fixes the entire curve. A bigger λ makes the curve fall away faster, meaning big days are rarer. e is the constant 2.71828..., and e−λx shrinks steadily as x grows, which is what makes the tail thin. f(x) is a density, not a probability. Probabilities are areas under this curve, never heights of it.

That last line is the one worth pausing on, because it is the usual stumbling point. For a continuous quantity like money, the probability of any exact value is zero: nobody spends exactly Rs 2,000.000000. Questions only make sense over ranges, and the answer to a range question is the area under the curve across that range. The total area under the whole curve is 1, because the day has to cost something.

3. Fitting the one parameter, worked in full

The exponential has exactly one dial, so we only need one fact about the data to set it. The slide uses the median, which is precisely the robust number Part 2 fought for. Here is why the relationship between the median and λ looks the way it does.

  1. Start from the tail. For an exponential, the probability of exceeding some level t has a famously clean form: P(X > t) = e−λt. This is the same integral worked in section 4 below, done with t in place of 2,000.
  2. Say what "median" means in that language. The median m is the level with half the days above it, so by definition P(X > m) = 0.5, which gives e−λm = 0.5.
  3. Take logarithms of both sides. −λm = ln(0.5) = −ln(2), so λm = ln(2), and therefore m = ln(2) / λ. This is the relationship the slide states.
  4. Turn it around to solve for the dial. λ = ln(2) / m, and ln(2) ≈ 0.693.
  5. Substitute the empirical median. λ = 0.693 / 415 = 0.00167 per rupee.
The assumption is doing real work hereNothing in the ten-day table establishes that spending is exponential. Ten points cannot identify a distribution family; they barely pin down a mean. And the shape does not obviously match: an exponential says the most likely region for a day is right next to zero and that days get steadily rarer as they get bigger, whereas the observed days bunch around Rs 400 with nothing below Rs 350. The exponential is chosen because it is the simplest one-parameter model of a positive quantity with a long right tail, and because it makes the next step a one-line calculation. That is a legitimate modelling choice, and it is also a choice that a credit committee is entitled to challenge. Say it out loud rather than letting it hide inside the arithmetic.

4. Reading the risk off the model, worked in full

Slide 10 states the policy in one sentence: approve the loan if there is less than a 5 percent chance of a spend spike above Rs 2,000. Now that we have a curve, that is an area question, and the area is an integral.

P(X > 2000) = ∫2000∞ λ e−λx dx = [ −e−λx ]2000∞ The ∫ sign means "add up the area under the curve", starting at 2,000 and running to infinity. The square brackets hold the antiderivative, the function whose slope is the thing inside the integral, and the small numbers say where to evaluate it.
  1. Find the antiderivative. Differentiating −e−λx gives back λe−λx, so −e−λx is the function we need in the brackets.
  2. Evaluate at the top limit. As x runs off to infinity, e−λx shrinks to 0, so −e−λx becomes 0.
  3. Evaluate at the bottom limit. At x = 2,000 the expression is −e−2000λ.
  4. Subtract, top minus bottom. 0 − (−e−2000λ) = e−2000λ. All that work collapses to one clean term, which is why the exponential is such a convenient model.
  5. Put the fitted rate in. 0.00167 × 2,000 = 3.34, so the answer is e−3.34.
  6. Evaluate. e−3.34 ≈ 0.035, which is 3.5 percent.

Compare that against the policy. The threshold is 5 percent, the estimate is 3.5 percent, and 3.5 is below 5. Loan approved.

5. The full pipeline, actor by actor

Not on the slide, but worth one minuteSuppose the credit team had used the mean, Rs 1,225, instead of the median. For an exponential the mean is 1/λ, so that gives λ = 1/1225 ≈ 0.000816, and the risk becomes e−2000/1225 = e−1.633 ≈ 0.195, or about 19.5 percent. That is nearly four times the 5 percent limit, and the loan would have been declined. This arithmetic is not in the deck; it is worked here because it closes the loop. The choice between mean and median in Part 2 was not a stylistic preference. It flipped the decision.

6. Check yourself

More logs arrive and the user's median daily spend is re-estimated at Rs 830, exactly twice the old figure. What happens to the fitted λ?
λ = ln(2) / m, so the median sits in the denominator and doubling it halves λ. That is the right direction physically too: a user whose typical day is twice as expensive should have a flatter curve and a fatter tail, and a smaller λ is exactly what flattens the curve. It is not proportional, or the relationship would put m on top. The threshold of Rs 2,000 never enters the fit, only the later risk calculation. And the logarithm applies to the constant 2, not to the median.
What do the ten observed days actually establish about the exponential assumption made on slide 9?
The exponential family is chosen before the data is consulted, and the data is used for exactly one job, which is setting λ from the median. Ten observations nowhere near identify a distribution family, so the second option overstates the evidence badly. The third has it backwards: the parameter is the part that was estimated, and the family is the part that was assumed. The fourth is wrong because the median of 415 was computed from the ten days in Part 2, so it is measured, not assumed.
Your teacher. Whenever a risk number lands on your desk, ask two questions in this order. First, what distribution was assumed, and who chose it? Second, what one summary of the data was used to fit it? In this lecture the answers are "exponential, chosen for convenience" and "the median". Both answers are defensible, and both are choices somebody made, which means both can be argued with.
