Lecture 1: Introduction

8 pages start here Aug 11, 2026. One lecture, split into eight sittings, because a whole lecture in one page is a lecture you close.

The one-minute version

  • Data science sits where statistics, probability, and computation meet. This lecture is about the first two.
  • Statistics runs backward: it takes data you already have and infers the model behind it.
  • Probability runs forward: it takes a model you assume and predicts data you have not seen.
  • Three worked business cases carry the whole lecture: a credit limit, a button test, and a hunt for a leading indicator. Every one of them is really the same move, which is separating signal from noise.

How this lecture is arranged

Lecture 1 is an introduction, so it does almost no formal machinery. What it does instead is more useful for a first sitting: it takes three ordinary business problems and shows that each one is unanswerable until you decide which engine you are running, forward or backward. Everything formal in the rest of the course exists to make those two engines precise.

The lecture is split here into eight content pages. Each one is a single idea, sized to one sitting, and each one ends by telling you why you are about to turn the page. Read them in order the first time. After that, the formula sheet is the faster way back in.

The eight parts

Each part names the slides it covers, so you can read alongside the deck if you have it open.

  1. Two engines: probability and statistics (slides 3 and 4). The one distinction the rest of the course is built on. Which engine a question needs, and how to tell in one reading.
  2. Robust summaries: mean against median (slides 5 and 6). Ten days of spend, one enormous outlier, and two summaries that disagree by a factor of three.
  3. Why the median survives outliers (slides 7 and 8). The mean answers to magnitude; the median answers to frequency. Worked with a mixture model and a binomial calculation.
  4. From baseline to risk: the exponential model (slides 9 and 10). Turning one robust number into a full probability model, then reading a risk off it and approving a loan.
  5. A/B testing, the CLT, and confidence intervals (slides 11 to 14). Why a four point lift can still be nothing, and how the Central Limit Theorem lets you say how nothing it might be.
  6. The power of n (slide 15). The same experiment with five times the users. The lift stops being deniable, and the reason is one square root.
  7. Leading indicators and correlation (slides 16 to 18). Two candidate predictors, one real and one imaginary, told apart by a single number between minus one and plus one.
  8. Synthesis and the road ahead (slides 19 to 21). What the three cases had in common, the plan for lectures 2 to 12, and the books to read alongside them.

How to read the pages. Every page leads with a picture, then names the mechanism, then works the numbers. If a formula ever arrives before you can picture what it is doing, that is a defect in the page and not in you. Your teacher. The single most useful thing you can do before starting Part 1 is to hold this question in your head: am I being told the model and asked about the data, or given the data and asked about the model? Almost everything in this lecture, and a surprising amount of everything after it, is one of those two.
