Bayes turns the question around: from effects back to causes

working 40 min Eight problems on paper. Small priors meeting strong evidence, one answer that is counter-intuitive and correct, and updating in sequence.

The one-minute version

  • Bayes rule reverses conditionals: P(Bi | A) = P(Bi)·P(A | Bi) / P(A), with P(A) expanded by total probability.
  • The three names: P(Bi) is the prior, P(A | Bi) is the likelihood, P(Bi | A) is the posterior.
  • A small prior survives surprisingly strong evidence. The base rate is part of the answer, not a footnote to it.
  • Updating is repeatable: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, and the result matches doing it all at once.

The concept in one paragraph

Conditional probabilities run in one direction: causes to observations. Bayes rule runs them backwards. If the events Bi partition the space into possible causes and A is the observation, then P(Bi | A) = P(Bi ∩ A)/P(A), and the multiplication rule rewrites the numerator as prior times likelihood, P(Bi)·P(A | Bi), while the law of total probability rewrites the denominator as the same product summed over all causes. The posterior is therefore a competition: each cause's prior weight multiplied by how well it explains what you saw, divided by the total explanatory weight. The lecture frames this as belief updating, prior belief in, data applied, updated belief out, and the problems below are that loop run by hand.

The problems

Problem 1warm-up

In the two-coin space, run Bayes formally: with A1, A2 the coin choices and E = "observed tails", compute P(A1 | E) by the formula, using P(E) from total probability. Compare with the direct computation you did in Set 13.

Show the worked solution
  1. Priors and likelihoods. P(A1) = P(A2) = 1/2. P(E | A1) = 1/2, P(E | A2) = 0.
  2. Evidence by total probability. P(E) = (1/2)(1/2) + (1/2)(0) = 1/4.
  3. Bayes. P(A1 | E) = P(A1)P(E | A1)/P(E) = (1/2 × 1/2)/(1/4) = (1/4)/(1/4) = 1.
  4. Compare. Set 13 computed the same 1 directly from P(A1 ∩ E)/P(E). Bayes is not new information; it is the direct computation reorganised around quantities you are likely to have: priors and likelihoods.

Sanity check. The two posteriors must sum to 1 over the partition: P(A2 | E) = 0, and 1 + 0 = 1.

Problem 2warm-up

A bag holds four fair coins and one two-headed coin. You pick a coin uniformly, toss twice, and see two heads.

Compute P(the coin is the two-headed one | both tosses heads) by Bayes. Set 14 already computed the evidence P(HH) = 2/5; reuse that.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the pieces. Prior: P(T) = 1/5 for the two-headed coin, P(F) = 4/5 for a fair one. Likelihoods: P(HH | T) = 1, P(HH | F) = 1/4.
  2. Evidence. From Set 14: P(HH) = (4/5)(1/4) + (1/5)(1) = 1/5 + 1/5 = 2/5.
  3. Bayes. P(T | HH) = (1/5 × 1)/(2/5) = (1/5)/(2/5) = 1/2.
  4. Read it. The prior of 1/5 doubled to a posterior of 1/2. Two heads is strong evidence, but the fair coin still explains it, one time in four, so it keeps half the posterior mass.

Sanity check. The posterior must exceed the prior, since HH supports the loaded coin: 1/2 > 1/5, and the two posteriors sum to 1/2 + 1/2 = 1.

Problem 3steady

A disease affects 0.1 percent of a population. A test detects every true case (sensitivity 1), but 5 percent of healthy people test positive. A random person tests positive.

Compute P(diseased | positive) by Bayes, using the tree above as a check on the arithmetic. Explain in one sentence why the answer is so much smaller than the sensitivity.

Hint

The denominator is total probability: true positives plus false positives. The tree counts both in whole people.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the pieces. Prior P(D) = 0.001. Likelihoods: P(+ | D) = 1, P(+ | H) = 0.05.
  2. Evidence. P(+) = (0.001)(1) + (0.999)(0.05) = 0.001 + 0.04995 = 0.05095.
  3. Bayes. P(D | +) = 0.001/0.05095 ≈ 0.0196, about a 2 percent chance.
  4. Check by counting. Per 100,000 people: 100 true positives and 4,995 false positives, so 100/5,095 ≈ 0.0196. Same answer, no floating point.
  5. Why so small. The healthy group is nearly a thousand times larger than the diseased group, so even a 5 percent leak from it dwarfs the true positives. The sensitivity answers "given diseased, how often positive"; the question asked runs the other way, and the base rate lives in that reversal.

Sanity check. The posterior must exceed the prior 0.001, since a positive is genuine evidence: 0.0196 is twenty times the prior, and still small.

Problem 4steady

The lecture works this one in full: in the two-coin space, with Ec = "observed heads", P(Ec) = 3/4, and the posteriors come out as P(A1 | Ec) = 1/3 and P(A2 | Ec) = 2/3.

Reproduce both posteriors from Bayes, showing P(A1 ∩ Ec) = P({(C1, H)}) = 1/4 and P(A2 ∩ Ec) = P({(C2, H)}) = 1/2 explicitly, and confirm the two posteriors sum to 1.

Show the worked solution
  1. Numerator for coin 1. P(A1)P(Ec | A1) = (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4, which is P({(C1, H)}) directly from the outcome table.
  2. Numerator for coin 2. P(A2)P(Ec | A2) = (1/2)(1) = 1/2, which is P({(C2, H)}).
  3. Evidence. P(Ec) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4, the lecture's figure, and also 1 - P(E) = 1 - 1/4 by the complement rule.
  4. Bayes. P(A1 | Ec) = (1/4)/(3/4) = 1/3 and P(A2 | Ec) = (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3. Sum: 1/3 + 2/3 = 1, as a partition's posteriors must.

Sanity check. Heads is twice as likely under the loaded coin, so the loaded coin's posterior should be twice the fair coin's: 2/3 is exactly twice 1/3.

Problem 5steady

The lecture closes by posing, without solving: pick one of the two coins, toss it twice, and observe heads both times. What are P(A1 | EHH) and P(A2 | EHH)?

Solve the lecture's exercise by Bayes with the two-toss likelihoods P(EHH | A1) = 1/4 and P(EHH | A2) = 1, and compare the shift with the one-toss posteriors of Problem 4.

Hint

The evidence is P(EHH) = (1/2)(1/4) + (1/2)(1). The structure is identical to Problem 4 with one likelihood changed.

Show the worked solution
  1. Evidence. P(EHH) = (1/2)(1/4) + (1/2)(1) = 1/8 + 1/2 = 5/8.
  2. Bayes for coin 1. P(A1 | EHH) = (1/2 × 1/4)/(5/8) = (1/8)/(5/8) = 1/5.
  3. Bayes for coin 2. P(A2 | EHH) = (1/2 × 1)/(5/8) = (1/2)/(5/8) = 4/5.
  4. Compare. One head shifted the beliefs to 1/3 - 2/3. The second head pushed further to 1/5 - 4/5. Each consistent observation strengthens the cause that explains it best, and the shifts compound.

Sanity check. The two posteriors sum to 1/5 + 4/5 = 1, and the loaded coin's posterior rose from 2/3 to 4/5, the right direction for more heads.

Problem 6steady

One of three servers served a bad response. From traffic shares, the priors are P(S1) = 0.5, P(S2) = 0.3, P(S3) = 0.2. A latency spike was observed, with likelihoods P(spike | S1) = 0.1, P(spike | S2) = 0.4, P(spike | S3) = 0.8.

Compute all three posteriors. State which server was the best guess before the data and which is the best guess after, and note how the ranking moved.

Show the worked solution
  1. Evidence. P(spike) = (0.5)(0.1) + (0.3)(0.4) + (0.2)(0.8) = 0.05 + 0.12 + 0.16 = 0.33.
  2. Posterior for S1. 0.05/0.33 = 5/33 ≈ 0.152.
  3. Posterior for S2. 0.12/0.33 = 12/33 = 4/11 ≈ 0.364.
  4. Posterior for S3. 0.16/0.33 = 16/33 ≈ 0.485. The three sum to 33/33 = 1.
  5. Ranking. Before the data: S1 (0.5), then S2 (0.3), then S3 (0.2). After: S3, then S2, then S1. The ranking inverted, because the likelihoods run the other way and the evidence was informative enough to overcome the priors.

Sanity check. The biggest prior fell to the smallest posterior, which is only possible when the likelihoods differ by more than the priors do: here 0.8 against 0.1 is a factor of 8, against a prior factor of 2.5.

Problem 7hard

A family has two children, each birth independently a boy or girl with probability 1/2. You learn that the family has at least one boy: compute P(both children are boys). Then you instead learn that the older child is a boy: compute P(both boys) again. Explain why the two answers differ, given that both conditioning events are true in every family where both children are boys.

Show the worked solution
  1. The sample space. Four equally likely birth orders: BB, BG, GB, GG, where the first letter is the older child.
  2. Condition on "at least one boy". The event holds BB, BG, GB: three outcomes. Both-boys holds only BB. P(both boys | at least one boy) = (1/4)/(3/4) = 1/3.
  3. Condition on "the older is a boy". The event holds BB, BG: two outcomes. Both-boys again holds only BB. P(both boys | older is a boy) = (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2.
  4. Why they differ. The two conditioning events are not the same event, so they shrink the sample space differently: three survivors versus two. The first statement is true for the GB family too, and that extra rival dilutes the answer. Equal truth on the favourable case does not mean equal information.

Sanity check. Weaker information must leave the answer closer to the unconditional 1/4 than stronger information does: 1/3 sits between 1/4 and 1/2, exactly as ordered.

Problem 8hard

Starting from P(A1) = P(A2) = 1/2 in the two-coin space: update on one head to get the posteriors of Problem 4. Then update again on a second, independent head, using those posteriors as the new priors and the one-toss likelihoods. Confirm that the final posterior matches Problem 5's one-shot answer of 4/5, and explain why the two routes must agree.

Hint

After the first head the priors are 1/3 and 2/3. The second head has the same likelihoods as the first: 1/2 under the fair coin and 1 under the loaded one, independent of the first toss given the coin.

Show the worked solution
  1. First update. From Problem 4: P(A1 | H) = 1/3, P(A2 | H) = 2/3.
  2. Second evidence. The second head is independent of the first given the coin, so the likelihoods are unchanged: P(H | A1) = 1/2, P(H | A2) = 1.
  3. New evidence total. P(second H | first H) = (1/3)(1/2) + (2/3)(1) = 1/6 + 2/3 = 5/6.
  4. Second update. P(A2 | H, H) = (2/3 × 1)/(5/6) = (2/3)/(5/6) = 4/5, and P(A1 | H, H) = 1/5.
  5. Match and why. Problem 5's one-shot Bayes with likelihoods 1/4 and 1 gave the same 1/5 - 4/5. The two routes agree because both compute the same quantity, P(A2 ∩ H ∩ H)/P(H ∩ H); sequential updating merely factors the joint as (first step) times (second step), and multiplication is associative. Any grouping gives the same number.

Sanity check. The intermediate evidence 5/6 sits between the two likelihoods 1/2 and 1, and the final posterior 4/5 exceeds the intermediate 2/3, since the second head again favoured the loaded coin.

Traps in this concept

  • Quoting the likelihood as the posterior. P(positive | diseased) is not P(diseased | positive). Problem 3's test is perfect on the diseased and still leaves a 2 percent posterior.
  • Dropping the base rate. The prior is a factor of the numerator, not decoration. Two problems here, 3 and 6, turn on priors losing or winning purely through their size.
  • Forgetting to renormalise. The raw products P(Bi)P(A | Bi) do not sum to 1; the division by P(A) is what makes the posteriors a distribution. Always check the posteriors sum to 1.
  • Treating "the same fact, heard differently" as the same evidence. Problem 7: "at least one boy" and "the older is a boy" describe overlapping but different conditioning events, and the posteriors differ.
  • Reusing stale priors. In sequential updating the second step must start from the first step's posterior. Starting it again from 1/2 - 1/2 throws away the first observation and lands on the wrong answer.
Your teacher. The exit test is Problem 3 cold: prior, likelihoods, total-probability denominator, one division, and a sentence on why the base rate dominates. That single pattern is the shape of every base-rate question you will ever meet, in exams and in incident reviews alike.
