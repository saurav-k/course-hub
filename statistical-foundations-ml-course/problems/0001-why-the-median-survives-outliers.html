Problem set 2 · Lecture 1 · after Part 3

One value has one vote, and the median only counts votes

45 min working Seven problems on magnitude against frequency, with paper. Attempt each before opening its solution.

How to use this set

  • The recap is one paragraph and one picture. Answer the two check questions from memory, then work the problems without the lesson open.
  • The difficulty of each problem is stated in its header and is meant literally. Problem 5 is the hardest on this set.
  • Solutions are worked to the course standard: every symbol named, every step written. They are for checking, not for reading first.

The recap

The mean is a weighted average: each possible value enters multiplied by how likely it is. The deck's model, a quiet Rs 500 day with probability 0.90 and an extreme Rs 50,000 day with probability 0.10, gives E[X] = 0.90 × 500 + 0.10 × 50,000 = 5,450, a value no day ever comes near, because a hundredfold difference in size beats a tenfold difference in rarity. The median is a vote count: it moves only when a majority of the sorted positions change hands. For ten days that means at least six extreme days before the middle pair falls, and the deck prices that at about 0.01%. The mean answers to magnitude and can be moved by one unusual value; the median answers to frequency and needs a conspiracy.

Check the recap

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the deck's model a day is Rs 500 with probability 0.9 and Rs 50,000 with probability 0.1. Why does the mean land at 5,450?
E[X] = 0.9 × 500 + 0.1 × 50,000 = 450 + 5,000 = 5,450: each outcome weighted by its probability, and the enormous one wins because a hundredfold size advantage beats a tenfold rarity penalty. Option B confuses the expectation of a model with the total of a sample; no ten days are being added here. Option C is backwards, since the median stays at 500 by counting days instead of rupees. Option D complains about the model rather than explaining the arithmetic, and the arithmetic alone fixes 5,450.
What is the smallest number of the ten days that must be extreme before the median of the ten moves?
The median of ten sorted values is the average of the 5th and the 6th, so both of those positions must be held by extreme days before the median joins them, and that takes six. One extreme day leaves the middle pair untouched however large it is, which is the mean's weakness and not the median's. Five extreme days still leave the 5th position holding the largest quiet day. And ten is not a threshold at all: six already moves it.

The problems paper and pen; open each solution only after committing to an answer

Problem 1warm-up

The values 2, 5 and 8 have mean 5 and median 5. Replace the 8 with 108. Recompute both summaries, and state in one sentence why exactly one of them moved.

Show the worked solution
  1. The original summaries. The total is 2 + 5 + 8 = 15, so the mean is 15 ÷ 3 = 5. Sorted, the middle value is 5. The two agree on this tidy little set.
  2. The new mean. The total becomes 2 + 5 + 108 = 115, so the mean is 115 ÷ 3 = 38.3, to one decimal place. Raising one value from 8 to 108 multiplied the mean itself by more than seven, from 5 to 38.3.
  3. The new median. Sorted, the list is [2, 5, 108] and the middle value is still 5. The 108 is still just "the largest one"; its size never reached the middle position.
  4. Why exactly one moved. The mean reads the size of every value, so changing a size changes it. The median reads only the rank, and the rank of the changed value did not change, so it had no effect.

Sanity check. The new mean must lie between 5 and 108, and 38.3 does; the new median must lie between 2 and 5 or between 5 and 108 depending on position, and the middle position still holds the 5.

Problem 2warm-up

A service answers in 200 ms on 95 percent of calls and in 5,200 ms on the remaining 5 percent, which hit a cold cache. Compute the mean response time and the median response time, and write one sentence a product manager could repeat about why the "average" call feels so much slower than the calls users actually see.

Show the worked solution
  1. Weight the fast outcome. 0.95 × 200 = 190. Nineteen calls in twenty contribute 190 ms between them to the long-run average.
  2. Weight the slow outcome. 0.05 × 5,200 = 260. One call in twenty contributes more than the other nineteen combined.
  3. Add them. E[X] = 190 + 260 = 450 ms.
  4. The median. Sort any large batch of calls: the middle one is a 200 ms call, because 95 percent of calls are. The median is 200 ms.
  5. The sentence. More than twice the mean response time comes from one call in twenty, so the "average" describes a mix that the typical user almost never experiences; the median describes the call they actually get.

Sanity check. The mean must sit between 200 and 5,200 and closer to 200 because the slow case is rare: 450 is one-twentieth of the way from 200 to 5,200, and the slow case is one-twentieth of the weight.

Problem 3working

Work with five days instead of the deck's ten, each independently extreme with probability 0.1. How many of the five days must be extreme before the median moves? What is the probability of that many or more? Compare your answer with the deck's ten-day figure of about 0.01 percent, and say what the comparison teaches about small samples.

Hint

The median of five sorted values is the 3rd one. Count the arrangements of k extreme days with C(5, k), price one arrangement, then add k = 3, 4 and 5.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the threshold. The median of five sorted values is the 3rd. Two extreme days leave the 3rd position with a quiet day. Three extreme days occupy positions 3, 4 and 5, so the median moves. The threshold is three of five.
  2. Price exactly three. C(5, 3) = 10 ways to choose which three days are extreme. One arrangement costs (0.1)3 × (0.9)2 = 0.001 × 0.81 = 0.00081. Together: 10 × 0.00081 = 0.0081.
  3. Price exactly four. C(5, 4) = 5, and one arrangement costs (0.1)4 × 0.9 = 0.00009. Together: 5 × 0.00009 = 0.00045.
  4. Price exactly five. One way, costing (0.1)5 = 0.00001.
  5. Add the tail. P(X ≥ 3) = 0.0081 + 0.00045 + 0.00001 = 0.00856, which is about 0.86 percent, or roughly one chance in 117.
  6. The comparison. Ten days needed six extreme days and cost about 0.01 percent; five days need only three and cost about 0.86 percent, nearly a hundred times more likely. The median's protection comes from the majority requirement, and a majority of a small dataset is itself small. Robustness is a property of the summary AND the sample size together.

Sanity check. The three tail terms must shrink fast, each at least a factor of nine below the last, since every extra required extreme day swaps a 0.9 for a 0.1 before the counts even fall: 0.0081, then 0.00045, then 0.00001, and their sum must stay well below P(exactly 2) = 0.0729, which it does.

Problem 4working

In the deck's model the extreme day is worth Rs 50,000. Suppose the rare outcome doubles to Rs 100,000, with the probabilities unchanged at 0.9 and 0.1. What happens to the mean and to the median? Then write the mean as a formula in V, the size of the extreme outcome, and use it to say exactly how much a one-rupee increase in V moves the mean.

Show the worked solution
  1. The new mean. E[X] = 0.9 × 500 + 0.1 × 100,000 = 450 + 10,000 = 10,450. Doubling the rare outcome nearly doubled the mean, from 5,450 to 10,450.
  2. The median. Unchanged at 500. The rare outcome is still one outcome in ten, so the majority vote still lands on the quiet days.
  3. The formula. E[X] = 0.9 × 500 + 0.1 × V = 450 + 0.1 V. The mean is a straight line in V, with the rare outcome's probability 0.1 as the slope.
  4. Read the slope. Every extra rupee on the extreme day lifts the mean by 0.1 rupees, which is just the probability of that day. There is no saturation and no limit: the mean keeps climbing as long as V does.

Sanity check. At V = 50,000 the formula gives 450 + 5,000 = 5,450, matching the deck, and doubling V to 100,000 adds exactly 0.1 × 50,000 = 5,000 to the mean.

Problem 5genuinely hard

Take the deck's nine ordinary days, 350, 380, 390, 400, 410, 420, 440, 460 and 500, and suppose you control the tenth value V and may set it to anything, positive or negative. (a) How far can you push the mean? (b) How far can you push the median? Find the full range of values the median can take as V varies, and locate the largest shift you can force.

Hint

The median of ten values is the average of the 5th and 6th sorted values. Work through the cases: V below 400, V between 400 and 410, V between 410 and 420, V above 420. In which cases do the two central positions change identity?

Show the worked solution
  1. The mean is unbounded. The nine honest days total 3,750, so the mean is (3,750 + V) ÷ 10. Setting V = 10,000,000,000 puts the mean near a billion. There is no ceiling and, with negative V, no floor.
  2. The median, V at or below 400. Sorted, V lands among the first five positions and the list ends [ ..., 400, 410, 420, 440, 460, 500]. The 5th value is 400 and the 6th is 410, so the median is 405.
  3. The median, V between 400 and 410. The list runs [ ..., 400, V, 410, ...], so the central pair is V and 410 and the median is (V + 410)/2, which slides from just above 405 up to 410.
  4. The median, V between 410 and 420. The central pair is 410 and V, so the median is (410 + V)/2, sliding from just above 410 up to just below 415.
  5. The median, V at or above 420. The central pair is back to 410 and 420, so the median is 415, the honest value.
  6. Assemble the range. As V sweeps every possible value, the median takes every value in [405, 415] and nothing outside it. The best attack pulls the median down by 10 rupees, from 415 to 405, by making V tiny. No value of V, however extreme, moves it up, and nothing moves it below 405.
  7. The contrast. One corrupted value moves the mean without bound and the median within a 10-rupee window bounded by honest neighbours. That boundedness, not immovability, is the precise meaning of "the median survives outliers".

Sanity check. Every median you can produce must lie between the 4th and 7th honest sorted values, 400 and 440, and the case work narrows that to [405, 415]; the endpoints 405 and 415 are themselves achieved by V at or below 400 and V at or above 420.

Problem 6working

For a window of n independent days, each extreme with probability 0.1, let t(n) be the smallest number of extreme days that moves the median. State t(5), t(10) and t(101) as a formula in n, and explain in words why the probability of reaching t(n) collapses as n grows, given that each extra required extreme day multiplies the price of one arrangement by about 0.1.

Show the worked solution
  1. The threshold formula. The median moves when the extreme days hold a majority of the sorted positions, so t(n) = the smallest integer strictly greater than n/2. For n = 5 that is 3; for n = 10 it is 6; for n = 101 it is 51.
  2. Check the odd case. With 101 days the median is the 51st sorted value. Fifty extreme days occupy positions 52 to 101 and leave position 51 honest, so the median holds. The 51st extreme day captures position 51 itself, and the median moves. The formula survives the odd count.
  3. Why the probability collapses. Reaching t(n) needs t(n) specific days to all be extreme, and each required day multiplies the price of one arrangement by 0.1. Adding ten days to the window raises the threshold by about five, which multiplies the price of a single arrangement by roughly 0.1 to the fifth, a factor of one in a hundred thousand. The combinatorial count C(n, t) grows too, but nowhere near fast enough to pay for that exponential discount in this range.
  4. The lesson. The median's robustness is not a fixed shield but a race between the majority requirement and the rarity of each additional defector. Bigger windows win that race by miles, which is why the deck's ten-day figure of about 0.01 percent is so much smaller than the five-day figure of 0.86 percent in Problem 3.

Sanity check. The thresholds 3, 6 and 51 are each just past half of their n, and the deck's own ten-day threshold was six, which the formula reproduces.

Problem 7working

The deck's model puts the median at exactly Rs 500. The ten observed days put it at Rs 415. Both are correct. Write two sentences: one saying what each figure is a property of, and one saying why they differ.

Show the worked solution
  1. What each figure belongs to. The 500 is a property of the model: in the two-outcome world where days are 500 with probability 0.9 and 50,000 with probability 0.1, the value a typical day takes is 500, full stop. The 415 is a property of the sample: it is the middle of those ten particular observed days, with all their ordinary scatter of 350 to 500.
  2. Why they differ. The model deliberately threw away the scatter to isolate one effect, so its "quiet day" is a single clean 500, while the real quiet days wobble around it and their middle lands at 415. The model is a caricature adopted to make the magnitude-against-frequency argument exact; the sample is the messy thing the argument was about.

Sanity check. Both figures sit in the same rough neighbourhood, a little above 400, which is what you expect when a model keeps the typical outcome of the data and drops only its noise.

Traps in this concept

  • Reading "robust" as "immovable". Enough corrupted values move any summary. The median's protection is that the price is a majority, and Problem 3 shows the majority of a small sample is cheap to buy.
  • Calling the mean wrong when it sits far from the data. The mean of 5,450 is not an error; it is the correct balance point of the model. It is answering a long-run totals question that nobody asked.
  • Forgetting that n is part of the protection. The same 10 percent extremity that is harmless at n = 100 is a one-in-a-hundred event at n = 5. Robustness claims should always carry the sample size.
  • Believing one bad value cannot touch a median at all. With an even count, one value landing strictly inside the central gap nudges the median, as Problem 5's 405 shows. The right claim is bounded, not zero.
  • Quoting a model's median and a sample's median as if they competed. One describes the assumed world, the other the observed one. Problem 7 is the difference.
Your teacher. The reusable question is "how much of my data would have to be wrong before this number lies to me". Ask it of any metric you rely on, and note the answer for the mean and the median of the same data: the gap between those two answers is the whole of this set.
