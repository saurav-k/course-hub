Add the singles, subtract the pairs, add the triples

working 35 min Seven problems on paper. Three-event inclusion-exclusion first, because you can draw it, then the general formula checked against the drawing.

The one-minute version

  • Inclusion-exclusion computes P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An) from the probabilities of all intersections.
  • The signs alternate: singles plus, pairs minus, triples plus, and so on, ending at (-1)n+1 times the n-fold intersection.
  • For n = 3 there are 3 singles, 3 pairs and 1 triple: seven terms, one per region of the three-circle Venn diagram.
  • The pairs alone are C(n, 2) terms. Counting terms is most of the arithmetic discipline this formula needs.

The concept in one paragraph

The addition rule of Set 10 handles two events. Inclusion-exclusion extends it to any number: to get the probability that at least one of A1, ..., An happens, add the n single probabilities, subtract the probability of every pair, add the probability of every triple, and keep alternating until the single term P(A1 ∩ ... ∩ An), whose sign is (-1)n+1. The mechanism is bookkeeping: a point lying in exactly k of the events is counted C(k, 1) - C(k, 2) + ... + (-1)k+1C(k, k) times by the right-hand side, and that alternating sum of binomials equals exactly 1 for every k from 1 to n. Three events is the case to internalise, because all seven terms have names you can point at on a drawing.

The problems

Problem 1warm-up

A platform tracks three features. P(a user touches search) = 0.40, P(alerts) = 0.30, P(exports) = 0.20. The pairwise overlaps are P(search ∩ alerts) = 0.12, P(search ∩ exports) = 0.08, P(alerts ∩ exports) = 0.06, and P(all three) = 0.03.

Find P(a user touches at least one feature).

Show the worked solution
  1. Add the singles. 0.40 + 0.30 + 0.20 = 0.90.
  2. Subtract the three pairs. 0.12 + 0.08 + 0.06 = 0.26, so the running total is 0.90 - 0.26 = 0.64.
  3. Add the triple. 0.64 + 0.03 = 0.67.

Sanity check. The answer must sit between the largest single (0.40) and 1, and it must be at least as big as any single event: 0.67 clears both tests.

Problem 2steady

Three sensors A, B, C watch a room. You are given P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(C) = 0.3, P(A ∩ B) = 0.18, P(A ∩ C) = 0.14, P(B ∩ C) = 0.10, and P(at least one sensor fires) = 0.8.

Find P(A ∩ B ∩ C) by rearranging inclusion-exclusion, and check the answer against the bound P(A ∩ B ∩ C) ≤ P(A ∩ B).

Hint

Write the three-event formula and move everything except the triple to the other side.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the formula. P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A ∩ B) - P(A ∩ C) - P(B ∩ C) + P(A ∩ B ∩ C).
  2. Isolate the triple. P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 0.8 - (0.5 + 0.4 + 0.3) + (0.18 + 0.14 + 0.10).
  3. Compute the sums. Singles sum to 1.2, pairs sum to 0.42, so the triple is 0.8 - 1.2 + 0.42 = 0.02.
  4. Check the bound. A ∩ B ∩ C ⊆ A ∩ B, so monotonicity demands the triple be at most 0.18: 0.02 ≤ 0.18 holds.

Sanity check. The triple must also be at most each pairwise term (0.18, 0.14, 0.10) and at least 0: 0.02 clears all four tests.

Problem 3steady

For four events A1, A2, A3, A4: count how many terms each layer of inclusion-exclusion contributes, write the full four-event formula with every term shown, and state the sign of the final quadruple term.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the singles. C(4, 1) = 4 terms, one per event.
  2. Count the pairs. C(4, 2) = 6 terms: 12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34.
  3. Count the triples. C(4, 3) = 4 terms: 123, 124, 134, 234.
  4. Count the quadruple. C(4, 4) = 1 term: 1234. In total 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 = 15 = 24 - 1 terms, one for every non-empty subset of the four events.
  5. Write the formula. P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ A3 ∪ A4) = ΣP(Ai) - Σi<jP(Ai ∩ Aj) + Σi<j<kP(Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak) - P(A1 ∩ A2 ∩ A3 ∩ A4). The signs run plus, minus, plus, minus: the last sign is (-1)4+1 = -1.

Sanity check. The sign of the last term alternates with n: plus for n = 3, minus for n = 4, matching (-1)n+1.

Problem 4steady

Throw two fair dice. Let A = "the first die shows 6", B = "the second die shows 6", C = "the two dice show the same number". Compute P(A ∪ B ∪ C) by inclusion-exclusion, then verify it by counting the complement on the grid.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the singles. Each of A, B, C holds 6 of the 36 cells, so each has probability 6/36 = 1/6, and the singles sum to 18/36.
  2. Name the pairs. A ∩ B = {(6, 6)}, so P = 1/36. A ∩ C: the dice are equal and the first is 6, again only {(6, 6)}, so P = 1/36. The same holds for B ∩ C. The pairs sum to 3/36.
  3. Name the triple. A ∩ B ∩ C = {(6, 6)} again, so P = 1/36.
  4. Run inclusion-exclusion. P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = 18/36 - 3/36 + 1/36 = 16/36 = 4/9.
  5. Verify by the complement. Outside the union means: first die not 6 (5 ways), second die not 6 (5 ways), dice unequal (5 of those 25 pairs per value: 25 - 5 = 20). The complement holds 20 cells, so the union holds 36 - 20 = 16 cells and P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = 16/36 = 4/9. The routes agree.

Sanity check. The three singles claim 18 cells but the union has only 16, so the overlaps must have overcounted by exactly 2: subtracting 3 pair-counts and adding 1 triple-count nets to -2. The picture shows why: cell (6,6) was counted three times and corrected to once.

Problem 5hard

Four events are perfectly symmetric: each has probability 0.3, every pair intersects with probability 0.1, every triple with probability 0.04, and all four with probability 0.01.

Compute P(at least one of the four happens), writing out the coefficient of each layer explicitly. The point of the problem is the term count: state how many terms each layer has before you multiply anything.

Hint

By symmetry every term in a layer is equal, so each layer contributes (number of terms) × (common value). The counts are C(4,1), C(4,2), C(4,3), C(4,4).

Show the worked solution
  1. Layer counts. C(4,1) = 4 singles, C(4,2) = 6 pairs, C(4,3) = 4 triples, C(4,4) = 1 quadruple.
  2. Layer sums. Singles: 4 × 0.3 = 1.2. Pairs: 6 × 0.1 = 0.6. Triples: 4 × 0.04 = 0.16. Quadruple: 1 × 0.01 = 0.01.
  3. Alternate the signs. P(union) = 1.2 - 0.6 + 0.16 - 0.01.
  4. Compute. 1.2 - 0.6 = 0.60; 0.60 + 0.16 = 0.76; 0.76 - 0.01 = 0.75.

Sanity check. The answer must lie between the largest single event (0.3) and 1: 0.75 does. If you got 0.61 you dropped the triples; if you got 0.76 you dropped the quadruple; if you got 1.15 you added the pairs instead of subtracting them.

Problem 6hard

Draw two cards from a standard 52-card deck, all C(52, 2) = 1,326 two-card hands equally likely. Let A = "both cards are hearts", B = "both cards are aces", C = "both cards are red".

Compute each of P(A), P(B), P(C) and the three pairwise probabilities, noticing which ones collapse. Then compute P(at least one of the three holds).

Hint

Every heart is red, so A ∩ C = A. Two aces cannot both be hearts, because the deck holds a single ace of hearts, so A ∩ B is empty. Count two-red-aces hands carefully: there are exactly two red aces.

Show the worked solution
  1. Singles. P(A) = C(13, 2)/C(52, 2) = 78/1,326 = 1/17. P(B) = C(4, 2)/C(52, 2) = 6/1,326 = 1/221. P(C) = C(26, 2)/C(52, 2) = 325/1,326.
  2. A ∩ B. Both cards hearts and both aces needs two heart aces, and the deck has one. So A ∩ B = ∅ and P = 0.
  3. A ∩ C. Hearts are red, so A ⊆ C and A ∩ C = A: P = 1/17. This term will cancel the P(A) in the singles.
  4. B ∩ C. Both aces and both red means both cards from {ace of hearts, ace of diamonds}: C(2, 2)/C(52, 2) = 1/1,326.
  5. Triple. Empty again, since A ∩ B is empty: P = 0.
  6. Run inclusion-exclusion. P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = (78 + 6 + 325)/1,326 - (0 + 78 + 1)/1,326 + 0 = 409/1,326 - 79/1,326 = 330/1,326.
  7. Simplify. 330/1,326 = 55/221 ≈ 0.249.

Sanity check. The union must be at least P(C) = 325/1,326 ≈ 0.245, since C is one of the three events, and 55/221 ≈ 0.249 clears that bar with the two non-heart-red pairs and the red-ace pairs added on top.

Problem 7hard

Using the region probabilities in the figure: compute P(A), P(B), P(C), the three pairwise probabilities, and P(A ∪ B ∪ C) by adding regions. Then run the inclusion-exclusion formula on those values and confirm it lands on the same union. Finally explain, region by region, why the alternating sum counts every region of the union exactly once and the outside region zero times.

Show the worked solution
  1. Singles by regions. P(A) = .10 + .05 + .05 + .05 = 0.25. P(B) = .15 + .05 + .10 + .05 = 0.35. P(C) = .20 + .05 + .10 + .05 = 0.40.
  2. Pairs by regions. P(A ∩ B) = .05 + .05 = 0.10. P(A ∩ C) = .05 + .05 = 0.10. P(B ∩ C) = .10 + .05 = 0.15. Triple: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 0.05.
  3. Union by regions. Add the seven numbers: .10 + .15 + .20 + .05 + .05 + .10 + .05 = 0.70.
  4. Run the formula. P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = (0.25 + 0.35 + 0.40) - (0.10 + 0.10 + 0.15) + 0.05 = 1.00 - 0.35 + 0.05 = 0.70. The routes agree.
  5. Why each region is counted once. A region inside exactly one circle appears in one single term: net count +1. A region inside exactly two circles, say A and B, appears in two singles (+2) and in one pair (-1): net +1. The middle region appears in three singles (+3), three pairs (-3), and the triple (+1): net +1. Every region of the union is counted exactly once, and the outside region is never counted, which is why the two sides must agree.

Sanity check. The seven regions plus the outside 0.30 sum to 1.00, as a probability space requires.

Traps in this concept

  • Dropping a layer. For four events there are fifteen terms. The symmetric Problem 5 tells you exactly what each dropped layer costs: no triples gives 0.61, no quadruple gives 0.76, against the true 0.75.
  • Forgetting that a pair can swallow a single. In Problem 6 the term P(A ∩ C) equals P(A) itself, because A sits inside C. Subsets make terms cancel, and that is easy to misread as an arithmetic error.
  • Assuming the triple is the product or zero. The triple intersection is its own quantity. In Problem 4 it equals each pair; in Problem 7 it is 0.05. Never infer it without being told or computing it.
  • Not sanity-checking against the biggest single. The union is always at least as likely as any one of its events. An inclusion-exclusion answer below max P(Ai) is wrong on arrival.
  • Counting C(n, 2) as n(n-1) instead of n(n-1)/2. The pairs are unordered: A1 ∩ A2 and A2 ∩ A1 are the same term.
Your teacher. Close the set by rebuilding the three-event formula from the figure in Problem 7 without looking: point at each of the seven regions and say which terms count it. If you can do that cold, the general n-event formula is just the same bookkeeping with binomial coefficients.
