Problem set 4 · Lecture 1 · after Part 5

A confidence interval is a promise about the procedure, not about the number

50 min problem set Seven problems on standard errors, honest readings, and the overlap test used in the one direction that is safe. Attempt each before opening its solution.

How to use this set

  • Everything here runs on one expression, SE = √( p̂(1 − p̂) / n ), and the deck's multiplier of 2 for 95 percent.
  • The computational problems ask for a number; the judgement problems ask what the number permits you to say. Both are on the exam of real work.
  • The deck's own case is the reference data throughout: 1,000 users per group, rates 0.30 and 0.34, intervals [27.1%, 32.9%] and [31.0%, 37.0%].

The recap

An observed click rate p̂ ("p hat") is a random quantity: re-run the experiment and it moves. Its centre is the true rate p and its spread is √( p(1 − p)/n ), which the Central Limit Theorem lets us treat as the spread of a bell curve for large n. A 95 percent confidence interval is the estimate plus and minus about two of those spreads, using p̂ in place of the unknown p. The deck's two groups give SEs of 0.0145 and 0.0150, and intervals [27.1%, 32.9%] and [31.0%, 37.0%]. The interval describes where repeated experiments would land, and the 95 percent belongs to the method, never to the one interval you happened to compute.

Check the recap

Answer from memory before revealing.

A 95 percent confidence interval for a click rate comes out as [0.30, 0.34]. What is the honest reading?
The 95 percent belongs to the method: across many repeats of the experiment, about 95 percent of the intervals it produces will cover the true rate. Option A pins the probability to the wrong object, because the true rate is fixed and the interval is the random part; this particular interval either covers it or does not. Options B and C describe users or clicks, and the interval makes no claim about the spread of individuals at all.
Two groups' 95 percent intervals overlap slightly. What follows?
Overlap leaves both stories live, because the proper test compares the observed difference against the standard error of the difference, which is smaller than the sum of the two separate errors; overlap can coexist with a real difference, as Problem 3 shows with numbers. Option A states the rule backwards: non-overlap is the direction that does imply a difference. Option C claims a proof of equality that no interval can deliver. Option D blames a construction that is working exactly as designed.

The problems paper and pen; open each solution only after committing to an answer

Problem 1warm-up

A coin of unknown fairness is flipped 400 times and lands heads 200 times. Using the deck's multiplier of 2, compute the standard error of the observed rate and the 95 percent interval for it.

Show the worked solution
  1. The observed rate. p̂ = 200 ÷ 400 = 0.5.
  2. The spread. p̂(1 − p̂) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. Divided by n: 0.25 ÷ 400 = 0.000625. The square root: SE = √0.000625 = 0.025.
  3. The interval. 2 × 0.025 = 0.05, so the 95 percent interval is 0.5 ± 0.05 = [0.45, 0.55].
  4. Read it. The data is consistent with a fair coin, and also with coins biased by as much as five points either way: 400 flips are not much evidence about fairness.

Sanity check. The product p̂(1 − p̂) is at its maximum of 0.25 exactly at p̂ = 0.5, so no other observed rate gives a wider interval at the same n.

Problem 2warm-up

Write, in exactly three sentences, what a 95 percent confidence interval claims, what it does not claim about the one interval you computed, and what it does not claim about the individuals in the sample.

Show the worked solution
  1. The claim. If the experiment were repeated many times and an interval constructed from each run in exactly this way, about 95 percent of those intervals would cover the true rate: the guarantee belongs to the procedure.
  2. The one interval. The interval you actually computed either covers the true rate or misses it, and no probability attaches to that single event after the fact; saying "there is a 95 percent chance the true rate is in here" moves the randomness from the interval, where it lives, to the rate, where it does not.
  3. The individuals. The interval says nothing about how spread out individual users are; a tight interval can sit on top of wildly varied behaviour, because its width measures only the uncertainty in the average, not the variety of the population.

Sanity check. All three sentences should survive the substitution of a coin for the click rate; if any of them only makes sense for "users", it is describing the wrong object.

Problem 3working

Two independent groups of 400 users each convert at p̂A = 0.30 and p̂B = 0.38. (a) Build both 95 percent intervals with the multiplier 2 and check that they overlap. (b) Now build the interval for the difference: its centre is 0.08 and its standard error is √( SEA² + SEB² ). Does that interval contain zero? (c) What does the pair of answers teach about the overlap test?

Hint

SEA = √(0.30 × 0.70 ÷ 400) and SEB = √(0.38 × 0.62 ÷ 400). Keep four decimal places until the end.

Show the worked solution
  1. The two standard errors. SEA = √(0.21 ÷ 400) = √0.000525 = 0.0229. SEB = √(0.2356 ÷ 400) = √0.000589 = 0.0243.
  2. The two intervals. A: 0.30 ± 0.0458 = [0.254, 0.346]. B: 0.38 ± 0.0486 = [0.331, 0.429]. They share the strip from 0.331 to 0.346: they overlap.
  3. The difference and its error. The observed difference is 0.38 − 0.30 = 0.08. The standard error of the difference is √(0.0229² + 0.0243²) = √(0.000525 + 0.000589) = √0.001114 = 0.0334.
  4. The interval for the difference. 0.08 ± 2 × 0.0334 = 0.08 ± 0.0668 = [0.013, 0.147]. Zero is not in it: by the deck's own two-standard-error ruler, the difference is real.
  5. The lesson. The same data produces overlapping individual intervals AND a significantly non-zero difference, because the difference carries one shared uncertainty rather than two separate ones. Overlap is a one-sided test: non-overlap establishes a difference; overlap establishes nothing.

Sanity check. The standard error of the difference, 0.0334, must sit between the larger single error, 0.0243, and the sum of the two, 0.0472; √(a² + b²) always does.

Problem 4working

The deck's button experiment is re-run with 10,000 users per group, and the same rates appear: 0.30 and 0.34. Build both 95 percent intervals with the multiplier 2, and state the gap between them.

Show the worked solution
  1. Group A. 0.30 × 0.70 = 0.21, and 0.21 ÷ 10,000 = 0.000021, so SEA = √0.000021 = 0.00458.
  2. Group B. 0.34 × 0.66 = 0.2244, and 0.2244 ÷ 10,000 = 0.00002244, so SEB = √0.00002244 = 0.00474.
  3. The intervals. A: 0.30 ± 0.00917 = [29.1%, 30.9%]. B: 0.34 ± 0.00947 = [33.1%, 34.9%].
  4. The gap. Group A reaches up to 30.9 percent and group B reaches down to 33.1 percent, leaving 2.2 points of clear space. Nothing is consistent with both.
  5. Where the gain came from. Ten times the users divided the variances by ten, so the standard errors fell by √10 ≈ 3.16, from about 0.015 to about 0.0046. The lift never changed; the resolution did.

Sanity check. The new standard errors must be the deck's old ones divided by √10: 0.0150 ÷ 3.16 = 0.0047, which matches group B's 0.00474.

Problem 5working

An oracle tells you the true rate is exactly p = 0.30. You do not use the oracle; you run the honest experiment with n = 1,000 and observe 268 clicks. Build the 95 percent interval. It will miss the oracle's truth. Did you or the method do anything wrong, and what would a notebook of a thousand such experiments show?

Show the worked solution
  1. The observed rate. p̂ = 268 ÷ 1,000 = 0.268.
  2. The standard error. 0.268 × 0.732 = 0.196176, and 0.196176 ÷ 1,000 = 0.000196176, so SE = √0.000196176 = 0.0140.
  3. The interval. 0.268 ± 0.028 = [0.240, 0.296]. The oracle's 0.30 lies outside it.
  4. Did anything go wrong? No. A 95 percent procedure misses one time in twenty by design; this run is one of the twenty. Nothing in the interval itself marks it as a miss, which is exactly why the guarantee has to be about the procedure.
  5. The notebook. Across a thousand runs at true p = 0.30, about 950 intervals would cover 0.30 and about 50 would not, and the misses would look exactly as plausible as the covers. That is the whole content of "95 percent confidence".

Sanity check. The observed 0.268 is about 2.3 standard errors below the true 0.30, an unlucky but ordinary draw from the bell of Part 5's recap figure.

Problem 6genuinely hard

You are designing an experiment and want the 95 percent interval's half-width to be at most 0.01, one percentage point, around a rate near 0.30. How many users per group does that need? How many if the rate might be near 0.5 instead? Write the inequality you solved, and say why experimenters size studies in advance rather than in arrears.

Hint

The half-width is 2√( p(1 − p)/n ). Set it ≤ 0.01 and solve for n. For the safety version, use the p that maximises p(1 − p).

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the requirement. 2√( p(1 − p)/n ) ≤ 0.01, which after dividing by 2 and squaring both sides becomes p(1 − p)/n ≤ 0.000025.
  2. Solve at p = 0.30. n ≥ 0.21 ÷ 0.000025 = 8,400 users per group.
  3. Solve at p = 0.5. The product p(1 − p) is largest at 0.5, where it equals 0.25, so the safe budget is n ≥ 0.25 ÷ 0.000025 = 10,000 users per group.
  4. Why in advance. Sizing in arrears means stopping when the interval looks decisive, and a wandering estimate looked at repeatedly will eventually look decisive even when nothing is there. The 95 percent guarantee describes one analysis of one pre-committed sample; it says nothing about the most flattering of many stopping points. Part 6's warning callout makes the same point from the other side.

Sanity check. At n = 8,400 and p̂ = 0.30 the half-width is 2√(0.21/8400) = 2√0.000025 = 2 × 0.005 = 0.01 exactly, so the budget is tight, not padded.

Problem 7genuinely hard

Instead of one analysis, your team checks the running experiment's intervals every day for ten days and stops the first day the two intervals separate. Explain, in three or four sentences, why the final interval's "95 percent" no longer means what Problem 2 defined, and why the corruption survives even though every individual day's computation is flawless.

Show the worked solution
  1. What the 95 percent described. It described one interval, produced by one pre-planned analysis of one fixed sample: across repeats, 95 percent of such intervals cover the truth.
  2. What the stopping rule changes. The team did not analyse one sample; they analysed ten growing samples and kept the first flattering one. Even if the two buttons are identical, the daily difference wanders, and a rule that stops on the first separation is guaranteed, with probability climbing toward certainty as the days pass, to eventually stop.
  3. Why flawless arithmetic does not help. Each day's interval is a correct 95 percent statement about that day's sample. The false guarantee appears in the selection: reporting the stopping day's interval as if it were the only analysis quietly discards the nine unflattering looks. The error is in the procedure, not in any computation, which is what makes it invisible on the page.
  4. The honest alternatives. Fix n in advance, as Problem 6 did, or use a sequential method whose guarantee is designed for repeated looks. What is not honest is reusing the one-look guarantee for a many-look procedure.

Sanity check. If ten fully independent looks each had a 5 percent false-separation chance, the chance of at least one false separation would be 1 − 0.9510 ≈ 40 percent; the real looks share data and are correlated, so 40 percent is an illustration of the direction, not a computation.

Traps in this concept

  • "There is a 95 percent chance the true value is in this interval." The most common sentence in business reporting and the wrongest one. The rate is fixed; the interval is the random object.
  • Reading overlap as equality. Overlapping intervals do not establish that two groups are the same; Problem 3 builds a case where they overlap and the difference is real.
  • Forgetting the substitution. The formula wants the true p and gets p̂. At n in the hundreds and thousands the cost is small, but it is an approximation, not an identity.
  • Expecting linear returns on n. Ten times the data bought about 3.16 times the precision, because the n sits under a square root. Budgets set in the language of "half the error" need four times the sample.
  • Stopping when the answer arrives. The multi-look trap of Problem 7 breaks the guarantee without breaking any single calculation, which is why it survives review after review.
Your teacher. Find one recurring report in your own work that quotes a percentage with no interval, and compute the interval for it once using SE = √( p̂(1 − p̂)/n ). If the interval is wider than the claims being made on the back of the number, you have found where the dashboard is overconfident.
