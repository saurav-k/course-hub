Problem set 8 · Lecture 2 · the probability space, part two

A candidate collection is a sigma-field only when every check passes

55 min problem set Eight problems on the closure axioms, checked exhaustively rather than conveniently. Attempt each before opening its solution.

How to use this set

  • The sigma-field F is the second pillar of the probability space: the collection of events that will be allowed a probability.
  • The axioms are three closure rules, and the skill this set builds is running all of them on every member, including the boring ones where failures hide.
  • The last three problems are the ones that separate recognising a sigma-field from understanding one: atoms, countable versus finite closure, and counting all the sigma-fields on a small space.

The recap

Not every subset of Ω needs a probability; the ones that get one are the members of a sigma-field F, a collection of subsets of Ω satisfying three axioms: (i) Ω ∈ F; (ii) if A ∈ F then its complement Ac is in F, the complement always taken inside Ω; and (iii) if A1, A2, ... are in F, then so is their union ∪n An. Two consequences come free: ∅ = Ωc is always in F, and F is closed under intersections, by De Morgan. The lecture motivates the rules as what a "collection of events" must allow: if A is an event, "not A" is an event; if A and B are events, so is "A or B". The discipline this set practises is exhaustive checking: every member's complement, every pair's union, before any verdict is announced.

Check the recap

Answer from memory before revealing.

On Ω = {H, T}, exactly one of these collections is a sigma-field. Which?
The trivial collection {∅, Ω} passes: Ω is a member, the complement of either member is the other, and both unions land back inside. Option A holds neither Ω nor ∅. Option B holds no Ω, and each singleton's complement is missing. Option C holds no ∅, and the complements are missing again. The passing answer is the smallest thing that satisfies all three axioms, which is why it is called the trivial sigma-field.
Why do the axioms insist that Ω itself belongs to the collection?
Ac means everything in Ω outside A, so the complement operation only means something relative to Ω, and with Ω in F the free member ∅ = Ωc arrives with it. Option A names a consequence, since P is defined on F, but the structural reason is the complement. Option C belongs to the third axiom's job. Option D has it backwards: ∅ enters precisely as the complement of Ω.

The problems paper and pen; open each solution only after committing to an answer

Problem 1warm-up

On Ω = {H, T}, check each candidate against all three axioms, listing every failure: (a) F = {{H}, {T}}; (b) F = {∅, Ω}; (c) F = {∅, {H}, {T}, Ω}.

Show the worked solution
  1. (a) fails, twice. Axiom (i): Ω is not a member. Axiom (iii): {H} ∪ {T} = Ω, which is not a member. (Complement closure happens to hold here, since {H}c = {T} and {T}c = {H} are both present, but one broken axiom is enough and this candidate breaks two.) Verdict: not a sigma-field.
  2. (b) passes. Ω ∈ F. Complements: ∅c = Ω and Ωc = ∅, both members. Unions: ∅ ∪ ∅ = ∅, ∅ ∪ Ω = Ω, Ω ∪ Ω = Ω, all members. This is the trivial sigma-field.
  3. (c) passes. Ω ∈ F. Complements: ∅ ↔ Ω and {H} ↔ {T}, all members. Unions: any pair lands in F, with {H} ∪ {T} = Ω the only non-trivial case. This is the power set, 2² = 4 members.

Sanity check. On a two-point space the only possible sigma-field sizes are 2 and 4 by the power-of-two census, and candidates (b) and (c) are exactly those two.

Problem 2warm-up

On Ω = {1, 2, 3}, check exhaustively: (a) F = {∅, {1}, {2, 3}, Ω}; (b) F = {∅, {1}, Ω}. For the one that fails, name the single axiom that kills it and the member whose complement is missing.

Show the worked solution
  1. (a), complements. ∅c = Ω ∈ F; {1}c = {2, 3} ∈ F; {2,3}c = {1} ∈ F; Ωc = ∅ ∈ F. Complement closure holds.
  2. (a), unions. The only union not obviously in F is {1} ∪ {2,3} = Ω ∈ F. All others return a member or Ω. Verdict: a sigma-field, the four-member one generated by the atom {1}.
  3. (b), the fatal check. {1} ∈ F, but its complement {2, 3} ∉ F. Axiom (ii) fails, and it fails on the very first complement taken. (Also Ω ∈ F holds and unions of members land in F, but one broken axiom is enough.) Verdict: not a sigma-field.

Sanity check. Candidate (a) has 4 = 2² members, a legal size, while (b) has 3, which is not a power of two and could not have survived however its members looked.

Problem 3working

On Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}, check each candidate exhaustively and give a verdict: (a) G1 = {∅, Ω, {1}, {2, 3, 4}}; (b) G2 = {∅, Ω, {1}, {2}, {1, 2}, {3, 4}}; (c) G3 = {∅, Ω} together with all six two-element subsets.

Show the worked solution
  1. (a), the checks. Ω ∈ G1. Complements: {1}c = {2, 3, 4} ∈ G1, and the other pairs swap. Unions: the only interesting one is {1} ∪ {2, 3, 4} = Ω ∈ G1. Verdict: a sigma-field, with 4 = 2² members.
  2. (b), the fatal check. {1} ∈ G2 but {1}c = {2, 3, 4} ∉ G2. Axiom (ii) fails. Verdict: not a sigma-field. (Note how tempting it is: the collection is closed under the unions you think of first, {1} ∪ {2} = {1, 2} is sitting right there. Exhaustive means checking {1}'s complement too.)
  3. (c), the fatal check. Take {1, 2} and {2, 3}, both in G3. Their union is {1, 2, 3}, which is neither empty, nor Ω, nor two-element. Axiom (iii) fails. Verdict: not a sigma-field. (Complement closure alone holds: the complement of any two-element subset of a four-point space is the other two elements, also in G3. One passing axiom does not rescue the others.)

Sanity check. The sizes are 4, 6 and 8; only 4 and 8 are powers of two, and exactly those two candidates survive, which agrees with the census.

Problem 4working

On Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}, construct the smallest sigma-field containing the set {1, 2}: list its members, and show that removing any one of them breaks an axiom. "Smallest" means every sigma-field on Ω that contains {1, 2} contains this whole collection.

Show the worked solution
  1. What membership forces. Any sigma-field containing {1, 2} must contain its complement {3, 4}, by axiom (ii). It already contains ∅ and Ω, as every sigma-field does.
  2. Check nothing new appears. The forced members are {∅, {1, 2}, {3, 4}, Ω}. Unions: {1, 2} ∪ {3, 4} = Ω, and all others return a member. Complements: the two non-trivial members swap. The collection closes. Verdict: {∅, {1, 2}, {3, 4}, Ω} is a sigma-field.
  3. Why smallest. Any sigma-field containing {1, 2} must contain all four members just listed, since each is either given or forced by the axioms. So this collection sits inside every competitor: it is the intersection of all of them.
  4. Why minimal in membership. Drop {3, 4} and complement closure dies. Drop ∅ and axiom (i)'s consequence dies, since Ωc must be a member. Drop Ω and axiom (i) itself dies. Drop {1, 2} and it no longer contains {1, 2}. Every member is load-bearing.

Sanity check. The answer has 4 = 2² members, matching the census for a two-atom collection, and its atoms are exactly {1, 2} and {3, 4}.

Problem 5working

On Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}, construct the smallest sigma-field containing both {1} and {2}. Identify the atoms, list all members, and verify closure under complements and unions.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the atoms. The members {1} and {2} are atoms. What remains of Ω is {3, 4}, which no member splits further, so the atoms are {1}, {2}, {3, 4}: three blocks that partition Ω.
  2. Every member is a union of atoms. Choosing each atom in or out gives 2³ = 8 members: ∅; {1}; {2}; {3, 4}; {1, 2}; {1, 3, 4}; {2, 3, 4}; Ω.
  3. Complement closure. Each member's complement is its atoms' complement: {1}c = {2, 3, 4}, {1, 2}c = {3, 4}, {1, 3, 4}c = {2}, and so on down the list. Every complement is in the collection.
  4. Union closure. The union of any two members is the union of their atoms, again a member: {1, 3, 4} ∪ {2, 3, 4} = Ω, {1} ∪ {2} = {1, 2}, {1} ∪ {3, 4} = {1, 3, 4}. The countable-union axiom holds with room to spare, since any union of members is the union of the atoms they touch.

Sanity check. Eight members is 2³, matching three atoms, and the eight listed sets are all distinct, which they must be if the atom-to-member correspondence is honest.

Problem 6working

The axioms mention complements and unions but never intersections. Prove that if A and B are in F, then A ∩ B is in F, using only the axioms and De Morgan's law. Then explain in one sentence why this proof is what lets the lecture describe F as "the collection of events" without listing intersection as a separate requirement.

Show the worked solution
  1. Complements first. A ∈ F gives Ac ∈ F by axiom (ii), and likewise Bc ∈ F.
  2. Union next. By axiom (iii) applied to two sets, Ac ∪ Bc ∈ F.
  3. De Morgan. (Ac ∪ Bc)c = A ∩ B. The left side is a complement of an F-member, so by axiom (ii) it is in F. Hence A ∩ B ∈ F.
  4. Why the lecture can omit it. Intersection closure is a theorem of the axioms, not an independent demand: any collection satisfying the three axioms automatically satisfies this one, so listing it would be redundant rather than wrong.

Sanity check. On Problem 5's eight-member collection, {1} ∩ {2} = ∅ and {1, 3, 4} ∩ {2, 3, 4} = {3, 4}: both intersections land in the list, as the theorem promises.

Problem 7genuinely hard

On Ω = {1, 2, 3, ...}, let F be the collection of all finite subsets together with all subsets whose complement is finite (the co-finite sets). Show that F satisfies axioms (i) and (ii) and is closed under finite unions, but fails axiom (iii): the union of the singletons {2}, {4}, {6}, ..., one for each even number, is not in F. Conclude what F is, and what it is not.

Hint

For the finite-union claim, check the three cases separately: finite with finite, finite with co-finite, co-finite with co-finite. For the failure, ask what the complement of the evens looks like.

Show the worked solution
  1. Axiom (i). Ω's complement is ∅, which is finite, so Ω is co-finite and Ω ∈ F.
  2. Axiom (ii). The complement of a finite set is co-finite, and the complement of a co-finite set is finite; either way it is back in F. Complement closure holds.
  3. Finite unions. Finite ∪ finite is finite. Finite ∪ co-finite has complement equal to an intersection of two finite sets, hence finite, so the union is co-finite. Co-finite ∪ co-finite has complement an intersection of two finite sets, hence finite, so the union is co-finite. Closed under finite unions.
  4. The failure. Each singleton {2n} is finite, so each is in F. Their union over all n is the set E of even numbers. E is infinite, so it is not finite, and its complement, the odd numbers, is also infinite, so E is not co-finite either. E ∉ F, and axiom (iii) fails.
  5. The verdict. F is a field: a collection satisfying the axioms with "countable" weakened to "finite". It is not a sigma-field. The gap between the two words is exactly the gap between finitely many unions and infinitely many, and this collection lives in it.

Sanity check. The evens and the odds are both infinite, which is the one configuration that escapes the finite/co-finite dichotomy; any set with a finite complement or a finite self is caught by F.

Problem 8genuinely hard

Every sigma-field on a finite space is the collection of all unions of the blocks of some partition of Ω, and different partitions give different sigma-fields. Use this to count all sigma-fields on Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4}: count the partitions with one block, two blocks, three blocks and four blocks, and add them up. You are counting the Bell number B(4).

Hint

For two blocks, the split sizes can be 3+1 or 2+2; count each kind separately. For three blocks, the split sizes must be 2+1+1.

Show the worked solution
  1. One block. The partition {{1, 2, 3, 4}}: 1 way. Its unions give {∅, Ω}, the trivial sigma-field.
  2. Two blocks, sizes 3 and 1. Choosing the lone element determines everything: 4 ways.
  3. Two blocks, sizes 2 and 2. The pairings: {1,2}|{3,4}, {1,3}|{2,4}, {1,4}|{2,3}: 3 ways. (Choosing the first pair determines the second, and each pairing is counted once.) Two-block partitions: 4 + 3 = 7.
  4. Three blocks, sizes 2, 1 and 1. Choosing the pair determines the partition, and there are C(4, 2) = 6 pairs: 6 ways.
  5. Four blocks. All singletons: 1 way, giving the full power set with 16 members.
  6. Total. 1 + 7 + 6 + 1 = 15 sigma-fields on a four-element space. Each is a different answer to "which questions may be asked", and exactly one of them, the 16-member power set, allows every question.

Sanity check. The corresponding member counts across the fifteen partitions are 2 once, 4 seven times, 8 six times and 16 once: 2, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 16. Every one is a power of two, as the census demands.

Traps in this concept

  • Spot-checking instead of checking. Problem 3's candidate (b) passes every union you think of first and dies on one complement. The verdict is only as good as the least convenient check.
  • Forgetting that ∅ comes free but must be present. ∅ = Ωc, so a collection holding Ω under axiom (ii) must hold ∅; a candidate missing either is already dead.
  • Trusting smallness. The trivial sigma-field {∅, Ω} is tiny and legal; Problem 3's six-member candidate is bigger and illegal. Size never decides by itself, though the power-of-two census prunes it fast.
  • Assuming finite closure implies countable closure. Problem 7's finite-cofinite collection is closed under every finite union and still fails the axiom. The word countable is doing real work.
  • Listing intersection closure as an axiom. Problem 6 proves it; adding it as a requirement would be harmless but redundant, and redundancy hides which rules are load-bearing.
  • Generating by hand instead of by atoms. The smallest sigma-field containing some sets is read off the partition their members induce; Problem 5's atom method scales, ad-hoc listing does not.
Your teacher. Take any candidate collection and run the three checks in the order of the flowchart, out loud, including the boring members. The habit being trained is exhaustiveness under time pressure; the axioms are the excuse, not the point.
