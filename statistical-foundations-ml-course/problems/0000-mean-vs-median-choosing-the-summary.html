Problem set 1 · Lecture 1 · after Parts 2 and 3

Choosing between the mean and the median is choosing the question

45 min problem set Seven problems with paper, graded from warm-up to genuinely hard. Attempt each before opening its solution.

How to use this set

  • The recap below is the whole idea in one paragraph and one picture. Read it, answer the two check questions, then close the lesson and work from memory.
  • Each problem states its difficulty honestly. A warm-up that fights back is telling you something worth knowing before the hard ones do.
  • Every solution is worked to the course standard: every symbol named, every step written. Open it only after you have an answer to check against.

The recap

The mean of a dataset is the total divided by the count. It uses the size of every value, which is why one enormous value can move it far away from the crowd: the deck's ten days of spend give a mean of 1,225 even though no single day came close. The median is the middle value of the sorted list, the average of the 5th and 6th of ten. It uses only each value's position, never its size, which is why the same ten days give a median of 415 and why the Rs 8,500 day cannot drag it. Neither summary is the right one. The mean is the only summary that multiplies back to the total; the median is the only one that describes a typical member. Choose the summary by asking what question you are answering, not by habit.

Check the recap

Answer from memory before revealing.

The ten days give a mean of Rs 1,225 and a median of Rs 415. What does that gap itself tell you?
A mean far above the median is the signature of skew: a value far from the crowd inflates the total, and the total is the only thing the mean reads. Option A is backwards, since both summaries were computed on the same ten days. Option C is real in tiny samples but far too small an effect to produce a gap of 810 rupees. Option D reaches for an error that is not needed: one honestly expensive day, the phone-buying day, produces this gap on its own.
Your p95 latency looks healthy but the mean-latency alarm keeps firing. What is the most likely explanation?
The mean answers to magnitude, so a small percentage of very slow calls can double it while the 95th percentile, which reads positions and not sizes, never notices them. Option A is a configuration story and the symptom points at the shape of the data, not at the threshold. Option B would destabilise both summaries equally, not one of them. Option C would make p95 look wrong, and your symptom is that p95 looks right.

The problems paper and pen; open each solution only after committing to an answer

Problem 1warm-up

A support queue resolves five tickets in 12, 18, 15, 140, and 16 minutes. Compute the mean and the median resolution time, then answer: how many of the five tickets took longer than the mean?

Show the worked solution
  1. Add the five times. Running the total in order: 12 + 18 = 30, then 30 + 15 = 45, then 45 + 140 = 185, then 185 + 16 = 201 minutes.
  2. Divide by the count. The mean is 201 ÷ 5 = 40.2 minutes. This figure is derived here rather than quoted from a source, so it carries no keynum marking.
  3. Sort before taking the median. In order the five times are [12, 15, 16, 18, 140]. Five is odd, so the median is the middle, the 3rd value: 16 minutes.
  4. Ask the comparison question. The tickets longer than the mean of 40.2 are exactly one: the 140-minute ticket. The mean is larger than four of the five values it claims to summarise.

Sanity check. The mean must land between 12 and 140, and 40.2 does; the median must land between the 2nd and 4th sorted values, 15 and 18, and 16 does.

Problem 2warm-up

The deck's ten days have a mean of 1,225 and a median of 415. Finance asks two questions: (a) how much cash did this user move in total across the ten-day window, and (b) what does an ordinary day cost? Show that only one of the two summaries can answer question (a), by multiplying each summary by 10 and comparing against the true total of 12,250.

Show the worked solution
  1. Multiply the mean by the count. 1,225 × 10 = 12,250, which is exactly the total the deck's table adds up to. This always works: the mean is defined as the total divided by the count, so multiplying it back by the count rebuilds the total.
  2. Multiply the median by the count. 415 × 10 = 4,150. That is not the total, and it is not anything: the median carries no information about magnitude, so no operation on it can recover one.
  3. Answer the two questions. Question (a), about the total, is a mean question, because the total is the sum of the sizes and the mean is the only summary that reads the sizes. Question (b), about a typical day, is a median question, because "typical" means "like the middle member", and the middle member's own spend was nowhere near 1,225.

Sanity check. 10 × 1,225 reproduces the printed total of 12,250 exactly, while 10 × 415 falls short of it by 8,100, which is close to the outlier's 8,500 minus what the median understates it by.

Problem 3working

Keep the deck's nine ordinary days, 350, 380, 390, 400, 410, 420, 440, 460 and 500, and suppose day 5 is replaced by a single value V. How large must V be for the mean of the ten days to reach Rs 2,000? And what, in that case, happens to the median?

Show the worked solution
  1. Total the nine ordinary days. Running the sum: 350 + 380 = 730, + 390 = 1,120, + 400 = 1,520, + 410 = 1,930, + 420 = 2,350, + 440 = 2,790, + 460 = 3,250, + 500 = 3,750.
  2. State the target total. A mean of 2,000 over ten days needs a total of 10 × 2,000 = 20,000.
  3. Solve for V. V = 20,000 − 3,750 = 16,250. One day would have to carry more than four times the outlier the deck actually shows.
  4. Check the median. With V = 16,250 the sorted list ends [ ..., 500, 16,250], so the 5th and 6th values are still 410 and 420 and the median is still exactly 415. The mean moved by 775 rupees; the median did not move at all.
  5. Read the leverage. Lifting the mean from 1,225 to 2,000 took one value worth about 39 ordinary days. That is what "the mean answers to magnitude" means as a number.

Sanity check. (3,750 + 16,250) ÷ 10 = 2,000 exactly, and the middle pair 410, 420 is untouched because the giant value sorts to the far right either way.

Problem 4working

Two warehouses report delivery delays in minutes. Warehouse A: 5, 7, 6, 8, 6, 7, 5, 6. Warehouse B: 5, 7, 6, 240, 6, 7, 5, 6. Compute the mean and the median for each, then defend a choice of summary for each warehouse separately.

Show the worked solution
  1. Warehouse A, the mean. 5 + 7 + 6 + 8 + 6 + 7 + 5 + 6 = 50, so the mean is 50 ÷ 8 = 6.25 minutes.
  2. Warehouse A, the median. Sorted: [5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8]. The 4th and 5th values are 6 and 6, so the median is 6 minutes. Mean and median nearly agree: this data has no outlier.
  3. Warehouse B, the mean. The total gains 240 − 8 = 232 over A's, giving 282, so the mean is 282 ÷ 8 = 35.25 minutes.
  4. Warehouse B, the median. Sorted: [5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 240]. The middle pair is again 6 and 6, so the median is 6 minutes, unchanged by the 240.
  5. Defend the choices. For A either summary serves, and the mean is the slightly better tool because it uses all eight values and there is nothing to protect against. For B the question "how long does a delivery usually take" is a median question: 6 minutes describes seven of the eight deliveries, while 35.25 describes none of them. If the question were "what fraction of our shipping budget does delay consume", the 240 would matter and the mean would earn its place.

Sanity check. Both A answers must sit between 5 and 8, and 6.25 and 6 do; B's mean must sit between 5 and 240, and 35.25 does, while B's median must sit between the 4th and 5th sorted values, 6 and 6.

Problem 5working

Construct a dataset of five values whose mean is below its median, and explain in one sentence what the direction of the mean-minus-median gap tells you. "The median beats the mean" must not silently become "the median is the smaller one".

Show the worked solution
  1. Put the outlier on the low side. Take the values 1, 90, 91, 92, 93. The total is 1 + 90 + 91 + 92 + 93 = 367.
  2. Compute both summaries. The mean is 367 ÷ 5 = 73.4. Sorted, the list is [1, 90, 91, 92, 93] and the middle value is 91. The mean sits 17.6 below the median.
  3. State what the direction means. A mean below its median is the mirror image of the deck's situation: it says the extreme value is on the low side, dragging the total down. The sign of the gap locates the outlier; its size says how hard the outlier is pulling.
  4. Note what this protects you from. A dashboard that treats "median < mean" as normal and "mean < median" as a fault has the rule backwards in half the cases. The honest rule is about the gap's existence and direction, not about which summary is larger.

Sanity check. The mean of 73.4 must lie between the smallest and largest values, 1 and 93, and above what the five values would average without the 1, namely 91.5; 73.4 clears both tests.

Problem 6genuinely hard

A dataset has 100 values. An adversary may corrupt some of them, setting each corrupted value to anything they like. (a) How many values must the adversary corrupt to move the mean to any target they choose? (b) How many to move the median at all? (c) Express both answers as a share of the dataset. This share is called the breakdown point, and it is the standard way to give the word "robust" a magnitude.

Hint

For (a), write the corrupted value needed to hit a target mean M in terms of the honest total S. For (b), remember that with 100 values the median is the average of the 50th and 51st sorted values, and ask which positions the corrupted values occupy as their count grows.

Show the worked solution
  1. The mean: one value is enough. Let the 99 honest values total S. With one corrupted value v the mean is (S + v) ÷ 100, so to hit any target M the adversary sets v = 100M − S. One corrupted row out of a hundred, a share of 1 percent, moves the mean anywhere.
  2. The median with 49 corrupted values. Send 49 values to +∞. Sorted, the 51 honest values occupy positions 1 to 51 and the corrupted ones occupy 52 to 100. The median is the average of the 50th and 51st values, both honest, so the median does not move at all, whatever the corrupted values are worth.
  3. The median with 50 corrupted values. Now the honest values hold positions 1 to 50 and the corrupted ones hold 51 to 100. Position 50 is the largest honest value and position 51 is +∞, so the median is (honest + ∞)/2: unbounded. The break happens at 50 values, a share of 50 percent.
  4. State the comparison. The breakdown points are 1 percent against 50 percent. Corrupting one row of a hundred moves the mean to any number the adversary likes; corrupting forty-nine rows moves the median by exactly zero. That ratio is what the word "robust" has been pointing at since Part 2.

Sanity check. With 49 corrupted, positions 50 and 51 both hold honest values because 51 honest values fill positions 1 to 51; adding the 50th corrupted value pushes exactly one honest value out of position 51, which is the first position the median reads.

Problem 7working

Seven judges score a routine: 9.8, 5.0, 9.5, 9.9, 9.2, 8.0, 9.7. The competition rule is the trimmed mean: delete the highest and lowest scores, then average the remaining five. Compute the full mean, the trimmed mean, and the median, put the three in order, and explain in two sentences what the trimmed mean trades away.

Show the worked solution
  1. Sort the scores. In order: [5.0, 8.0, 9.2, 9.5, 9.7, 9.8, 9.9]. The trimmed mean will drop 5.0 and 9.9.
  2. Full mean. The total is 5.0 + 8.0 + 9.2 + 9.5 + 9.7 + 9.8 + 9.9 = 61.1, so the mean is 61.1 ÷ 7 = 8.7286, to four decimal places.
  3. Trimmed mean. The remaining five total 8.0 + 9.2 + 9.5 + 9.7 + 9.8 = 46.2, so the trimmed mean is 46.2 ÷ 5 = 9.24.
  4. Median. The middle of seven sorted values is the 4th: 9.5. The order is 8.73, then 9.24, then 9.5.
  5. What the trimmed mean trades away. It buys most of the median's protection, because a single extreme judge can no longer reach the answer. It pays in information and in arbitrariness: two honest scores are discarded by rule even when they are genuine, and the fraction trimmed is a choice somebody made, so a 20 percent trimmed mean and a 10 percent one are different estimators of the same thing.

Sanity check. All three answers lie between 5.0 and 9.9, and because the two dropped scores sat at the two ends, the trimmed mean must land between the mean and the median: 8.73 < 9.24 < 9.5.

Traps in this concept

  • Treating the mean as "the normal day". Before trusting any average, ask whether a single actual member of the dataset sits near it. The deck's mean of 1,225 is higher than nine of the ten days it summarises.
  • Forgetting that the mean owns the totals. Mean × count rebuilds the total exactly; the median multiplied by anything means nothing. Any question about sums, budgets or capacities is a mean question.
  • Reading "median beats mean" as a rule. On clean, symmetric data the two nearly coincide and the mean is the more efficient summary. The median earns its place exactly when a small number of extreme values would otherwise decide the answer.
  • Reading the gap's direction carelessly. Mean above median means a high-side outlier; mean below median means a low-side one. Problem 5 is the mirror image of the deck's example.
  • Deleting the outlier instead of choosing the summary. Dropping the Rs 8,500 day changes the dataset. Choosing the median changes only the summary, and leaves the fraud team free to care about that day as much as they should.
Your teacher. Take one metric you own that is reported as an average, pull the raw values, and compute the median beside the mean. If the two nearly agree, the average was safe. If they do not, you have just learned which question your dashboard has really been answering.
