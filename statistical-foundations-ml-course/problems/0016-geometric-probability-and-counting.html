When outcomes form a continuum, probability is area

working 40 min Eight problems on paper. Grids, number lines and areas, each attacked by complement or by systematic enumeration.

The one-minute version

  • Uniform on a finite space: P = (favourable outcomes) / (total outcomes), and the whole game is counting both sides correctly.
  • Uniform on a region: P = (favourable area, length or volume) / (total), and the whole game is drawing the region.
  • The two workhorses: complement (count the opposite, subtract from 1) and systematic enumeration (fix an order and count without skips or doubles).
  • Geometric answers are ratios of measures, so the units and the total size cancel: only the shape matters.

The concept in one paragraph

When every outcome is equally likely, probability is a ratio of sizes. On a finite space the size of a set is its count, giving the classical formula P = favourable/total. On a continuous region, where individual outcomes have probability zero, the size is a length, an area or a volume, and the same formula survives with measure in place of count: the chance of landing in a subregion is its share of the whole. Two techniques do almost all of the work. Complement solves "at least one" problems by counting the opposite, easier event and subtracting from 1. Systematic enumeration solves counting problems by fixing a disciplined order, first this choice, then that, so that nothing is counted twice and nothing is skipped.

The problems

Problem 1warm-up

Count, with one line of reasoning each: the outcomes of tossing three coins; the outcomes of throwing two dice; the number of five-card hands from a 52-card deck.

Show the worked solution
  1. Three coins. Two outcomes per toss, three independent tosses: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 ordered outcomes.
  2. Two dice. Six per die, independent: 6 × 6 = 36 ordered outcomes. Order matters in the list even when the sum does not care.
  3. Five-card hands. A hand is an unordered selection: C(52, 5) = 52 × 51 × 50 × 49 × 48 / 5! = 311,875,200/120 = 2,598,960.

Sanity check. Ordered counts are products; unordered counts divide the product by the number of orderings. Five cards have 5! = 120 orderings, which is exactly the division made.

Problem 2warm-up

Two fair dice are thrown once. Compute P(at least one die shows a 6) two ways: by systematic enumeration of the favourable outcomes, and by the complement. Confirm both routes give the same fraction.

Show the worked solution
  1. Enumeration. First die 6, second not: 5 outcomes. Second die 6, first not: 5 outcomes. Both 6: 1 outcome. Total 5 + 5 + 1 = 11 favourable of 36, so P = 11/36.
  2. Complement. "No six" means both dice avoid 6: 5 × 5 = 25 outcomes. So P(at least one 6) = 1 - 25/36 = 11/36.
  3. Agreement. 36 - 25 = 11: the complement route is the enumeration route counted from the other end, which is why they must agree.

Sanity check. The enumeration must not count (6,6) twice: the split 5 + 5 + 1 handles the overlap explicitly, which is inclusion-exclusion in counting clothes.

Problem 3steady

Two colleagues agree to meet at noon. Each arrives at a time uniformly distributed between 12:00 and 13:00, independently of the other, and each waits exactly 15 minutes before leaving.

Compute P(they meet) by measuring the square above. State the inequality the band represents and the area of each triangle.

Hint

Let X and Y be the arrival times in minutes past noon. Meeting means |X - Y| ≤ 15. Draw that region inside the 60 by 60 square and measure what is left.

Show the worked solution
  1. The region. The pair of arrival times is a point of the 60 × 60 square, uniformly distributed. They meet exactly when |X - Y| ≤ 15, the diagonal band.
  2. Measure the complement. The two corner triangles are |X - Y| > 15. Each is right-angled with legs 60 - 15 = 45, so each has area 45 × 45/2 = 1,012.5, and together 2,025.
  3. Complement probability. P(miss) = 2,025/3,600 = (45/60)² = (3/4)² = 9/16.
  4. Answer. P(meet) = 1 - 9/16 = 7/16 = 0.4375.

Sanity check. The band must hold more than half the square when the wait exceeds half the hour, and less than half when it is a quarter of the hour: 7/16 is just under half, consistent with a 15-minute wait in a 60-minute window.

Problem 4steady

A point is chosen uniformly at random inside a square of side 2, centred at the origin.

Compute P(the point lies within distance 1 of the centre). Then compute P(the point lies within distance 1 of the centre and has both coordinates positive), and explain the relationship between the two answers without recomputing.

Show the worked solution
  1. First answer. The favourable region is the inscribed circle of radius 1, area π. The square has area 4. P = π/4 ≈ 0.785.
  2. Second answer. Both coordinates positive cuts the plane into quadrants; the quarter of the circle in the first quadrant has area π/4. P = (π/4)/4 = π/16 ≈ 0.196.
  3. The relationship. The four quadrants carve the circle into four congruent quarters, so the second probability is exactly one quarter of the first: π/16 = (π/4)/4. Symmetry gives the ratio for free.

Sanity check. Both answers must be below 1 and the second below the first: 0.785 and 0.196 satisfy both, and 4 × 0.196 = 0.785.

Problem 5steady

A four-digit PIN is generated uniformly at random from 0000 to 9999, leading zeros allowed.

Compute P(no digit repeats) by systematic enumeration: count the total, then build the favourable count digit by digit. Also compute P(all four digits are distinct and the PIN starts with a 9).

Show the worked solution
  1. Total. Four independent positions, ten digits each: 104 = 10,000 equally likely PINs.
  2. Favourable, digit by digit. First digit: 10 choices. Second: any but the first, 9. Third: any but those two, 8. Fourth: 7. Favourable = 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5,040.
  3. First answer. P(no repeats) = 5,040/10,000 = 0.504.
  4. Restricted event. Fix the first digit at 9: the remaining three positions draw distinct digits from the other nine: 9 × 8 × 7 = 504 favourable. P = 504/10,000 = 0.0504.
  5. Cross-check the ratio. Conditioning on a leading 9 should divide by 10, since the leading digit was uniform: 0.504/10 = 0.0504. Agrees.

Sanity check. The product 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 must fall short of 104 because repeats are common: just over half of all PINs have a repeat, which matches 0.504.

Problem 6steady

A rod of length L is broken at a point chosen uniformly at random along its length.

Compute P(the longer piece is more than 3L/4). Then compute P(both pieces are at least L/4), and describe the favourable set of the second event on the same number line.

Show the worked solution
  1. Model. The break point is X, uniform on [0, L]. The two pieces have lengths X and L - X; the longer is max(X, L - X).
  2. First event. max(X, L - X) > 3L/4 holds when X < L/4 or X > 3L/4. The favourable set is two intervals of length L/4 each, so P = (L/2)/L = 1/2.
  3. Second event. Both pieces at least L/4 means X ≥ L/4 and L - X ≥ L/4, that is L/4 ≤ X ≤ 3L/4: the middle interval, length L/2. P = 1/2.
  4. On the line. The second event's favourable set is exactly the complement of the first's. Two events that are complements have probabilities summing to 1, and here 1/2 + 1/2 = 1, visible as the two shaded ends versus the shaded middle.

Sanity check. "The longer piece exceeds 3L/4" and "both pieces are at least L/4" sound different but are exact complements here, since if neither piece is long, neither is short: the two answers must sum to 1, and they do.

Problem 7hard

Five people are in a room. Ignore leap years, take all 365 days equally likely, and take birthdays independent.

Compute P(some two of the five share a birthday) by the complement, keeping the exact fraction structure before converting to a decimal. State the answer to three decimal places.

Hint

The complement is "all five birthdays distinct": 365 choices for the first person, then one fewer for each subsequent person.

Show the worked solution
  1. Complement event. All five distinct: the birthdays run 365, 364, 363, 362, 361 choices, so P(all distinct) = (365 × 364 × 363 × 362 × 361)/3655.
  2. Numerator. 365 × 364 = 132,860; × 363 = 48,228,180; × 362 = 17,458,601,160; × 361 = 6,302,555,018,760.
  3. Denominator. 3655 = 6,478,348,728,125.
  4. Complement value. 6,302,555,018,760/6,478,348,728,125 ≈ 0.9729.
  5. Answer. P(some shared birthday) = 1 - 0.9729 ≈ 0.027, about 2.7 percent.

Sanity check. With 23 people this same calculation famously clears 50 percent; with only 5 people the answer must be small but not tiny, since 5 people make C(5, 2) = 10 pairs each with chance 1/365: 10/365 ≈ 0.027 is the union bound, and the exact answer sits just under it because the pairs overlap. It does.

Problem 8hard

Two numbers X and Y are chosen independently and uniformly at random on the interval [0, 1].

Compute P(|X - Y| ≤ 1/2) by measuring the square. Then compute P(|X - Y| ≤ 1/10) the same way, and state the general formula for P(|X - Y| ≤ d) when 0 ≤ d ≤ 1.

Hint

Work with the complement as the figure does: two right-angled triangles with legs 1 - d.

Show the worked solution
  1. The sample space. The pair (X, Y) is a uniform point on the unit square of area 1.
  2. The complement. |X - Y| > 1/2 is two right triangles with legs 1 - 1/2 = 1/2. Each has area (1/2 × 1/2)/2 = 1/8; together 1/4.
  3. First answer. P(|X - Y| ≤ 1/2) = 1 - 1/4 = 3/4.
  4. Second answer. The same construction with d = 1/10: two triangles of legs 9/10, total area 2 × (9/10)²/2 = 81/100. P = 1 - 81/100 = 19/100 = 0.19.
  5. General formula. P(|X - Y| ≤ d) = 1 - (1 - d)2 = 2d - d2, for 0 ≤ d ≤ 1. At d = 0 it gives 0 and at d = 1 it gives 1, as it must.

Sanity check. For small d the answer should be roughly 2d, the length of the interval Y must fall in: at d = 0.1 the formula gives 0.19, just under the 0.2 that the edge effects trim off.

Traps in this concept

  • Counting unordered things as ordered, or the reverse. Dice pairs are ordered (36 outcomes); card hands are not (C(52, 5)). Decide which the problem describes before counting anything.
  • Double-counting the overlap in "at least one". Problem 2's 5 + 5 + 1 split exists precisely so the (6,6) case is counted once. A naive 6 + 6 = 12 overcounts by one cell.
  • Forgetting the complement when you see "at least". Problems 2 and 7 are both one line by complement and many lines by direct enumeration. The complement is not a trick; it is the shorter proof.
  • Measuring the wrong total. Geometric answers are ratios, and the denominator is the whole region the point is drawn from, not the favourable one. Drawing the total region first prevents this.
  • Assuming independence of X and Y after conditioning. In Problem 3, given that they did not meet, the two arrival times are no longer independent. Complement probabilities of joint events must be computed on the joint space.
Your teacher. The exit test is Problem 3 done cold: draw the 60 by 60 square, name the band |X - Y| ≤ 15, measure two triangles of legs 45, and land on 7/16. If you can run that without the figure, every meeting-style and buffer-time question is the same problem with new numbers.

Where this came from

The classical ratio definition, geometric probability, and the complement and enumeration techniques are the standard toolkit of Sheldon Ross, A First Course in Probability, chapters 1 and 2, which the Lecture 1 slide 21 of Statistical Foundations of Machine Learning (Nikhil Karamchandani and D. Manjunath, IIT Bombay) prescribes as the course canon. Grimmett and Stirzaker's One Thousand Exercises in Probability carries a large supply of problems of exactly this shape for further practice.
