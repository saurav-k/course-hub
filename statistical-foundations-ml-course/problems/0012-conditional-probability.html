Conditioning shrinks the universe, then renormalises it

working 35 min Eight problems on paper. The definition, the direction of the vertical bar, and one problem where the intuitive answer is simply wrong.

The one-minute version

  • P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), defined only when P(B) > 0. Read it: the probability that A happens, given that B happened.
  • The picture is two moves: restrict your attention to the outcomes inside B, then renormalise so they total 1 again.
  • The bar is not symmetric: P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different quantities with different denominators.
  • Conditioning on an event can move a probability a little, a lot, or all the way to 0 or 1.

The concept in one paragraph

Learning that B happened changes the question. The outcomes outside B are now impossible, so the sample space shrinks to B, and probabilities must be scaled up by the factor 1/P(B) so that the shrunk space again totals 1. What remains of A inside the shrunk space is the overlap A ∩ B, which gives the definition P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B): the probability of the part of A that lives inside B, measured relative to the new total P(B) rather than the old total P(Ω) = 1. The lecture proves that this new assignment is itself a valid probability measure, so every tool you own - complements, monotonicity, additivity - works unchanged inside the conditioned world.

The problems

The two-coin space you will reuse throughout: pick one of two coins, C1 fair and C2 always heads, and toss it once. The four outcomes carry probabilities 1/4, 1/4, 1/2, 0 in the order (C1, H), (C1, T), (C2, H), (C2, T).

Problem 1warm-up

Using the ten-point space in the figure: compute P(A | B) and P(B | A) from the definition, and confirm that the two answers differ even though they describe the same overlap.

Show the worked solution
  1. Condition on B. P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B) = (2/10)/(4/10) = 2/4 = 1/2. Inside the four-point world of B, both of B's top points still belong to A.
  2. Condition on A. P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B)/P(A) = (2/10)/(5/10) = 2/5. Inside the five-point world of A, two of the five points lie in B.
  3. Compare. The same overlap, 2 points, divided by two different totals: 4 gives 1/2 and 5 gives 2/5. The denominators differ because the shrunk universes differ.

Sanity check. Both answers must lie between 0 and 1, and P(A | B) = 1/2 happens to equal P(A) = 5/10: knowing B occurred neither helped nor hindered A here.

Problem 2warm-up

In the two-coin space, let A1 = "picked coin 1" and E = "observed tails". The lecture states P(E | A1) = 1/2 and P(E | A2) = 0.

Verify both statements from the definition P(E | Ai) = P(E ∩ Ai)/P(Ai), listing the outcomes of every set involved.

Show the worked solution
  1. List the sets. A1 = {(C1, H), (C1, T)}, A2 = {(C2, H), (C2, T)}, E = {(C1, T), (C2, T)}.
  2. First conditional. E ∩ A1 = {(C1, T)} with probability 1/4, and P(A1) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. So P(E | A1) = (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2. Given the fair coin, tails has probability one half, as it should.
  3. Second conditional. E ∩ A2 = {(C2, T)} with probability 0, and P(A2) = 1/2. So P(E | A2) = 0/(1/2) = 0. The two-headed coin can never show tails, so conditioning on it kills the event.

Sanity check. The two conditionals answer different questions - "tails given the fair coin" and "tails given the loaded coin" - so there is no reason for them to agree, and here they do not.

Problem 3steady

Draw one card from a standard 52-card deck, uniformly. Let A = "the card is an ace" (4 cards), R = "the card is red" (26 cards), H = "the card is a heart" (13 cards), F = "the card's rank is jack or higher, including ace" (16 cards: four each of jack, queen, king, ace), and G = "the card is a face card in the strict sense: jack, queen or king" (12 cards).

Compute P(A | R), P(A | H), P(A | F), and P(A | G). Explain in one sentence each why the first two leave P(A) unchanged, the third triples it, and the fourth destroys it.

Hint

Count the aces inside each conditioning event, then divide by the size of the conditioning event. Everything is counting.

Show the worked solution
  1. P(A) for reference. 4/52 = 1/13 ≈ 0.077.
  2. Condition on red. Red aces: 2. P(A | R) = (2/52)/(26/52) = 2/26 = 1/13. Unchanged: the red half of the deck holds its proportional share of aces.
  3. Condition on hearts. Heart aces: 1. P(A | H) = (1/52)/(13/52) = 1/13. Unchanged again, same reason.
  4. Condition on rank jack or higher. Aces in F: 4, and F holds 16 cards. P(A | F) = (4/52)/(16/52) = 4/16 = 1/4. Tripled: the high-rank world concentrates aces, four of its sixteen cards.
  5. Condition on strict face cards. An ace is not a face card, so A ∩ G = ∅ and P(A | G) = 0. The conditioning event rules A out completely.

Sanity check. Conditioning on a subset can only keep or reshape A's share within that subset: 1/13, 1/13, 1/4, 0 are all of the form (aces inside)/(size of subset), and each is between 0 and 1.

Problem 4steady

Let (Ω, F, P) be a probability space and B an event with P(B) > 0. Define a new assignment on F by PB(A) = P(A | B) for every event A.

Prove that PB is itself a valid probability measure: check PB(∅) = 0, PB(Ω) = 1, and additivity over disjoint events. This is the claim the lecture states and the tutorial proves.

Hint

Write each check as a ratio with the same denominator P(B), and let the axioms for P do the work in each numerator.

Show the worked solution
  1. Empty set. PB(∅) = P(∅ ∩ B)/P(B) = P(∅)/P(B) = 0/P(B) = 0, using P(∅) = 0 for P itself.
  2. Whole space. PB(Ω) = P(Ω ∩ B)/P(B) = P(B)/P(B) = 1, using Ω ∩ B = B. The shrunk universe totals 1, which is the renormalisation in action.
  3. Disjoint additivity. Let A1, A2 be disjoint. Then (A1 ∩ B) and (A2 ∩ B) are disjoint subsets whose union is (A1 ∪ A2) ∩ B. So PB(A1 ∪ A2) = P((A1 ∪ A2) ∩ B)/P(B) = [P(A1 ∩ B) + P(A2 ∩ B)]/P(B) = PB(A1) + PB(A2), using additivity of P in the numerator.
  4. Conclusion. All three axioms hold, so conditioning produces not an ad-hoc formula but a brand new probability space on the same events. Every theorem derived from the axioms, including everything in Set 10, applies verbatim to PB.

Sanity check. The three checks mirror the three axioms one for one, each numerator inherited from P. If any check had needed an assumption beyond P(B) > 0, the claim would be false - and it is not.

Problem 5steady

Derive the conditional complement rule P(Ac | B) = 1 - P(A | B), and use it to compute P(the card is not an ace, given that it is red) in the setup of Problem 3.

Show the worked solution
  1. Split inside the shrunk world. Within B, the events A ∩ B and Ac ∩ B are disjoint and their union is B.
  2. Add them. P(A ∩ B) + P(Ac ∩ B) = P(B), by additivity of P on the disjoint pair.
  3. Divide by P(B). P(A | B) + P(Ac | B) = 1, which rearranges to P(Ac | B) = 1 - P(A | B). Alternatively: Problem 4 showed PB is a measure, and every measure satisfies the complement rule.
  4. Apply it. From Problem 3, P(A | R) = 1/13, so P(not an ace, given red) = 1 - 1/13 = 12/13. Direct count: 24 of the 26 red cards are not aces, and 24/26 = 12/13.

Sanity check. The conditional complement rule is the ordinary complement rule applied inside the shrunk universe, so it needs no new memory: Problem 4 already earned it.

Problem 6hard

A fair coin is tossed three times. Given that at least one toss came up heads, what is the probability that all three tosses came up heads? Compute it from the definition, and explain why the answer is not 1/4, the answer to the different question "given the first toss is a head".

Hint

List the eight outcomes, cross out the ones ruled out by the conditioning event, and count what remains. Then compare which outcomes survive under "first toss is a head" instead.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the events. A = {HHH}, the all-heads outcome. B = at least one head = all eight outcomes except TTT, so B holds 7 outcomes.
  2. Apply the definition. P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B) = (1/8)/(7/8) = 1/7.
  3. Why not 1/4. Conditioning on "the first toss is heads" keeps only {HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT}: four equally likely outcomes of which one is all-heads, giving 1/4. That conditioning event keeps a different, smaller set of survivors than "at least one head".
  4. The lesson. A conditional probability is a ratio of surviving outcomes, and the survivor set depends on exactly how the information arrived. "At least one head" is weaker information than "the first is a head", so it leaves more rivals for A and the answer drops from 1/4 to 1/7.

Sanity check. Conditioning on B, an event that contains A, can only help A or leave it alone, so the answer must be at least the unconditional 1/8: 1/7 ≈ 0.143 clears that, and it stays below the 1/4 that the stronger information gives.

Problem 7hard

Back in the two-coin space, with Ai = "picked coin i" and E = "observed tails".

Compute P(A1 | E) and P(A2 | E) directly from the definition. Explain the answers in plain words, and contrast them with the forward conditionals P(E | A1) = 1/2 and P(E | A2) = 0 from Problem 2: same four numbers in the space, completely different questions.

Show the worked solution
  1. Reverse conditional, first coin. P(A1 | E) = P(A1 ∩ E)/P(E). The overlap is {(C1, T)} with probability 1/4. P(E) = P({(C1, T)}) + P({(C2, T)}) = 1/4 + 0 = 1/4. So P(A1 | E) = (1/4)/(1/4) = 1.
  2. Reverse conditional, second coin. P(A2 | E) = P(A2 ∩ E)/P(E) = 0/(1/4) = 0.
  3. Plain words. Tails came up. Coin 2 cannot produce tails, so the coin in your hand is certainly coin 1. The observation, weak as it looks, pins the cause completely.
  4. Contrast. P(E | A1) = 1/2 asks: if I hold the fair coin, how likely is tails? P(A1 | E) = 1 asks: if I saw tails, which coin do I hold? The two run in opposite directions, and Set 15 turns that reversal into a formula.

Sanity check. The two reverse conditionals sum to 1, as they must: A1 and A2 form a partition, so given E exactly one of them is true.

Problem 8hard

True or false, with a one-line justification each: for events A, B, C in any space where all conditionals are defined,

(a) P(A | B) + P(Ac | B) = 1.    (b) P(A | B) + P(A | Bc) = 1.    (c) P(E | A1) + P(E | A2) = 1 when A1, A2 partition the space.

Test each claim on the two-coin space with E = tails, where the four relevant conditionals are 1/2, 0, 1 and 0 from Problems 2 and 7, before stating your general conclusion.

Show the worked solution
  1. Claim (a): true. By the conditional complement rule of Problem 5, the two terms are complements within the same shrunk universe B. Check: P(Ec | A1) = 1 - 1/2 = 1/2, and indeed 1/2 + 1/2 = 1.
  2. Claim (b): false. The two terms live in different shrunk universes, B and Bc, so nothing forces them to sum to 1. Counterexample: P(E | A1) = 1/2 and P(E | A2) = 0 sum to 1/2, not 1. They are answers to two different questions.
  3. Claim (c): false. The conditioning events differ again, so the sum need not be 1. The same two numbers 1/2 and 0 are the counterexample: A1, A2 do partition the space, yet the conditionals sum to 1/2.
  4. General conclusion. Conditional probabilities may only be mixed and matched when they share the same conditioning event. The reflex: before adding two conditional probabilities, check that the expressions after the bar are identical.

Sanity check. The one identity that does always hold across a partition is the law of total probability, P(E) = P(A1)P(E | A1) + P(A2)P(E | A2) - weighted, not bare. Set 14 makes it a set of its own.

Traps in this concept

  • Reading the bar as symmetric. P(A | B) and P(B | A) share a numerator but never a denominator. Problems 1 and 7 exist to burn this in from both directions.
  • Conditioning on a probability-zero event. P(A | B) is defined only when P(B) > 0. In the two-coin space, P(· | {(C2, T)}) has no meaning.
  • Adding conditionals with different conditioning events. Problem 8 is the trap in its purest form: 1/2 + 0 is not 1, and nothing is wrong.
  • Trusting the intuitive survivor count. In Problem 6 the information "at least one head" leaves 7 rivals, not 4 or 8. Write out the survivor set; never eyeball it.
  • Forgetting that conditioning can create certainty. P(A1 | E) = 1 in Problem 7: an ordinary uncertain event became certain because the conditioning event ruled out every alternative cause.
Your teacher. If Problem 6 did not surprise you, try its sibling cold: a family has two children, and you learn they include at least one girl. What is the chance both are girls? Set 15 settles it formally; for now, list the four birth orders and cross out survivors.
