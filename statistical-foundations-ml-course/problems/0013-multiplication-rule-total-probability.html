Chain the conditionals forward, or split the space and add

working 40 min Eight problems on paper. Draws without replacement that force the chain, and total-probability problems where choosing the partition is the actual work.

The one-minute version

  • The multiplication rule rearranges the definition of conditioning: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B | A).
  • For a sequence, the chain rule multiplies down the path: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A)·P(B | A)·P(C | A ∩ B).
  • The law of total probability runs it in reverse: if B1, B2, ... partition the space, P(A) = Σ P(Bi)·P(A | Bi).
  • Forward problems give you the conditions and ask for the intersection. Total-probability problems give you the pieces and ask for the whole. Choosing the partition is the work.

The concept in one paragraph

The definition of conditional probability, P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), is an identity, and multiplying both sides by P(B) turns it into the multiplication rule: an intersection is a first factor times a conditional second factor. Applied down a sequence it becomes the chain rule, which is exactly how draws without replacement are computed: each factor is the conditional probability of the next draw given everything drawn so far. The law of total probability applies the same identity across a partition: if the partition events Bi slice the space into disjoint pieces that cover it, then A is the disjoint union of its slices A ∩ Bi, and each slice factors through its own conditional. One rule multiplies forward along a path; the other adds up the paths.

The problems

Problem 1warm-up

In the two-coin space (C1 fair, C2 always heads), the lecture computes P(A1) = P(A2) = 1/2, P(E | A1) = 1/2, and P(E | A2) = 0, where E = "observed tails".

Use the multiplication rule to compute P(A1 ∩ E) and P(A2 ∩ E), and verify both against the outcome probabilities 1/4, 1/4, 1/2, 0.

Show the worked solution
  1. Multiply the first path. P(A1 ∩ E) = P(A1) · P(E | A1) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.
  2. Multiply the second path. P(A2 ∩ E) = P(A2) · P(E | A2) = 1/2 × 0 = 0.
  3. Verify. A1 ∩ E = {(C1, T)}, whose probability is 1/4 in the outcome table. A2 ∩ E = {(C2, T)}, whose probability is 0. Both match.

Sanity check. The multiplication rule is the definition of conditioning rearranged, so agreement with the direct table is guaranteed; the point is to see the two descriptions as the same number.

Problem 2warm-up

Draw two cards from a standard 52-card deck without replacement.

Compute the probability that both are aces by the chain rule, naming the conditional probability at each stage, and confirm the result against the counting answer C(4, 2)/C(52, 2).

Show the worked solution
  1. First factor. P(first is an ace) = 4/52.
  2. Second factor. Given an ace is gone, 3 aces remain among 51 cards: P(second is an ace | first is an ace) = 3/51.
  3. Chain them. P(both aces) = (4/52) × (3/51) = 12/2,652 = 1/221.
  4. Confirm by counting. C(4, 2)/C(52, 2) = 6/1,326 = 1/221. The chain and the combinatorial count agree.

Sanity check. The answer must be smaller than 4/52 ≈ 0.077, since the second draw has to succeed too: 1/221 ≈ 0.0045 clears that.

Problem 3steady

Draw three cards from the deck without replacement.

Write the three-factor chain for P(all three are hearts), evaluating each conditional explicitly, and compute the final fraction.

Show the worked solution
  1. First factor. P(first heart) = 13/52.
  2. Second factor. P(second heart | first heart) = 12/51.
  3. Third factor. P(third heart | first two hearts) = 11/50.
  4. Multiply. (13/52) × (12/51) × (11/50) = (1/4) × (12/51) × (11/50) = 1,716/132,600.
  5. Simplify. Divide by 156: 1,716/1,32,600 = 11/850 ≈ 0.0129.

Sanity check. Each factor shrinks the running product, so the answer must sit well below 1/4: 0.0129 does.

Problem 4steady

The lecture computes P(E) for the two-coin space by total probability: the events A1 = "picked coin 1" and A2 = "picked coin 2" partition the space.

Reproduce that computation, quoting the lecture's result, and check it against the direct outcome table. Then explain why the partition {A1, A2} is the natural one for this question.

Show the worked solution
  1. Check the partition. A1 and A2 are disjoint, since one toss cannot come from both coins, and A1 ∪ A2 = Ω, since some coin was picked. A partition, as the law requires.
  2. Apply the law. P(E) = P(A1)·P(E | A1) + P(A2)·P(E | A2) = (1/2)(1/2) + (1/2)(0) = 1/4 + 0 = 1/4, the lecture's figure.
  3. Check against the table. E = {(C1, T), (C2, T)} with probabilities 1/4 and 0, totalling 1/4. Agrees.
  4. Why this partition. The conditional probabilities P(E | Ai) are the quantities you actually know - how each coin behaves - and the law assembles the whole from exactly those pieces. The partition is chosen so that the conditionals are the easy, known numbers.

Sanity check. The two slices A ∩ A1 (probability 1/4) and A ∩ A2 (probability 0) are disjoint and exhaust E, so their sum must be P(E) = 1/4.

Problem 5steady

A plant runs two production lines. Line 1 makes 60 percent of the output and 2 percent of its items are defective; line 2 makes the other 40 percent and 5 percent of its items are defective. One item is drawn at random from the day's output.

Identify the natural partition, compute P(the item is defective) by the law of total probability, and state what fraction of defective items comes from line 2.

Hint

The partition should make the conditional probabilities P(defective | cell) the numbers you were given. Then each slice of "defective" is an output share times a defect rate.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the partition. L1 = "made on line 1" with P(L1) = 0.6, L2 = "made on line 2" with P(L2) = 0.4. Disjoint, exhaustive: a partition.
  2. Apply the law. P(D) = P(L1)P(D | L1) + P(L2)P(D | L2) = 0.6 × 0.02 + 0.4 × 0.05.
  3. Compute. 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032.
  4. Line 2's share of defects. The slice from line 2 is 0.020 of the total 0.032, so 0.020/0.032 = 5/8 of all defective items come from line 2, even though line 2 makes only 40 percent of output.

Sanity check. P(D) must lie between the two conditional rates 0.02 and 0.05, weighted towards the line with more output: 0.032 sits between them, closer to 0.02.

Problem 6steady

Using the tree above: compute P(both tosses are heads) by the law of total probability with the partition {fair, two-headed}, and verify each path probability by the chain rule. Keep the answer as a fraction.

Show the worked solution
  1. Partition. F = "picked the fair coin" with P(F) = 4/5, T = "picked the two-headed coin" with P(T) = 1/5.
  2. Conditionals. P(HH | F) = (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4 by the chain rule, since the two tosses of a fair coin are independent stages. P(HH | T) = 1 × 1 = 1: the two-headed coin shows heads every time.
  3. Total probability. P(HH) = (4/5)(1/4) + (1/5)(1) = 1/5 + 1/5 = 2/5.
  4. Path check. Fair path: (4/5) × (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/5. Loaded path: (1/5) × 1 × 1 = 1/5. Sum 2/5, matching.

Sanity check. P(HH) = 2/5 must exceed P(pick fair) × something small plus the loaded contribution of 1/5, and indeed 2/5 is exactly double the loaded coin's guaranteed 1/5.

Problem 7hard

Using the tree: compute P(exactly 2 of the 3 balls drawn are red) by the chain rule on each path plus the law of total probability across paths. Then verify the answer by counting unordered hands: C(5, 2)·C(3, 1)/C(8, 3).

Show the worked solution
  1. Path RRB. (5/8) × (4/7) × (3/6) = 60/336 = 5/28.
  2. Path RBR. (5/8) × (3/7) × (4/6) = 60/336 = 5/28. The second factor dropped to 3/7 because a blue left, and the third rose to 4/6 because only one red had left.
  3. Path BRR. (3/8) × (5/7) × (4/6) = 60/336 = 5/28.
  4. Add the disjoint paths. P(exactly 2 red) = 5/28 + 5/28 + 5/28 = 15/28 ≈ 0.536.
  5. Verify by counting. Choose which 2 of the 5 reds and which 1 of the 3 blues: C(5, 2) × C(3, 1) = 30 favoured hands out of C(8, 3) = 56 equally likely hands: 30/56 = 15/28. The chain and the count agree.

Sanity check. The eight leaf probabilities must total 1. In numerators over the common denominator 8 × 7 × 6 = 336: 60 + 60 + 60 + 30 + 60 + 30 + 30 + 6 = 336, so the tree closes. And 15/28 ≈ 0.536 sits between 0 and 1, a little above even, as expected when reds outnumber blues.

Problem 8hard

A deployment must pass three stages in order. Stage 1 passes with probability 0.9. Given stage 1 passed, stage 2 passes with probability 0.8. Given both passed, stage 3 passes with probability 0.7. All the conditionals are the honest ones: each is measured on the runs that reached that stage.

Compute P(the deployment ships, meaning all three stages pass) by the chain rule. Then compute P(the run reaches stage 3), and explain why the second number is not 0.8.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the events. A = stage 1 passes, B = stage 2 passes, C = stage 3 passes. Shipping is A ∩ B ∩ C.
  2. Chain rule. P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) · P(B | A) · P(C | A ∩ B) = 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.7.
  3. Compute. 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72; 0.72 × 0.7 = 0.504.
  4. Reaching stage 3. The run reaches stage 3 exactly when stages 1 and 2 both passed: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A) = 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72.
  5. Why not 0.8. The 0.8 is P(B | A), a conditional: it applies only to the 90 percent of runs that cleared stage 1. Unconditionally, stage 2 is cleared by 0.72 of all runs, and the chain rule is what converts the conditional into the joint.

Sanity check. Shipping probability 0.504 must be below the reach-stage-3 probability 0.72, since stage 3 can still fail: it is.

Traps in this concept

  • Multiplying conditionals that do not chain. P(B | A) may only follow P(A) if the conditioning matches: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) needs P(C | A ∩ B), not P(C | B) and certainly not P(C).
  • Forgetting the denominator shrank. In draws without replacement, each factor's urn is smaller and its composition changed. Writing 4/52 twice for two aces is the classic error: the second factor is 3/51.
  • Choosing a partition that hides the given numbers. The right partition makes the conditionals P(A | Bi) the values you actually know. If your conditionals are not the given rates, the partition is wrong.
  • Adding paths that overlap. The law of total probability adds the slices A ∩ Bi because they are disjoint. Paths of a tree are always disjoint; ad-hoc cases often are not. Check disjointness before adding.
  • Reporting a conditional as if it were unconditional. Problem 8's 0.8 is a property of the runs that reached stage 2. Quoting it as a share of all runs overstates the pipeline by exactly the factor the chain rule exists to supply.
Your teacher. The exit test is Problem 7 done cold: full tree, three rust paths, 15/28, and the counting cross-check. If the tree and the C(5, 2)C(3, 1)/C(8, 3) count agree, you own both of the subject's workhorses, the chain and the partition, in one problem.
