A bound you can trust beats an exact answer you cannot get

working 35 min Seven problems on paper. Learn when the union bound is useless, when it is enough, and what each Bonferroni step buys.

The one-minute version

  • The union bound says P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ An) ≤ ΣP(Ai). It needs no information about overlaps.
  • Odd truncations of inclusion-exclusion are upper bounds; even truncations are lower bounds. That is the Bonferroni family.
  • A bound above 1 tells you nothing; a bound below your threshold settles the question. Judging which case you are in is the skill.
  • The lecture's hundred-balls-twenty-bins calculation squeezes the answer into [.1134, .118] with two terms and [.1134, .1135] with three.

The concept in one paragraph

Inclusion-exclusion is exact but demands every intersection. The Bonferroni inequalities are what you get when you stop the alternating sum early: after the singles you have an upper bound, after subtracting the pairs a lower bound, after adding the triples an upper bound again, and each extra layer costs C(n, k) more terms but shrinks the gap. The union bound is the first of these and the cheapest to use: the probability that at least one of many things happens is at most the sum of their individual probabilities, whatever the overlaps. The lecture uses exactly this ladder to pin down the chance that some bin is empty when 100 balls are thrown into 20 bins, where the exact answer needs twenty layers and the first two already give a tight bracket.

The problems

Problem 1warm-up

Three independent-ish services have daily failure probabilities 0.010, 0.020 and 0.015. You care whether at least one fails tomorrow.

Give the union bound on P(at least one failure), and state why the bound is usable here even though you know nothing about how the failures overlap.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the events. F1, F2, F3 are the three failure events, and "at least one fails" is F1 ∪ F2 ∪ F3.
  2. Apply the union bound. P(F1 ∪ F2 ∪ F3) ≤ 0.010 + 0.020 + 0.015 = 0.045.
  3. Say why it is usable. The bound is a number below 1, so it genuinely constrains the answer: whatever the overlaps, the chance of any failure tomorrow is at most 4.5 percent. No intersection probabilities were needed.

Sanity check. The bound must be at least the largest single probability, 0.020: 0.045 clears that.

Problem 2warm-up

Twenty services each fail on a given day with probability 0.1.

Apply the union bound, explain why the result is useless, and state the one non-trivial fact it does still give you.

Show the worked solution
  1. Apply the bound. P(at least one failure) ≤ 20 × 0.1 = 2.0.
  2. Say why it is useless. No probability can exceed 1, so a bound of 2.0 rules nothing out: the answer could still be anything from 0.1 up to 1.
  3. The one non-trivial fact. The bound does certify that the twenty events cannot all be disjoint, since twenty disjoint events of probability 0.1 would need total probability 2.0 inside a space of total mass 1. The overlaps must carry at least 1.0 of double-counting. The union bound is useless as an estimate here, but the gap between 2.0 and the maximum possible 1 is hard evidence of overlap.

Sanity check. Whenever ΣP(Ai) > 1, the bound is vacuous as a bound but certifies overlap of at least ΣP(Ai) - 1.

Problem 3steady

Three events A, B, C are mutually disjoint, with P(A) = 0.2, P(B) = 0.3, P(C) = 0.1.

Compute P(A ∪ B ∪ C) exactly, and compare it with the union bound and with the second Bonferroni lower bound. State the general lesson about when each is tight.

Show the worked solution
  1. Exact by disjointness. The additivity axiom applies directly: P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.6.
  2. Union bound. 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.6. The upper bound equals the answer: it is tight, because there is no overlap to double-count.
  3. Second Bonferroni lower bound. ΣP(Ai) - Σi<jP(Ai ∩ Aj) = 0.6 - (0 + 0 + 0) = 0.6. Also tight: every pairwise intersection is empty.
  4. Lesson. For disjoint events both bounds are exact, and the whole Bonferroni ladder collapses to the single additivity axiom. The bounds only become loose, and only gradually useful, when overlaps appear.

Sanity check. Disjoint probabilities may be added by axiom, so 0.6 is not an estimate: it is the definition doing its job.

Problem 4steady

Two events: P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.6, P(A ∩ B) = 0.3.

Show that the union bound is useless here but the second Bonferroni inequality is exact, and explain why the second inequality had to be exact for two events.

Show the worked solution
  1. Union bound. P(A ∪ B) ≤ 0.5 + 0.6 = 1.1. A bound of 1.1 exceeds 1 and rules nothing out: useless.
  2. Second Bonferroni. P(A ∪ B) ≥ 0.5 + 0.6 - 0.3 = 0.8.
  3. The upper side. Any probability is at most 1, so 0.8 ≤ P(A ∪ B) ≤ 1 from general principles. But the addition rule gives the exact value: P(A ∪ B) = 0.5 + 0.6 - 0.3 = 0.8.
  4. Why exact. For two events the second Bonferroni lower bound and the addition rule are the same expression. The ladder stops being an inequality and becomes an equality at n = 2, because there is nothing left to alternate: the full inclusion-exclusion for two events is exactly singles minus the pair.

Sanity check. The overlap 0.3 is consistent with its own bounds: it must lie between P(A) + P(B) - 1 = 0.1 and min(0.5, 0.6) = 0.5, and 0.3 sits inside.

Problem 5steady

Throw 3 balls independently and uniformly into 3 bins. Let Ei be "bin i is empty". The lecture computes P(Ei) = 8/27 for each bin, P(Ei ∩ Ej) = 1/27 for each pair, and P(E1 ∩ E2 ∩ E3) = 0.

Compute the union bound and the second Bonferroni bound on P(at least one empty bin). Compare the lower bound with the exact answer the lecture gets, 21/27, and explain the coincidence.

Hint

The second Bonferroni bound is the two-layer truncation of inclusion-exclusion. What does the third layer contribute here?

Show the worked solution
  1. Union bound. P(E1 ∪ E2 ∪ E3) ≤ 3 × 8/27 = 24/27 = 8/9 ≈ 0.889.
  2. Second Bonferroni. P(union) ≥ 24/27 - 3 × 1/27 = 21/27 = 7/9 ≈ 0.778.
  3. Compare with exact. The lecture's full inclusion-exclusion gives 24/27 - 3/27 + 0 = 21/27, the same number. The lower bound is exact here.
  4. Why. The layer the bound neglects is the triple intersection, and all three bins cannot be empty when there are balls to place: its probability is 0. A truncation is exact whenever every neglected layer is zero, which is why the second bound already closes the case for 3 balls and 3 bins.

Sanity check. 7/9 ≈ 0.778 sits between the largest single event 8/27 ≈ 0.296 and the upper bound 8/9, as it must.

Problem 6hard

The lecture's headline calculation: throw 100 balls independently and uniformly into 20 bins, and let Ai be "bin i is empty", for i = 1 to 20.

Derive P(Ai) and P(Ai ∩ Aj) from the way the balls fall. Compute the union bound and the second Bonferroni bound, reproducing the lecture's figures of about .118 and about .1134. State what the two bounds together tell you that neither gives alone.

Hint

For one bin to be empty, each of the 100 balls must land in one of the other 19 bins, independently each time. For two named bins to be empty, each ball must land in one of the other 18.

Show the worked solution
  1. Single bin empty. Each ball avoids bin i with probability 19/20, independently across the 100 balls, so P(Ai) = (19/20)100 ≈ 0.00592.
  2. Union bound, upper side. P(at least one empty bin) = P(A1 ∪ ... ∪ A20) ≤ 20 × (19/20)100 ≈ 20 × 0.00592 = 0.118, the lecture's figure.
  3. Two named bins empty. Each ball avoids both with probability 18/20, so P(Ai ∩ Aj) = (18/20)100 ≈ 0.0000266.
  4. Second Bonferroni, lower side. The pair sum has C(20, 2) = 190 terms, so the bound is 0.118 - 190 × (18/20)100 ≈ 0.11841 - 0.00505 = 0.1134, matching the lecture's corrected figure.
  5. What the pair gives. Neither bound alone pins the answer: 0.118 is only a ceiling and 0.1134 only a floor. Together they say the true probability lies in [0.1134, 0.118], an interval of width about 0.005, which is already a precise statement about a quantity whose exact form needs twenty inclusion-exclusion layers.

Sanity check. Both bounds sit well below 1 and above the largest single event 0.0059, and the lower bound is below the upper: all three structural tests pass.

Problem 7hard

Continuing Problem 6: compute the third Bonferroni upper bound, using C(20, 3) × (17/20)100 for the triple layer. Verify that the bracket has essentially closed, and explain the pattern of the alternating partial sums: which truncations are upper bounds, which are lower bounds, and why each extra layer shrinks the gap so fast here.

Show the worked solution
  1. Triple layer. Three named bins empty forces every ball into one of the remaining 17 bins: P(Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak) = (17/20)100 ≈ 0.0000000875. The layer sum is C(20, 3) × (17/20)100 = 1140 × 0.0000000875 ≈ 0.0001.
  2. Third Bonferroni upper bound. 0.11841 - 0.00505 + 0.00010 = 0.11346, against the second-order lower bound of 0.11337. The bracket is [0.1134, 0.1135] to four decimal places.
  3. Alternation pattern. Partial sums with an odd number of layers (singles, singles-minus-pairs-plus-triples) are upper bounds; partial sums with an even number are lower bounds. The sequence zigzags down towards the true value from above and below.
  4. Why so fast here. Each layer's common factor is ((20 - k)/20)100, and raising to the 100th power is merciless: 0.95100 ≈ 0.0059, 0.9100 ≈ 0.0000266, 0.85100 ≈ 0.0000000875. Each layer is roughly one two-hundredth of the one before, so the first neglected layer is already far below the resolution anyone cares about.

Sanity check. The true answer must lie between 0.11337 and 0.11346, and the fourth layer would move the answer by at most about C(20,4) × 0.8100 ≈ 0.000001, which cannot disturb the fourth decimal place.

Traps in this concept

  • Using a vacuous bound. A union bound above 1 is not a small upper bound; it is no information. Problem 2 is the reflex to build: check the sum against 1 before quoting the bound.
  • Forgetting which side a truncation bounds. Odd layer count: upper. Even: lower. The zigzag picture in Problem 7 is the memory hook, not a table to memorise.
  • Subtracting the pairs from the wrong running total. The second Bonferroni bound subtracts the C(n, 2) pairwise terms from the sum of singles. With 20 events that is 190 terms, not 20: the term count is the arithmetic risk.
  • Assuming the bound needs independence. Nothing on this page uses independence. The union bound and every Bonferroni inequality hold for arbitrary dependence between the events.
  • Stopping at the bound when the exact answer is cheap. For 3 balls and 3 bins, Problem 5 shows the second bound is already exact. Check whether a neglected layer is zero before paying for more layers.
Your teacher. The exit test for this set is Problem 6 done cold: name P(Ai) = (19/20)100, count the 190 pairs, and land on the lecture's two numbers. That one calculation is the template for every "at least one of many failures" question you will ever care about, in exams and in production reviews alike.
