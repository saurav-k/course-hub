Problem set 3 · Lecture 1 · after Part 4

One dial in, one risk out: the exponential worked both directions

50 min working Seven problems on fitting the rate and reading risks back off it, with paper. Attempt each before opening its solution.

How to use this set

  • The recap is one paragraph and one picture. Answer the two check questions, then work from memory.
  • Two moves dominate this set: fit λ from a baseline, and read P(X > t) = e−λt back off the fitted curve. Every problem is one or both.
  • Check every answer against the sanity line at the foot of its solution before moving on. A wrong rate silently corrupts every risk read off it.

The recap

The exponential model describes a positive quantity with a density f(x) = λe−λx, where x is a possible daily spend and λ ("lambda") is the rate: the one dial that fixes the whole curve. A bigger λ makes the curve fall faster, so big days get rarer. Because the model has one dial, one trusted summary sets it: the deck uses the median m, for which m = ln(2)/λ, so λ = 0.693/415 ≈ 0.00167 per rupee. Risks then come free: the probability of exceeding any level t is e−λt, so the chance of a day above Rs 2,000 is e−3.34 ≈ 0.035. Fit the dial from a robust baseline, read every risk off the curve the dial sets, and say out loud that the shape itself was assumed, not measured.

Check the recap

Answer from memory before revealing.

For an exponential the median is ln(2)/λ. If the median doubles, what happens to λ?
λ = ln(2)/m puts the median in the denominator, so doubling m halves λ. That is the right direction physically: a dearer typical day means a flatter curve and a fatter tail, and a smaller λ is exactly what flattens the curve. Direct proportion would need m on top, not underneath. The logarithm acts on the constant 2 and never on the median. And λ cannot stay fixed, because the median is the one measurement the dial is set from.
The deck fits λ from the median of ten observed days. What do those ten days establish about the exponential shape itself?
The exponential family was chosen before the data was consulted, and the data's only job was setting λ through the median; ten observations cannot identify a distribution family at all. Option B overstates the evidence enormously. Option C has it backwards: the rate is the part that was estimated and the family is the part that was assumed. Option D is wrong because the median of 415 was computed from the ten days, so it is the one measured ingredient.

The problems paper and pen; open each solution only after committing to an answer

Problem 1warm-up

Job durations on a build farm are modelled as exponential with rate λ = 0.002 per minute. What is the median duration, in minutes?

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the relationship. For an exponential, the median m satisfies e−λm = 0.5, and taking logarithms gives m = ln(2)/λ.
  2. Substitute. m = 0.693 ÷ 0.002 = 346.5 minutes, which is about 5 hours 47 minutes.
  3. Check the units. λ is per minute, so 1/λ is minutes, and the median inherits those units. A rate without its units is a number without a meaning.

Sanity check. The median must equal ln(2) ≈ 0.693 mean durations, and the mean is 1/λ = 500 minutes, so 0.693 × 500 = 346.5.

Problem 2warm-up

With the same rate, λ = 0.002 per minute, what is the probability that a job runs longer than 1,000 minutes?

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the tail formula. For an exponential, P(X > t) = e−λt: the whole area to the right of t collapses to one term, which is why the model is so convenient.
  2. Form the exponent. λt = 0.002 × 1,000 = 2.
  3. Evaluate. P(X > 1,000) = e−2 ≈ 0.135, about 13.5 percent.

Sanity check. The threshold 1,000 is about 2.9 medians, so the answer must be well under one half; 0.135 clears that comfortably.

Problem 3working

A monitoring tool reports that the median API response is 50 ms, and you adopt the exponential model. (a) Fit λ. (b) Without a calculator beyond e ≈ 2.718, find P(X > 200 ms) exactly as a power of 2. (c) Generalise: what is P(X > k × median) for any whole number k?

Hint

Write the threshold 200 as a multiple of the median before touching λ. The exponent λt then becomes a multiple of λm, and λm = ln(2).

Show the worked solution
  1. Fit the rate. λ = ln(2)/m = 0.693 ÷ 50 = 0.01386 per ms.
  2. Express the threshold through the median. 200 = 4 × 50, so λ × 200 = 4 × (λ × 50) = 4 × ln(2) = ln(24).
  3. Read the tail. P(X > 200) = e−4 ln 2 = 2−4 = 1/16 = 0.0625, exactly.
  4. Generalise. P(X > k × median) = 2−k: past twice the median a quarter of requests remain, past three times an eighth, past four times a sixteenth. Each additional median halves the survivors. This doubling ladder is worth deriving once and reusing forever.

Sanity check. The ladder must land at one half for k = 1, and 2−1 = 0.5 is exactly the definition of the median.

Problem 4working

Under the model of Problem 3, a request has already been running for 100 ms. What is the probability that it takes more than 200 ms in total? Derive the answer from the tail formula first, then observe what the answer says about the model's most famous property.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the conditional probability. P(X > 200 | X > 100) = P(X > 200) ÷ P(X > 100), because the event X > 200 sits inside the event X > 100.
  2. Use the ladder. P(X > 200) = 2−4 and P(X > 100) = 2−2, so the ratio is 2−4/2−2 = 2−2 = 1/4.
  3. Recognise the property. One quarter is exactly P(X > 100), the probability a fresh request runs past 100 ms. The 100 ms already spent do not matter: given survival so far, the future looks like a fresh start. This is the memoryless property, and the exponential is the only continuous distribution that has it.
  4. Say what it costs. Memorylessness is also a modelling claim: it says a request that has crawled for 100 ms is not more likely to finish soon. For cold starts that may be exactly right; for jobs that fail progressively it is wrong, and the tail risk would be understated.

Sanity check. Conditioning on "already past 100 ms" must enlarge the chance of "past 200 ms" relative to its unconditional value: 1/4 is indeed larger than 1/16, and it stays below 1/2, the chance of clearing the 100 ms mark already granted.

Problem 5working

A payment path has median latency 300 ms. The policy approves the design if the chance of a request exceeding 1,500 ms is below 10 percent. Two analysts fit the exponential separately: analyst A fits on the median, 300 ms; analyst B fits on the mean of the logged latencies, which a minority of pathological requests has inflated to 900 ms. Compute both tail probabilities and state each analyst's recommendation.

Hint

Analyst A's threshold is 5 medians, so the doubling ladder applies directly. Analyst B's mean gives λ = 1/mean, because the exponential's mean is 1/λ.

Show the worked solution
  1. Analyst A, the rate. λA = 0.693 ÷ 300 = 0.00231 per ms.
  2. Analyst A, the risk. 1,500 = 5 × 300, so P(X > 1,500) = 2−5 = 0.031, about 3.1 percent. Below the 10 percent limit: approve.
  3. Analyst B, the rate. For an exponential the mean is 1/λ, so λB = 1 ÷ 900 ≈ 0.00111 per ms.
  4. Analyst B, the risk. λB × 1,500 = 1,500/900 = 1.667, so P(X > 1,500) = e−1.667 ≈ 0.189, about 18.9 percent. Above the limit: decline.
  5. Read the disagreement. The two fits disagree by a factor of six on the tail risk, and the decision flips, because the mean let the pathological minority write itself into the rate. Fitting on a magnitude-sensitive summary imports the outliers into the dial; fitting on the median does not.

Sanity check. The two risks must stand in the ratio e raised to the difference of their exponents: e3.465 − 1.667 = e1.8 ≈ 6, and 0.189 ÷ 0.031 ≈ 6, which agrees.

Problem 6genuinely hard

The deck's fitted model puts P(a day above Rs 2,000) at 0.035, and the credit policy approves because 3.5 percent sits under the 5 percent limit. But the limit is granted for a whole month of independent days, not for one. (a) What is the probability that both of the next two days exceed Rs 2,000? (b) What is the probability that at least one of them does? (c) What does the answer suggest about the policy, and which assumption is carrying the extra weight?

Show the worked solution
  1. Both days, using independence. For independent days the probabilities multiply: 0.035 × 0.035 = 0.001225, about 0.12 percent. Two bad days in a row is very rare.
  2. At least one, by the complement. P(neither exceeds) = 0.965 × 0.965 = 0.931225, so P(at least one exceeds) = 1 − 0.931225 = 0.0688, about 6.9 percent.
  3. Compare with the policy. A single day clears the 5 percent bar, but the chance of seeing at least one bad day in a two-day window is 6.9 percent, above it. Over a month of about 30 independent days the probability of at least one spike is 1 − 0.96530 ≈ 0.65: two chances in three.
  4. What carries the weight. The multiplication in step (a) used independence, an assumption the ten-day sample never established: spending days could be correlated by payday, by the user's own cycles. The single-day policy number is honest; extending it to a month silently adds both independence and a question the policy never stated.

Sanity check. The four two-day outcomes must have probabilities summing to one: 0.931225 + 2 × 0.033775 + 0.001225 = 1 exactly, and "at least one" is the three non-calm cells together, 0.068775.

Problem 7working

The deck's fitted rate is λ = 0.00167 per rupee. (a) What mean daily spend does the fitted model imply? (b) The observed ten days had a mean of 1,225 and a median of 415. Why can no single λ match both, and what does that tell you about the fit?

Show the worked solution
  1. The model's mean. For an exponential the mean is 1/λ, so the fitted model implies a mean of 1 ÷ 0.00167 ≈ 599 rupees.
  2. Compare the three numbers. The model's mean, 599, sits between the observed median, 415, and the observed mean, 1,225. It matches neither.
  3. Why both cannot be matched. The exponential has one dial. Fitting it to the median forces its mean to be 415/0.693 ≈ 599; fitting it to the mean of 1,225 would force its median to be 0.693 × 1,225 ≈ 849. No member of the family carries mean 1,225 and median 415 at once.
  4. What the fit is really saying. The gap between the observed mean and median is the signature of the heavy outlier. The exponential's own mean-to-median ratio is fixed at 1/0.693 ≈ 1.44, while the data's ratio is 1,225/415 ≈ 2.95: the real data is more outlier-prone than any exponential can be. The deck chose to fit the robust summary and let the model understate the tail; that choice should be said aloud, and Part 4's callout works out what fitting on the mean would have done to the decision.

Sanity check. Every exponential has mean/median = 1/ln(2) ≈ 1.44, so a dataset whose ratio is far from 1.44 cannot be exponential, whatever dial it is fitted with.

Traps in this concept

  • Quoting λ without units. 0.00167 per rupee, 0.01386 per millisecond. A rate's units are what make 1/λ a duration or a price, and dropping them is how unit errors survive every sanity check.
  • Fitting on the mean when outliers are present. The mean imports the extremes into λ, and Problem 5 shows the decision flipping as a result. Fit on the median unless there is a specific reason not to.
  • Reading the tail as a height. f(x) is a density; P(X > t) is an area. The tail formula e−λt is the area, and it is the only thing with a direct meaning.
  • Extending a one-day risk to a window without saying so. Problem 6: 3.5 percent a day is 6.9 percent over two days and about 65 percent over a month, and the multiplication assumes independence the data never established.
  • Forgetting which part was measured. The median came from the data; the exponential shape did not. Any sentence of the form "the data shows spending is exponential" is false, and the honest version costs one clause.
Where this set is deliberately looseProblems 5 and 6 hand you a mean and an independence assumption without the raw data a real analyst would demand. That is faithful to the lecture, which does the same, and it is exactly the kind of shortcut this course keeps flagging: the fitted model is a tool for reasoning, not a finding about the user.
Your teacher. Take one latency or spend distribution you own, compute its mean-to-median ratio, and compare it with 1.44. Ratios near 1.44 are the cases where the exponential fit is honest; ratios far from it are the cases where the fit will understate or overstate the tail, and you now know which direction.
