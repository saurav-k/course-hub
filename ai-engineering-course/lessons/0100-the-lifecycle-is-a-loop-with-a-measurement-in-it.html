Module 01 · Foundations · Lesson 0100

You have no training step, so you version everything else

foundation 10 min Framed against the two papers that established what makes a machine-learning system different from ordinary software. · Hidden Technical Debt

The one-minute version

  • You borrowed a lifecycle from classical machine learning: collect data, train, deploy, monitor. You have no training step, so three of its four stages do not apply to you.
  • What changes weekly in your system is the corpus, the prompt, the model id and the eval set. Those four are the artefacts, and they are the things to version.
  • The loop is only a loop if a measurement closes it. Without one you have a sequence of edits and a feeling.
  • Change one artefact between two measurements. Change three and the regression has three candidate causes and no owner, which is the Tuesday nobody enjoys.
  • The output of this page is a one-page charter in your own repository, and it is the capstone's first artefact.

1. Why the borrowed shape misleads

Ask an engineer to draw the lifecycle of an AI product and most will draw the classical one: gather a dataset, train a model, deploy it, watch it drift, retrain. It is a good picture of a system whose behaviour lives in weights that somebody fits. Yours does not. You call a model that somebody else trained, at a version you selected from a list, and you will not fit anything.

Take the training box out and the remaining picture is misleading rather than merely incomplete. Drift, in the classical loop, is the world moving away from the training distribution, and the response is to retrain. Nothing you own can retrain. When your answers get worse, one of four things you control has changed, or the provider shipped a new model version underneath you, and the response is to find out which. That is a different discipline with a different cadence: not a quarterly retraining schedule but a change, a measurement and a decision, several times a week.

2. The four artefacts

Sculley and colleagues made the argument that stuck: the interesting risk in a machine-learning system is not the modelling, it is everything the model is wired into. Their paper names eight kinds of debt, and the abstract lists them plainly: boundary erosion, entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers, data dependencies, configuration issues, changes in the external world, and system-level anti-patterns. Read that list as a retrieval checklist and every entry has an exact analogue in the system you are about to build.

Entanglement is what happens when you cannot change the chunk size without changing every score. Data dependencies are the documents somebody else maintains and re-exports on a schedule you do not control. Undeclared consumers are the three teams who found your endpoint and now depend on the exact wording of its answers. Changes in the external world are a provider deprecating the model id you pinned.

Amershi and colleagues studied the same problem inside a large software company and reported three ways machine learning differs from prior software engineering, and the first of them is the one that matters here: discovering, managing and versioning the data is much more complex and difficult than it is in other kinds of software engineering. Their case study describes a nine-stage workflow; this course does not name the nine stages, because the publication page does not enumerate them and nobody on this course has opened the paper's own text.

3. One change between two measurements

Here is the arithmetic that turns a preference into a rule. Suppose you are holding three corpus snapshots, two model ids and two versions of your eval set, and you are working on the prompt. Every distinct combination of those is a system whose behaviour could differ, and the count is the product of the four. With five prompt versions in flight that is 60 distinct systems, and the number your dashboard shows belongs to exactly one of them.

The trap is not the size of the number, it is its growth. Adding one more prompt version adds twelve systems, not one, because it multiplies against everything else. So the discipline is not "keep good notes"; it is to hold three of the four still while you move the fourth, and to re-measure at each step. A regression you can attribute in one minute and a regression you cannot attribute at all differ only in whether somebody obeyed that rule two days earlier.

4. What one iteration actually is

An iteration in this course has five moves and finishes in the repository rather than in a conversation. You state what you expect to move and by roughly how much, before the run. You change exactly one artefact. You re-run the eval set. You compare against the recorded baseline. You either ship the change or revert it, and you write down which and why.

The fourth move is the one worth defending. A comparison needs a baseline, and a baseline is only a baseline if it lives somewhere a colleague can find in eighteen months. A number in a notebook cell is a number nobody will ever argue with, which is another way of saying it is not evidence.

The fifth move is the one that gets skipped, and skipping it is what makes month three unpleasant. A change that did not help is as much a result as one that did, and the record of it is what stops the next person trying it again. Modules 2 and 4 both end with a table that has to contain at least one row where the number did not move.

The eight debts and the three differences quoted in section 2 are from the abstracts of both papers, which is what this course opened. The widely reproduced claim that only a small fraction of a real machine-learning system is machine-learning code comes from a figure inside the first paper, not from its abstract, so this course does not state it or attach a percentage to it. The nine stages of the second paper's workflow are likewise not named here.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which stage of the classical machine-learning lifecycle has no counterpart in the system this course builds?
You call a model somebody else fitted, at a version you pinned, so there is no training stage of your own. Monitoring still applies and matters more, not less. Collecting data becomes curating a corpus, which is the same stage with a different name. Deploying is unchanged. Only the fitting step is genuinely absent, and taking it out is what changes the cadence of the loop.
You hold 3 corpus snapshots, 2 model ids and 2 eval sets fixed. What does a sixth prompt version add?
The count is a product, so each prompt version multiplies against 3 x 2 x 2, which is twelve. One more is the intuition that makes people change three things at once. Six confuses the count of versions with the count of combinations. Two counts only the model ids and forgets the corpora and the eval sets. The growth being multiplicative is the whole reason for the one-change rule.

Practice about 25 minutes, with an editor

Practice 1foundation

Write the charter. It is one Markdown file in the repository you will carry through this course, it is under a page, and it is the capstone's first artefact.

Five headings and nothing else. The corpus: what it is, roughly how large in tokens, who owns it and how often it changes. The question: one sentence describing what a user will actually ask, written as a user would ask it rather than as a feature. The number: which single measurement decides whether a change ships, even though you cannot compute it yet. The rollback: the command that undoes a bad change, and the person who calls it. Out of scope: three things this system will not do, so the next person does not add them.

Then score yourself on the figure above and put the two lowest-weighted gaps at the bottom of the file as the next two things to fix.

Hint

The hard heading is the third one. You do not have a metric yet, and writing "answer quality" there is what this practice exists to prevent. Write the shape of it instead: what would have to be true of a run for you to call it better, stated so that two people would agree on whether it happened.

If you cannot name an owner for the rollback, write "unowned" rather than inventing one. An honest gap is worth more than a plausible name.

Show what a usable charter looks like
  1. The corpus heading is where most charters go wrong, and the failure is vagueness about ownership. "Our documentation" is not a corpus; "the 340 pages under docs/ in the handbook repository, re-exported by the platform team on merge, about 210,000 tokens" is one. The ownership clause is what tells you, in month three, why the answers changed on a day you shipped nothing.
  2. The question heading has to be in the user's words. "Semantic search over policy documents" is a feature. "Am I allowed to expense this, and what is the limit" is a question, and it is the one that shows you immediately that half your corpus is irrelevant to it.
  3. The number heading is a promise you cannot keep yet, and that is correct. Write "the fraction of our thirty questions whose answering passage appears in the top ten retrieved" and leave it. Lesson 0160 turns that sentence into a computed value; the sentence has to exist first, or 0160 has nothing to compute against.
  4. Out of scope is the heading that pays off latest. Three lines here save an argument in module 5 about whether the system should also file tickets. Write them while nobody is asking for anything.
  5. Commit it before you write any code. A charter written after the pipeline exists is a description of what you built, which is a different document and a much less useful one.

Sanity check. Hand the file to somebody who has not seen your project and ask them what the system does and how you would know it got worse. If they can answer the first and not the second, your third heading is still a feature description. The test for that heading is whether two people reading it would agree on whether a given run passed.

Your teacher. If the four artefacts feel arbitrary, ask your agent to take a wrong answer from a system you have used and, for each of the four, say what change to it could have produced that answer and what evidence would rule it out. If the multiplication in section 3 felt abstract, ask it to redo the count with the version numbers you are actually holding right now and to say which artefact you should freeze first.
