Module 02 · Retrieval quality and context engineering · Lesson 0230

Put the missing words back, and pay for them once

working 8 min Grounded in Anthropic's own contextual retrieval measurements and in the late chunking paper, read at arXiv:2409.04701v3. · arXiv:2409.04701

The one-minute version

  • Cutting a document produces orphan chunks: passages whose subject, date or product name was in a sentence that is now in a different row.
  • A query names the subject. The orphan does not contain it, so no setting of top-k will find it and the failure looks like a retrieval bug.
  • Repair one: write the context in. Generate a short situating sentence per chunk at ingest and prepend it before embedding.
  • Repair two: embed first, cut second. Run the whole document through the encoder and split after the transformer, so each chunk's vector already saw its neighbours.
  • They cost differently and no head-to-head evaluation exists that this course could open. Run both on your thirty questions.

1. The orphan chunk

Open twenty chunks from your own index and read them as a stranger would. A good number of them will be sentences whose subject is a pronoun, a table row with no table header, a paragraph beginning "This means that", or a figure caption with no figure. Each one was perfectly clear in its document and is unfindable out of it.

This matters more than it looks, because the failure is invisible from every direction you normally check. The chunk is present, the embedding is fine, the index is healthy, and recall is simply lower than it should be with no single question you can point at. It is also the failure that a bigger model cannot help with: the model never sees the passage, because the passage was never a candidate.

2. Repair one: write the context in

Anthropic's contextual retrieval write-up is the clearest published statement of the first repair. Before embedding, generate a short passage that situates the chunk - their prompt asks for "a short succinct context to situate this chunk within the overall document for the purposes of improving search retrieval" - and prepend it. Their generated context is typically 50 to 100 tokens per chunk.

The cost is a generation pass over the whole corpus at ingest. They put the one-time cost at $1.02 per million document tokens, using Claude 3 Haiku with prompt caching, which is the mechanism that makes it affordable: the document is the same on every chunk of that document, so it is written to the cache once and read many times.

Two things follow that are easy to miss. It is an ingest-time cost and not a request-time one, so it belongs in the lesson 0200 pipeline and it does not touch your latency budget. And the added words help the keyword index as much as the vector one, which is why lesson 0260 gets a second benefit from the same money.

The last two rungs of that ladder are lessons 0260 and 0270, so it is also a map of where this module is going. Read the whole shape rather than the final number: two thirds of the improvement is already there before the reranker arrives, and the reranker is the only rung that costs you latency on every request.

3. Repair two: embed first, cut second

The second repair does not generate anything. Late chunking, from Günther and colleagues, runs the entire document through a long-context embedding model first, and applies the chunking "after the transformer model and just before mean pooling". Every token has already attended to the rest of the document by the time the split happens, so each chunk's vector carries the context even though the stored text does not.

The paper says the method "works without additional training" and is "generic enough to be applied to a wide range of long-context embedding models". The bill is therefore one encoder pass per document instead of one per chunk, and no generation at all - a completely different cost shape from repair one.

Both sources describe their own method and neither compares itself with the other on one corpus, and this course could not open any evaluation that does. So there is no honest ranking to give you here. Run both on your own thirty questions, record both deltas and both bills, and keep whichever your own numbers support. If a page anywhere tells you one of these simply wins, ask which corpus it won on.

4. What this changed

This is the first lesson where a large move is plausible, and it is also the one where you are most likely to be fooling yourself, so measure it the same way as the rest: one change, the same thirty questions, the number written down whatever it is.

Contextualise a few hundred chunks rather than the whole corpus for the first run. Re-run the golden set and compare recall@10 with the lesson 0220 row. Then price the full corpus from what the sample cost, so the decision to contextualise everything is a number rather than an enthusiasm.

Two rows are worth keeping even if they are flat. If your documents are short - a page each, with the subject in every paragraph - then the orphan problem barely exists in your corpus and the repair buys nothing. That is a real finding about your data and it belongs in the capstone table beside the changes that worked.

The whiteboard framing of what an embedding can and cannot carry is Embeddings in AI System Design for Staff+ Interviews.

Practice about 50 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

Before you contextualise anything, find out whether you have the problem.

Read twenty chunks cold. Sample them at random, print only the chunk text, and mark each one that cannot be understood without its document. That fraction is your orphan rate, and it predicts how much this lesson can possibly buy you.

Contextualise a sample. Take a few hundred chunks that cover your golden set, generate a situating passage for each with the document in the prompt, prepend it, and re-embed only those.

Re-run the golden set and record recall@10 against the lesson 0220 row.

Price the whole corpus from what the sample cost, in tokens and in money, and write that beside the delta.

Hint

Put the document first in the prompt and the chunk last, so the document is the part a prompt cache can hold across all the chunks of that document. Getting this the wrong way round is what makes contextualisation expensive.

Ask for one or two sentences and cap the length. A generated context that runs to a paragraph is competing with the chunk it was meant to serve.

Show what a good result looks like
  1. The orphan rate is the prediction. If two of twenty chunks were unreadable alone, there are about 10% of rows this can help and a large delta would be surprising. If twelve of twenty were, expect a real move and budget for the full corpus.
  2. Price it from your own sample. Three hundred chunks at 400 tokens is 120,000 chunk tokens plus one document read per document. Multiply out to your corpus size and compare with the published $1.02 per million document tokens, which used Claude 3 Haiku with caching. A figure far above that usually means the cache is not being hit.
  3. Compare like with like. Only the sampled chunks were contextualised, so a golden-set question whose answer lives outside the sample is measuring nothing. Either restrict the scoring to questions the sample covers, or say plainly that the delta is a lower bound.
  4. Write the row with its bill attached. "recall@10 0.68 to 0.74, one-time ingest cost about $4 for the full corpus" is a complete row. A delta with no bill is half a result, and this is the lesson where the bill is easiest to leave out.

Sanity check. Your contextualised rows should be roughly a fifth longer than the originals - 50 to 100 tokens added to a few hundred. If they doubled, the model is summarising the chunk rather than situating it, and you are diluting the passage you wanted to find. Read five generated contexts by eye before you trust any delta.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to sample twenty chunks and, for each, to say what a reader would need to know that the chunk does not contain. Then ask it to write the contextualisation prompt with the document placed for caching, run it over three chunks only, and show you the generated context beside the original. Read those three yourself before you spend anything on the corpus.

Primary source to go deeper

The repair that costs no generation: Late Chunking: Contextual Chunk Embeddings Using Long-Context Embedding Models, read at v3. The abstract is enough to see the mechanism - all tokens embedded first, chunking applied after the transformer and just before mean pooling - and to see that the fine-tuning it proposes is an extra rather than a prerequisite.
