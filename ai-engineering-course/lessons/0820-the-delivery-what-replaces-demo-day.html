Module 08 · Final delivery · Lesson 0820

The demo is an artefact, and the part you rehearse is the failure

frontier 9 min Composed rather than quoted, and the written record it asks for is the decision record a staff engineer already owes. · The written decision record

The one-minute version

  • A demo that needs you in the room reaches whoever was free that afternoon. Three artefacts reach everybody else: a recording, a design record and a live URL.
  • Record it against the deployed system, not against localhost. A demo of your laptop is a demo of your laptop.
  • The design record is not new writing. It is the six questions of lesson 0730, answered in prose, and you have already answered two of them with numbers.
  • Rehearse the failure. Everybody rehearses the happy path, which is the one nobody doubts. One induced outage, shown on purpose, is the most persuasive minute of the five.
  • There is no cohort and no grader, so the last act is a self-assessment with named criteria, written before you record rather than after.

1. Three artefacts, and none of them is a meeting

The syllabus this course was built from ended in a demo day, which is a good thing for a cohort and unavailable to a reader working alone. What survives the conversion is everything that mattered about it: somebody who did not build the system forms a view of it.

An event reaches the people who were free. A recording reaches the person reading your application at eleven at night, the engineer deciding whether to interview you, and the colleague who has to maintain it in a year. It is also the only one of the three that you can watch back and be honest about.

The live URL is the artefact people underrate, because it is the one that turns a claim into a check. A reviewer who can send their own question to your system learns more in thirty seconds than five minutes of video can tell them, and they learn it about their own question rather than about the one you chose.

2. Five minutes, in the order that survives contact

Five minutes is about 300 seconds and it is enough for six things, which means each one has a budget you can write down before you record.

Open with what it is and who it is for, in thirty seconds, and resist the temptation to explain the architecture first. Then one real request, end to end, against the deployed URL. Then the numbers, which is where this course's whole argument lands: the golden-set score with its k and its set size, and the cost per request. Then the failure, induced on purpose. Then one design decision and the arithmetic behind it. Then thirty seconds saying where the repository is.

3. The design record is six questions you have already answered

The written half of the delivery is the one people dread, and it is almost entirely done. Lesson 0730's six questions are the outline: the unit of work and its request shape, where untrusted text enters and what it may do, the p95 token count and cost, what degrades when the provider is down and who is told, how long a regression stays invisible, and how far one wrong answer travels.

Answer each in a paragraph. Two of them are already numbers you read out of your own traces, one is the arithmetic from lesson 0720, and one is a behaviour you induced in staging rather than a policy you wrote down. What makes it a record rather than a description is the last line of each answer: what you would change if you had another week, and why you did not do it now. A record with no rejected alternative in it reads as a system that was never designed, only assembled.

This is the same artefact a staff engineer is expected to produce for a decision nobody can relitigate later, and Carrying the organisation is where that discipline is developed. Borrow the shape from there rather than inventing one here.

4. Rehearse the failure, because everybody rehearses the other thing

The happy path is the part nobody in the audience doubts. They assume it works, because you would not be showing them otherwise, and the thirty seconds you spend proving it are thirty seconds spent on the least contested claim you have.

The degraded path is the opposite. It is the thing a reviewer has learned to suspect is absent, because in most systems it is, and showing it is the fastest way to be believed about everything else. Induce it on camera: revoke the key, point the provider at a dead host, or trip whatever breaker lesson 0610 left you. Then show what a user sees and say who gets told.

5. Assess it yourself, because there is nobody else to

A cohort has a grader and you do not, so the criteria have to be written before the recording rather than negotiated with yourself afterwards. Six are enough, and each is a yes or a no: the recording runs against a deployed URL; a number with its k and its set size is spoken aloud; a failure is induced rather than described; one rejected alternative appears in the design record; a stranger could open the URL unattended; and the whole thing is under six minutes.

Then watch it back once, with the list beside you, before you send it to anybody. The second recording is always better than the first and the first one is never the one to send.

A live URL is a system with your key behind it, open to whoever has the link. Before you publish one, put a rate limit on it, cap the tokens a single request may spend, and give it a budget you can watch - lesson 0670 is where those signals came from. A demo endpoint that quietly bills you for a month is the most common way this last step goes wrong, and it goes wrong after everybody has stopped watching.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does the recording run against the deployed URL rather than against a local instance?
The whole point of the artefact is that somebody else can reach the system, and a localhost demo proves nothing about anything they can reach. The score runs identically either way. Screen quality is unrelated. And the provider bills the call, not the machine that made it - if anything, the deployed path costs the same and shows the probes and caches you added in module 6.
A reviewer has five minutes. Which minute is the one most demos leave out, and which claim does it carry?
A degraded path is the claim reviewers have learned to suspect is missing, so demonstrating it buys more credit than any further evidence of the happy path. The opening and the closing are short but rarely omitted. The live request is the one thing every demo has, and it argues the least, because nobody doubted it.

Practice about 60 minutes, with a screen recorder

Practice 1frontier

Make all three artefacts, in this order, because each one makes the next easier.

Write the design record first. Six paragraphs, one per question from lesson 0730, each ending with what you would change and why you have not. It is about a page and it is the thing you will find yourself quoting while you record.

Rehearse the failure second, off camera, until you know exactly what a user sees and how long it takes. If the answer is a stack trace, you have found work rather than a rehearsal.

Then record once, watch it back against the six criteria, and record again. Publish the recording, the record and the URL from the README the previous lesson is about.

Hint

Say the k and the set size out loud with every number. “Recall at ten was 0.68 on thirty hand-written questions” is a sentence a reviewer can weigh; “we get about seventy per cent” is one they cannot, and they will assume the worse reading.

If revoking a real key is too alarming, point the provider base URL at a host that refuses connections. It exercises the same path and you can undo it in one line.

Show what the first attempt usually reveals
  1. The first recording runs eight minutes, not five. Almost all of the overrun is the opening: three minutes of architecture before a single request was served. The fix is to move the request to second and let the architecture appear inside the design record.
  2. The failure rehearsal finds a real defect. Revoking the key produced a 500 and an empty page rather than the retrieved passages, because the degraded path was written and never exercised. That is lesson 0610's increment being checked for the first time by somebody with a reason to look.
  3. Two of the six questions had no answer. The p95 cost was never read out of the traces, and nobody had ever timed how long a regression stays invisible. Both are half an hour of work and both are the questions a reviewer asks first.
  4. The second recording is four minutes and forty seconds. It contains a number, a failure and one rejected alternative, and it is the artefact that goes in the application rather than the one that impressed you while you made it.

Sanity check. Count how many seconds of your recording contain a number spoken with its k and its set size. If the answer is zero, you have recorded a product tour rather than a delivery, and every reviewer who has seen a product tour will discount it accordingly.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to watch your design record rather than your recording, and to answer the six questions of lesson 0730 using only what is written there - then to mark each answer as stated, inferred or missing. The inferred ones are the paragraphs where you described a mechanism instead of stating a behaviour. If the failure rehearsal is the part you are avoiding, ask it to list every way your deployment can lose the provider and which of them you have actually seen happen.
