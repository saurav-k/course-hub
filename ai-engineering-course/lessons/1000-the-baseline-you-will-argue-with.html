A number nobody can reproduce is a number nobody can contradict

frontier 8 min The first capstone stop. Everything here was produced by module 1; this page is about committing it as one thing rather than six. · The eval harness

The one-minute version

  • Module 1 made six artefacts. Committed together they are a baseline. Committed apart they are six files, and a file nobody can join to the others proves nothing.
  • A baseline is not the numbers. It is the numbers plus enough identity to say which system produced them - which set, which corpus, which prompt, which model id.
  • Without that identity your dashboard is showing one of sixty possible systems and cannot say which.
  • The gate for this page is blunt: the baseline is in the repository, not in a notebook. A notebook is where a baseline goes to be lost.
  • Everything modules 2 to 6 do is a delta against this. If it is wrong or missing, every later number in the course is unfalsifiable.

1. Six artefacts, and why they travel together

Module 1 finished with six things in your hands: a one-page charter from 0100, a lockfile and a configured exporter from 0110, a corpus ingested and indexed from 0140, a thirty-question golden set from 0160, a trace whose Retrievals span carries document ids and scores from 0150, and three numbers from 0160 and 0170.

The temptation is to treat the numbers as the deliverable and the rest as scaffolding. That is exactly backwards. A score is meaningless without the thing that produced it, and the other five artefacts are that thing. Lesson 0100 counted three corpus snapshots against two model ids against two eval-set versions against five prompt versions and got sixty distinct systems; a number arriving without its identity belongs to an unknown one of those sixty.

So the unit of work here is a commit rather than a file. Six artefacts, one commit, and a short record at the top of it that says which of the sixty this is.

2. The set you just wrote is the smallest it will ever be

One property of the golden set decides whether the capstone holds together: it only ever grows. Nothing is removed, because a set that shrinks when a question becomes inconvenient has stopped being evidence.

By module 4 it is materially bigger than what you wrote by hand, and none of the additions were written the same way. Module 2 adds a deliberately poisoned chunk and leaves it there permanently. Module 4 adds five questions the corpus genuinely cannot answer, so that a refusal can be scored rather than assumed, and three failures promoted out of real traffic.

That is why every later module re-runs this set rather than making its own. Module 6's semantic-caching lesson runs it through the cache to count the answers that changed, and module 6's CI gate puts a threshold on one of its scores. Two harnesses would give two numbers and no way to choose between them.

3. What the commit actually buys

The word for what you have just built is falsifiability. Every later module claims an improvement, and a claim that cannot fail is not a claim.

Module 2 says hybrid search improved retrieval. Against what? Against recall@10 = 0.61 on golden-v1, corpus handbook-v7, and if it comes out at 0.60 then it did not, and that row goes in the table anyway. That is the whole mechanism, and it only works if the left-hand side of the comparison was written down before the change was made.

4. The gate, and why it is worded that way

The gate for this capstone stop is one sentence: the baseline is written down in the repository, not in a notebook.

The wording is deliberate rather than fussy. A notebook holds a number beside the code that produced it, which feels like the same thing and is not: the notebook is not versioned with the corpus, it is not read by CI, its cells run in an order nobody records, and in four weeks nobody can say which of the sixty systems the output belonged to. A file in the repository is diffable, is reachable from the module 6 gate, and travels with the commit that produced it.

If your numbers are currently in a terminal or a notebook cell, moving them costs ten minutes and is the highest-value ten minutes in this module.

The values in the record on this page - 0.61, 0.44, 0.78, 17/20, handbook-v7 - are a worked example showing the shape of a complete record. They are not targets, not typical, and not measured on anything. Your own numbers come from your own corpus, and the whole point of this page is that a number without its corpus and its set is not a number at all.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Four weeks on, a colleague reads recall@10 = 0.71 in your repository and nothing else. What can they conclude?
Without a named question set and a corpus version the figure identifies none of the sixty possible systems, so it supports no conclusion at all - which is the reporting rule stated as a consequence. The first two options both assume a comparison the record does not carry: there is no left-hand number and no statement of what changed. The fourth invents a fact; nothing in a bare score says anything about the corpus.
Why does module 4 add five questions the corpus cannot answer to a set used to score retrieval?
A system that should decline has to be measured on whether it declines, and that needs questions whose correct answer is a refusal. The second option inverts the purpose: the aim is coverage of a behaviour, not a lower headline. Thirty-nine questions is nowhere near a sample for a tail statistic, and the set is explicitly not statistically comfortable. The fourth describes the three promoted failures, which are a different addition with a different reason.

Practice about 30 minutes, in your own repository

Capstonefrontier

This is a consolidation stop rather than new work: everything below already exists somewhere in your module 1 output. The exercise is making it one object.

Write evals/baseline.md with all four blocks from the assembler above: what system this is, the retrieval numbers, the generation number with its judge agreement beside it, and the command that reproduces the run.

Commit the six artefacts together in one commit whose message names the baseline. If any of the six is missing - most commonly the judge agreement, occasionally the charter - go and get it before committing, because a partial baseline is the failure this page exists to prevent.

Then check it from outside. Clone your own repository into a fresh directory, run the command in the record, and confirm you get the same two retrieval numbers. If you cannot, the record is incomplete and you have just found out cheaply.

Hint

If the fresh clone produces different numbers, the usual cause is state that never entered the repository: an index built from a corpus snapshot you no longer have, or an environment variable that changes the model. Both are the four-artefacts rule catching you, which is what it is for.

The judge agreement is the artefact most often missing, because it takes an hour of hand-scoring and produces no visible feature. Record it anyway; module 6's gate is unusable without it.

Show what a complete baseline looks like
  1. Three numbers, each with its set and its corpus version. recall@10 and nDCG@10 from lesson 0160, faithfulness from 0170. If any of the three appears without its identity, the reporting rule has not landed yet.
  2. The judge agreement sits on the line under the faithfulness score, not in a separate document. Physical adjacency is doing real work here: it is what stops a later reader quoting the score alone.
  3. The baseline commit is boring and that is correct. No feature, no improvement, no cleverness. It is the only commit in the course whose entire value is that a later commit can be compared to it.
  4. The fresh-clone check is the one people skip and the one that finds things. Reproducing your own number from a clean directory is the difference between a record and a note to yourself.
  5. Write down what you could not reproduce, if anything. An honest "the index is rebuilt from a snapshot not in this repository" is worth more than a clean-looking record that quietly is not reproducible, and it names the first thing module 2 should fix.

Sanity check. Your recall@10 should be well under 1.0 and probably under 0.7. A baseline near 0.95 almost always means the golden-set questions were written from the passages rather than in an asker's own words, which lesson 0160 warns about; rewrite three of them the way a colleague would ask and measure again. A comfortable first number is a first number that measured nothing.

Your teacher. If the six-artefacts rule feels like bookkeeping, ask your agent to take your repository as it stands and say, for each of the six, whether a stranger could find it and what it would take to reproduce your recall number from a clean clone. If your fresh-clone check disagreed with your original run, hand it both outputs and ask which of the four versioned artefacts differed between them.
