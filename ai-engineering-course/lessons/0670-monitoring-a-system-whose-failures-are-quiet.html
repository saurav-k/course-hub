Your worst outage returns 200 on every request

working 9 min Span and metric names read at commit fee465db, where every GenAI document is marked Development and the repository has published no release or tag. · GenAI spans

The one-minute version

  • Every failure mode in this course - a bad retrieval, a false cache hit, a truncated answer, an injected instruction - returns HTTP 200 with a fluent paragraph.
  • So the signals worth alerting on are not error rates. They are token counts, finish reasons, cache hit shares and eval scores.
  • All of them already have names. The GenAI conventions publish the span shape and twelve metrics, and adopting the names is cheaper than inventing them.
  • gen_ai.input.messages is Opt-In, and that is a privacy decision rather than a verbosity setting: switching it on writes user text into your traces.
  • Scale on queue depth, not CPU. A worker waiting on a socket is idle by every measure a Resource metric can see.

1. Everything in this module fails with a 200

Walk back through the module and count the failures that produce an error. There are none.

A retrieval that returns the wrong ten passages returns 200. A false hit from lesson 0640's semantic cache returns 200, faster than usual. An answer truncated because it hit the output limit returns 200 with a complete-looking paragraph that simply stops. An injected instruction obeyed as in lesson 0650 returns 200. A silent fallback to a cheaper model returns 200. A provider degradation that shortens answers returns 200.

So the ordinary monitoring reflex - error rate, latency, saturation - detects none of it, and worse, several of them make your dashboard look better. A cache serving wrong answers cuts latency and cost at once. A model that truncates answers is faster than one that finishes them.

The signals that do move are all about content rather than about transport, and there are four of them worth a page at three in the morning:

  • Output token distribution. A sudden fall means truncation or a model swap; a sudden rise means a prompt change or a loop.
  • Finish reasons. A rise in the length-limit reason is truncation, arriving as successful responses.
  • Cache hit share. A jump means your prefix stopped moving or your semantic threshold drifted; a collapse means the prefix started moving and lesson 0630's saving just evaporated.
  • The nightly eval score from lesson 0660, which is the only one of the four that can tell you the answers got worse.

2. Use the names somebody has already argued about

You do not need to invent this vocabulary. The OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions define the span shape and the attributes, and adopting them costs a file of constants and buys you a chance that your tooling already understands your traces.

An inference span is named {gen_ai.operation.name} {gen_ai.request.model}. Two attributes are Required: gen_ai.operation.name and gen_ai.provider.name. Several are Recommended, including gen_ai.usage.input_tokens, gen_ai.usage.output_tokens and gen_ai.response.finish_reasons - which is three of the four signals above, already named. The twelve metrics are all Histograms, which is what makes the percentiles from lesson 0620 computable at all.

The caveat travels with the names every time this course prints one. Every document in that repository is marked Status: Development, and the repository has published no release and no tag; these names were read at commit fee465db. What you are committing to is the shape - one span per stage, tokens and finish reasons recorded on the inference span - and a rename costs you one file of constants, which is exactly why the constants belong in one file.

Then there is the group marked Opt-In, and it is not opt-in because it is verbose. gen_ai.input.messages, gen_ai.output.messages and gen_ai.system_instructions carry the actual text: the user's question, the retrieved passages, your system prompt. Turning them on writes customer data and your own corpus into whatever your traces are stored in, for however long that retains, readable by everybody with access to your observability tool. That is a decision with a data-protection answer, and the honest middle path is to sample it, redact before export, and keep it off by default.

3. Scale on the queue, because the CPU is lying

The last piece is the one that surprises people who have scaled ordinary services.

A worker that is waiting twenty-four seconds for a provider to finish generating is doing no work. It holds a socket and a little memory, and its CPU utilisation is close to nothing. So a Horizontal Pod Autoscaler targeting CPU sees an idle fleet at exactly the moment every worker is occupied and new requests are queueing.

The HPA's algorithm is not the problem; it is doing what it says. Kubernetes documents it as desiredReplicas = ceil(currentReplicas × currentMetricValue / desiredMetricValue), with a default tolerance of 0.1 that skips scaling when the ratio is close to 1. Feed it a CPU number that stays near zero under load and the ratio never leaves the tolerance band, so it never scales.

The fix is the metric rather than the algorithm. HPA supports four metric types - per-pod resource metrics, per-pod custom metrics, object metrics and external metrics - and the signal you want is in-flight requests per pod or queue depth, which arrives as a custom or external metric rather than a Resource one. Lesson 0600's queued shape makes this easy, because the job store's pending count is already the number you need.

Every attribute and metric name here is marked Status: Development in a repository that has published no release and no tag, and was read at commit fee465db. Names can change; the shape is the durable part. The two charts are illustrations of mechanisms - a blocked worker's CPU, a threshold inside a tolerance band - rather than measurements of any system, and the HPA formula and its 0.1 default are Kubernetes documentation at v1.37.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Overnight your p95 latency fell 40% and your cost per request fell by a third. What should you check first?
Latency and cost both falling together is the exact signature of answers getting shorter, so the first check is the output-token distribution and the finish reasons: a rise in the length-limit reason means truncation and a fall in tokens with no reason change means a model or prompt change. Traffic volume is worth ruling out and does not change per-request cost. A new region changes latency and not token counts. Treating it as good news is the failure mode the whole page is about.
Which HPA metric type does "in-flight requests per pod" arrive as, and why not the usual one?
Resource metrics are CPU and memory from the resource metrics API, and neither moves when a worker is blocked on a network read, so concurrency has to arrive as a per-pod custom metric or an external one. Being per-pod does not make a number a Resource metric; the type is about where it comes from. Object metrics describe a single object and are the right shape for a shared queue's depth, which is a reasonable alternative, but concurrency per pod is not one.

Practice about 50 minutes, with your traces and a dashboard

Practice 1working

This is the last increment module 6 makes to the capstone, and it closes the loop the course opened in module 1.

Emit the constants file from the assembler above, with the Opt-In block left off, and confirm that one request produces an inference span carrying the operation, the provider, both token counts and the finish reason.

Build one dashboard with four panels: output token distribution, finish reasons by kind, cache hit share from lesson 0630, and the latest nightly eval score from lesson 0660. Four panels, not fourteen.

Then break each of them on purpose and confirm the panel moves. Lower max_tokens until answers truncate. Add a per-request value to the top of your prompt so the cache stops hitting. Point at a different model id. Each takes two minutes and each proves one panel is wired.

Finally, set one alert, on the signal you would actually want to be woken for, and write down what the on-call person should do when it fires.

Hint

If your finish-reason panel is empty, your client is probably discarding the field before you see it. It is on the raw response object rather than on the convenience accessor in several SDKs.

Resist adding panels. A dashboard with four panels that somebody reads beats one with fourteen that nobody does, and everything else is already queryable from the trace when you have a specific question.

Show what a finished monitoring setup looks like
  1. Four panels, each tied to a failure you have actually caused. The deliberate breaks are the point: a panel nobody has ever seen move is a panel nobody will trust at three in the morning, and half of them are usually wired wrongly the first time.
  2. Output tokens as a distribution, never a mean. Truncation shows as the right tail being cut off while the mean barely moves, so a mean-only panel misses precisely the failure this page opened with.
  3. One alert, not four. For most capstones the honest choice is a fall in the output-token p50 or a rise in the length-limit finish reason, because those catch truncation and model swaps and both are silent. The eval score is a daily report rather than a page.
  4. The runbook line matters as much as the alert. "Check whether the model id changed, then check whether the prompt changed, then run the golden set" is a runbook. "Investigate" is not.
  5. Keep the Opt-In block off, and write down why. One sentence in the repository saying that message content is not exported, and what you would need to change to sample it safely, is the artefact that keeps the decision from being re-made accidentally.

Sanity check. After lowering max_tokens, your latency and cost panels should both improve while the finish-reason panel deteriorates. If you see the improvement and not the deterioration, your dashboard is currently capable of showing a truncation outage as a successful optimisation, which is exactly the state this lesson exists to leave behind.

Your teacher. If the four signals feel arbitrary, ask your agent to list the ways your own system can return a wrong answer and, for each, which of your existing panels would move. The ones that move nothing are your gaps. If the Opt-In decision is genuinely open, ask it to draft the two-sentence data-retention note both ways and take the pair to whoever owns that question where you work.
