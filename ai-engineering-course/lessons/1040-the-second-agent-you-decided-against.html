A recorded no, with the arithmetic, is a finished capstone increment

frontier 7 min The fifth capstone stop by module order. Two companies that both ship these systems published opposite advice, and this stop is where you take a side about your own. · The eval harness

The one-minute version

  • The capstone increment for module 5 is a decision record, not an architecture. Two numbers and a verdict.
  • The measured single-agent cost from your own traces, the projected multi-agent cost from the published multipliers, and the go or no-go.
  • Deciding against the second agent is a pass, and for most readers it is the right answer. What is not a pass is not deciding.
  • The evidence is genuinely contested: one company reports a large win on an unpublished internal set, another shipping the same class of system published the opposite advice. The course does not resolve it and neither should your record.
  • If you did split, one thing has to be true: you can tell from a trace which agent did what, and where it ran.

1. Two numbers, one measured and one borrowed

The pressure to split is real and worth stating fairly: one context window holding a system prompt, twenty tool schemas, retrieved passages and a growing trace gets worse as it gets longer. That is a genuine problem and module 3's ceilings only bound it.

The relief is expensive, and the multipliers are published. Anthropic reports that agents use about 4× the tokens of a chat and multi-agent systems about 15×, on its own systems. Those are the numbers to project with, and the projection is the honest part of this exercise: you multiply your measured single-agent cost by a multiplier somebody else measured, and you say so in the record.

Your own number is the one that has to be measured rather than borrowed. It comes from the token histograms module 6 made you emit, at the tail rather than the median, and it is the only figure in this decision that is about your system.

2. The evidence is contested, and your record should say so

This is the one place in the course where two organisations shipping the same class of system published opposite advice, and the honest thing is to present both rather than pick the tidier one.

One position reports a large win from an orchestrator with parallel subagents - on an internal evaluation set that is not published, which makes it a company's claim about its own measurement rather than a result anybody can check. The other, from a company shipping the same class of system, argues for sharing full traces and preferring a single agent with a compression step.

Module 5 does not resolve that and neither should you. What your record can do is say which of the two your situation resembles: whether your work genuinely parallelises, whether your subtasks are independent enough to survive being separated, and whether you can afford the operational surface the split adds.

3. If you did split, one thing has to be true

A multi-agent run that goes wrong has three possible addresses, and rewording a prompt fixes at most one of them. Fourteen observed failure modes cluster into three categories - system design, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification - and telling them apart is a property of your trace rather than of your intuition.

So the requirement is specific: your trace must record which agent did what, and whether it ran in this process or somewhere else. The GenAI conventions distinguish a remote agent invocation from a local one, which is exactly the boundary an A2A surface creates. Without that distinction a broken run has three candidate causes and no way to choose.

That is the same discipline module 1 installed for retrieval and module 3 extended to trajectories: the instrument comes before the debugging, not after it.

The 4× and 15× multipliers are Anthropic's published figures for its own systems, and the large win they accompany was measured on an evaluation set that is not published. The opposing advice is another company's published position on systems it ships. Both are evidence about somebody else's systems. Neither is a measurement of yours, and the dollar readings above rest on an assumed token price that is nobody's published rate.

Practice about 30 minutes, mostly writing

Capstonefrontier

One document, and it is short. The work is in being honest about which half of it is yours.

Measure your single-agent cost at the tail, from the token histograms module 6 made you emit. Median is the wrong statistic here and the tail is where a split would bite.

Project the split using the published multiplier, and label it as borrowed in the same sentence.

Write the decision: go or no-go, in one paragraph, naming which of the two published positions your situation resembles and why. If it is no-go, say what would change your mind and at what number.

If it is go, add the trace requirement: confirm you can tell from one recorded run which agent did what and where it ran.

Hint

If you cannot read a tail cost, that is the finding and it comes before the decision. A p50 will understate the case for splitting and a mean will hide it entirely.

"What would change my mind, and at what number" is the sentence that makes this a decision record rather than an opinion. It is also what a successor needs in six months.

Show what a finished decision looks like
  1. Two numbers, with their provenance on the same line. Yours measured, theirs published, and the projection labelled as the product of the two.
  2. A verdict in one paragraph, and usually it is no. For a single-corpus question-answering system the work rarely parallelises enough to earn a fifteenfold bill, and saying so with the arithmetic is the module landing.
  3. A reversal condition. "We would revisit at four independent sub-questions per request, or if tail cost per request falls below X." Without it the record cannot be acted on later.
  4. Both published positions named, neither presented as settled. A record that cites only the one supporting your decision is the failure this stop is written against.
  5. It goes in the design record for lesson 1060, where it is one of the strongest things in the delivery. A recorded refusal with numbers shows judgement that another shipped component does not.

Sanity check. Your projected multi-agent monthly cost should be roughly fifteen times your single-agent one, because that is what the multiplier says. If your projection is close to the single-agent figure, you have applied the multiplier to one request rather than to the workload, and the decision is being made on a number an order of magnitude too small.

Your teacher. If your decision came out "yes" quickly, ask your agent to name the four operational surfaces the split adds and who will own each one at three in the morning. If it came out "no", ask it to write the reversal condition as a number you could actually observe, rather than as a feeling about complexity.

Primary source to go deeper

The harness whose tail statistic this decision rests on, and the reporting rule that makes your half of the arithmetic checkable: The eval harness. Whatever you decide, the record is only useful if the number in it names the set and the conditions it came from.
