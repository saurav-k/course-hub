Capstone · Read after module 08 · Lesson 1060

Everything you built, handed to somebody who was not in the room

frontier 7 min The last capstone stop. Nothing new is built; six earlier stops are packaged so that a stranger can run, read and judge the result. · The eval harness

The one-minute version

  • Every previous stop produced an artefact. This one makes them reachable by somebody who was not there, which is a different property from being correct.
  • Four things carry it: a repository a stranger can run, three tiers of test, the design record, and a five-minute recording against the deployed system.
  • The design record is not new writing. It is the six questions of lesson 0730 in prose, and two of them you already answered with numbers.
  • Rehearse the failure, not the happy path. One induced outage shown on purpose is the most persuasive minute of the five.
  • There is no cohort and no grader, so the final act is a self-assessment against criteria you wrote before recording.

1. The repository is a promise, and it has four parts

A public repository claims that somebody else can reproduce your result. Four artefacts carry that claim and each fails differently when it is missing.

The README fails the returning reader when it is all tutorial: four documentation modes exist and a first-run walkthrough is only one of them. The environment fails when the lockfile is not the thing CI uses. The licence is the one people treat as optional, and "no licence" is a decision - the decision that nobody may use it. The system card is the honest version of the README's claims: what the system is for, how it was evaluated, and where it fails. You did not train the model and you still owe one, because everything a reader needs to know about the failures is yours rather than the vendor's.

The evaluation section of that card writes itself, because module 1 made you record the harness and every module since has re-run it. That is the payoff for having one set rather than several.

2. Three tiers, and the one that cannot run on every push

Module 8 sorted your tests into three tiers and the sorting rule is what to fake at each: deterministic tests around the model, contract tests against recorded tool responses, and the golden set with a threshold. Only the first two run on every push.

That is the same structure module 6's CI gate already installed, which means this stop is mostly checking that what is in CI matches what the README claims. A repository whose README promises a test suite that CI does not run is a repository that will be red for the first stranger who forks it.

3. The record is transcription; the recording is rehearsal

The design record is the cheapest document in the course, because it is the six questions of lesson 0730 answered in prose - and lesson 1070 already answered two of them with numbers. If writing it feels like research, the review was not run.

The recording has one rule that separates it from every demo people actually give: rehearse the failure. Everybody rehearses the happy path, which is the one nobody doubts. One induced outage, shown on purpose, with the degraded mode from lesson 0610 doing its job, is the most persuasive minute available to you and it is the minute that proves the other four.

Record against the deployed system rather than localhost. A demo of your laptop is a demo of your laptop, and the difference is visible to exactly the audience you are trying to reach.

4. Present the things you decided not to build

This course asked you to decide against things twice, with arithmetic: the second agent in module 5, and the semantic cache in module 6. Both are increments to the capstone and both are complete when the decision is recorded with its numbers.

The temptation at delivery is to present only what was built, because a list of features looks like more work. It is the wrong instinct. A recorded refusal with a measured false-hit rate or a projected token multiplier beside it is stronger evidence of engineering judgement than another shipped component, and it is the part of a capstone that a reviewer cannot get from a screenshot.

So both belong in the design record and one of them belongs in the five minutes.

Nothing on this page is a new technique and nothing here has a number of its own. Every figure quoted in your record and your recording came from an earlier stop, and each carries its own provenance - measured on your corpus, derived from your traces, or somebody else's published measurement labelled as such. The one rule this stop adds is that the labels travel with the numbers into the record, because that is the document somebody will quote back at you.

Answer from memory before revealing.

Writing the design record turns into a week of research. What does that tell you?
The record is the six questions answered in prose, with two of them already answered by numbers at the previous stop, so it should be transcription. Needing research means the review has not actually been done and the writing is doing it for the first time. The first two options treat a symptom as a cost of the format. The last confuses the record with the recording, which are two artefacts answering different questions.
Which single minute of a five-minute recording does the most work?
Everybody rehearses the happy path and nobody doubts it, so the minute that carries the other four is the one where something breaks on purpose and the degraded mode does its job. An architecture diagram is better read than watched. A best answer is unfalsifiable to a viewer. Scores belong in the record where they can be checked rather than in narration where they cannot.

Practice about an hour, and a stopwatch

The last increment. Four artefacts, and one of them needs another person for ten minutes.

Fill in the scorecard above before anything else, so your criteria exist before the work that will be judged by them.

Test the ten minutes on a real person. Hand them the repository URL and nothing else, start a stopwatch, and write down where they stop. Where they stop is the README's next edit.

Write the design record from your 1070 answers: six questions, two of them already numbers.

Record five minutes against the deployed system, with one induced failure in the middle, and include at least one decision you made not to build something, with its arithmetic.

Hint

If nobody is available for the ten-minute test, use a fresh machine and a clean clone with no environment variables set. It is a weaker test and it still finds the missing setup step.

Record the induced failure last if it makes you nervous, then cut it into the middle. Rehearsing it once is usually enough to stop it feeling like an admission.

Show what a finished delivery looks like
  1. The stranger stopped somewhere, and you wrote it down. Almost nobody gets through in ten minutes on the first attempt; the value is entirely in knowing which step stopped them.
  2. The system card's failure section came from the golden set. The rows your system gets wrong are your failure modes, already enumerated and already measured, and copying them across takes minutes.
  3. The design record is one page and reads as answers. If it reads as an essay, it is describing the system rather than reviewing it.
  4. The recording contains a failure and a refusal. The induced outage, and at least one thing you decided not to build with the number that decided it.
  5. The scorecard was filled in before the recording. A rubric written after the artefact is a description of that artefact, which is the trap lesson 0170 names for judges and it applies here unchanged.

Sanity check. Your five minutes should spend roughly one on what the system does, one on how you know it works, one on the induced failure, one on a decision you did not take, and one on what you would do next. If more than two of the five are the system working, the recording is a product demo rather than an engineering account, and the audience you want is the one that can tell.

Your teacher. If the ten-minute test has no available human, ask your agent to follow your README literally on a clean clone and report the first instruction that assumes something not stated. If your design record is hard to write, hand it your 1070 answers and ask which of the six questions you have not actually answered yet.
