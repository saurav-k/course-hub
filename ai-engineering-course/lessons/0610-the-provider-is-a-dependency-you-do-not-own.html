A health check that calls the model is a way to fail twice

working 10 min Probe defaults read from the Kubernetes source at tag v1.37.0, in SetDefaults_Probe, rather than from prose about them. · Configure probes

The one-minute version

  • A health check answers one question about your process. The moment it also asks a question about somebody else's service, an outage over there becomes a self-inflicted outage over here.
  • The three probes control three different things and only one of them can kill you: liveness restarts, readiness withdraws traffic, startup delays the other two.
  • With the shipped defaults, a liveness probe that calls the provider kills the container after three failures ten seconds apart, which is thirty seconds into an outage nobody could have fixed.
  • Readiness may test anything you own. Liveness may test almost nothing, and the shorter it is the better it works.
  • Your SDK already retries. Anthropic's retry transient failures twice by default with exponential backoff, so a retry loop you write on top is a multiplier, not a policy.

1. Three probes, three different verbs

Kubernetes gives a container three health checks and they are not three severities of the same idea. They are three different actions.

Liveness restarts. If it fails, the kubelet kills the container and starts it again. The only question it should ever ask is "is this process wedged in a way that only a restart fixes" - a deadlock, a corrupted in-memory state, an event loop that stopped turning. Almost nothing qualifies.

Readiness withdraws traffic. If it fails, the pod is removed from the service's endpoints and no request is routed to it. Nothing is killed and nothing is lost, so this is where you may be generous: the index has not finished loading, the job store is unreachable, the worker is saturated. A pod that fails readiness is resting, not broken.

Startup gates the other two. While it is failing, liveness and readiness are suspended, which is what lets a container that takes two minutes to load an index avoid being killed at second thirty by a liveness probe that was written for a container that starts in one second.

The defaults matter here because they are what you get if you write no numbers, and they are in the Kubernetes source rather than in folklore. SetDefaults_Probe at tag v1.37.0 sets timeoutSeconds to 1, periodSeconds to 10, successThreshold to 1 and failureThreshold to 3. So a default liveness probe declares a container dead after three consecutive failures ten seconds apart, and that arithmetic is the whole of the next section.

2. The restart storm, in arithmetic

Here is the failure this page exists to prevent, and it is written by somebody sensible. You want your health check to be meaningful, so you make /healthz send a one-token request to the model. It passes in development, it looks rigorous in review, and it is wired to liveness because that is the endpoint everyone wires to liveness.

Now the provider has an incident and starts returning 529 overloaded_error. Your process is fine. Your index is fine. Your job store is fine. But every pod's liveness probe is now failing, and with the shipped defaults every pod is killed thirty seconds later - three failures at ten-second intervals. Each one restarts, comes back, fails again, and is killed again.

Take a fleet of six pods and a provider outage lasting ten minutes. Before the change, capacity during the outage is six pods that cannot generate but can still serve everything that does not need the model: cached answers, retrieval-only endpoints, the job status endpoint from lesson 0600, and a clear error message. After the change, capacity is zero, and it stays zero for a while after the provider recovers because the pods are in a restart cycle rather than serving.

3. What each probe is allowed to know

The rule that falls out is short. Liveness tests only what a restart could fix. In practice that is an endpoint that returns 200 if the process can execute a handler at all, and it is allowed to be as boring as it sounds. If you cannot name the wedged state your liveness probe detects, delete the probe rather than making it clever.

Readiness tests everything you own and nothing you do not. The index is loaded, the job store answers, the queue depth is below the point where another request would simply time out. All three are yours, all three are recoverable without a restart, and withdrawing traffic from a pod that fails any of them is exactly the right response.

The provider belongs in neither. What the provider's health should drive is a degraded mode, which is an application decision rather than a probe: serve from the cache in lesson 0630, queue the request as lesson 0600 taught, return retrieval results with no generated summary, or return a clear error. All four keep the pod serving, and none of them is a restart.

4. Which errors to retry, and the retries you already have

The published error codes tell you what to do without much interpretation. 400 invalid_request_error is your bug and retrying it produces the same 400 at the same price. 401 and 403 are credentials and permissions, which no amount of waiting repairs. 429 rate_limit_error and 529 overloaded_error are the two that genuinely mean "later", and 500 api_error carries the documentation's own instruction to "retry the request with exponential backoff".

The part that surprises people is that this is already happening. Anthropic documents that the official SDKs "automatically retry transient failures (such as connection errors, rate limits, and 5xx server errors) with exponential backoff, twice by default, honoring the retry-after header when present". So the retry loop you wrap around the client does not add retries, it multiplies them: three of your attempts around a client that retries twice is nine requests, and the wall-clock time your caller waits is the product rather than the sum.

Set the client's maximum-retries option explicitly to whatever you actually want, and put your own policy at one level only. The general mechanism - backoff, jitter, circuit breakers, budgets - belongs to Production Systems, Resilience patterns, and the whiteboard framing to AI System Design, Reliability and security. What is specific to this course is only that the retried call is expensive and non-idempotent, so a retry storm here has a bill attached that a retry storm on a database read does not.

The probe defaults on this page were read from SetDefaults_Probe in the Kubernetes source at tag v1.37.0, because two attempts to read them out of the documentation prose returned values hedged as "standard configuration" rather than quoted. The error codes and the SDK retry behaviour are Anthropic's published documentation for its own API. Neither set transfers to another provider without checking, and the six-pod outage chart is this page's arithmetic over those defaults rather than an incident anybody measured.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your index takes ninety seconds to load and pods are being killed during deployment. Which probe change fixes it without weakening anything?
A startup probe suspends liveness and readiness until the slow work finishes, which is precisely the case it exists for, and it leaves the steady-state liveness setting untouched. Raising the liveness threshold also stops the killing and does it by making genuine wedges take longer to detect for the life of the pod. Removing liveness fixes the symptom and gives up the one thing liveness is for. Pointing readiness at the index is correct and separate: it stops traffic arriving early and does nothing about being killed, because readiness never kills.
You wrap the SDK in your own loop of three attempts. The provider returns 529 for a minute. How many requests reach the provider per call?
The documented SDK behaviour is two automatic retries on transient failures, so each of your three attempts is three requests and the total is nine. Your loop bounds only your own layer, and the SDK's bound is per call rather than per operation. The last option inverts the documentation: 529 overloaded_error is exactly the class that is retried, while 400 invalid_request_error is the one that should not be.

Practice about 40 minutes, with a cluster or a local equivalent

Practice 1working

The point of this exercise is that you have seen the failure rather than read about it, because the restart storm is entirely convincing in prose and entirely forgettable until you have watched the restart counter climb.

Write the three probes for your capstone. Liveness on an endpoint that touches nothing external. Readiness on an endpoint that checks the index and the job store and names whichever failed. A startup probe sized from a timed cold start.

Then induce a provider outage. Point your application at a local proxy that returns 529 for everything, and leave it there for five minutes with your real probes running. Record the restart count per pod and whether any endpoint still served.

Then repeat it with the liveness probe deliberately wrong - have it call the model - and record the same two numbers. Keep both numbers; they are the evidence for the manifest you keep.

Hint

You do not need a real cluster. Anything that runs the same probe semantics is enough, and even a shell loop that curls the two endpoints every ten seconds and counts three consecutive failures reproduces the arithmetic.

The proxy is twenty lines. It needs to return the status code and a body of the right shape, because your SDK's typed exception is chosen from the status and the error type.

Show what the two runs should tell you
  1. The correct manifest restarts zero times. The process was never unhealthy, so nothing kills it. That is the number to keep, and it should be exactly zero rather than low.
  2. The correct manifest still serves something. At minimum the job status endpoint and any retrieval-only path. If yours served nothing, your degraded mode is the missing piece rather than your probes, and that is the fourth row of the scorecard.
  3. The wrong manifest restarts repeatedly and serves nothing. Expect the first kill about thirty seconds in, from the three-failure default at ten-second intervals. The exact count afterwards depends on your start time and on the kubelet backing off between restarts.
  4. The recovery is the part worth watching. Turn the proxy off and time how long until each fleet serves again. The correct one is serving immediately, because it never stopped. That gap is the real cost of the mistake and it is invisible in the outage window itself.
  5. Write the four numbers and the manifest into the repository. Restarts and served-endpoints for each run. Lesson 0670 turns "did anything serve" into an alert, and it needs to know what normal looks like.

Sanity check. If both runs restarted zero times, your induced outage did not reach the probe: check that the probe endpoint really calls the proxy and that the proxy really returns a non-2xx. If the correct run restarted even once, something in your liveness path reaches outward that you did not intend, and finding it is the whole exercise.

Your teacher. If the liveness and readiness split still feels arbitrary, ask your agent for five checks your service could plausibly make and have it assign each to liveness, readiness or neither, giving the restart-would-fix-it test as its reason each time. If the retry multiplication surprised you, ask it to read your own client construction and say exactly how many requests one call can become before your code sees an exception.
