Module 07 · Agentic AI system design · Lesson 0720

Five numbers price a system that does not exist yet

frontier 8 min Worked against Anthropic’s published list prices, which are a vendor’s stated rate rather than anybody’s measurement, and against the multipliers from lesson 0700. · Claude API pricing

The one-minute version

  • A monthly bill for an agentic system is one multiplication of five numbers, and four of them you choose.
  • Because it is a product, a one percent change anywhere moves the bill by one percent. Sensitivity is not about the terms, it is about how far each one can travel.
  • Traffic can move by a factor of ten. Model choice moves the price a token by about five. Prompt wording moves nothing at all.
  • Caching is the one term that changes the shape rather than the size, because it splits input tokens into two prices.
  • Write the number down before you look at a bill, so the gap between the two teaches you something about the estimate.

1. The five terms, and the one multiplication

Everything an agentic system costs is the same product. Write it once and stop deriving it.

cost a month = R × T × F × (Kin × pin + Kout × pout) × 30 Read as: the monthly bill is the number of requests a day R, times the number of model turns each request takes T, times the fan-out width F, which is how many agents run each turn, times what one turn costs, which is its input tokens Kin at the input price pin plus its output tokens Kout at the output price pout, all over thirty days.

The whiteboard framing of this arithmetic, together with per-query and per-user variants, belongs to AI System Design, and this lesson does not repeat it. What this lesson does is run it on your own traces, which is a different act: every term above is a number you can already read out of the spans you have been recording since module 1.

2. Worked, on numbers you can replace

Take a service handling 2,000 requests a day, three model turns each, no fan-out, 8,000 input and 1,000 output tokens a turn, on Claude Sonnet 5 at its published list price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output.

  1. Price one turn. 8,000 × $2 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.016 of input, plus 1,000 × $10 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.010 of output. One turn is $0.026.
  2. Notice what that split says. Output is 11% of the tokens and 38% of the cost, because the published output rate is five times the input rate. A change that shortens answers is worth roughly five changes that shorten prompts.
  3. Count the turns. 2,000 requests × 3 turns × 1 agent = 6,000 turns a day.
  4. Multiply out. 6,000 × $0.026 = $156 a day, and × 30 = $4,680 a month.
  5. Express it as a blended rate, for the estimator below. $0.026 for 9,000 tokens is $2.89 per million tokens, which is the single number to carry when you want tokens and dollars on one axis.

3. Caching splits one term into two prices

Prompt caching is the exception to the shape above, because it stops input tokens having one price. Anthropic’s published multipliers are a cache read at 0.1x the base input rate, a five-minute cache write at 1.25x and a one-hour write at 2x, and their own guidance is that the five-minute form “pays off after one cache read”.

Apply that to the worked example. If 6,000 of the 8,000 input tokens are a stable prefix that hits the cache, input becomes 2,000 × $2 plus 6,000 × $0.20, per million: $0.004 plus $0.0012, which is $0.0052 rather than $0.016. A turn falls to $0.0152, and the month falls to $2,736, about 42% off, with no change to what the system does. What that arithmetic assumes is that the prefix really is stable, and the mechanism that decides whether it is belongs to module 6.

4. Which term actually moves

A product has no privileged term: one percent anywhere is one percent on the answer. That is a true statement and a useless one, and it is why sensitivity analysis on a product has to ask a different question. Not how much does the bill move per percent, but how far can this term realistically travel.

5. Where the estimate will be wrong

An estimate is useful in proportion to how specifically you can say where it breaks, so name the four before anybody asks.

Every price in this lesson is a vendor’s published list rate, read from the pricing page, and list rates change without changing anything about your system. Pin the rate you used inside the estimate itself. An estimate that does not say which price list it was computed against cannot be re-checked later, and it will be quoted long after it stops being true.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does a per-percent sensitivity analysis of this formula tell you almost nothing?
In a pure product a one percent change in any factor is one percent on the result, so ranking the terms that way returns a tie. The terms are largely independent in the estimate. Output does cost five times more per token, which is a reason to shorten answers rather than a sensitivity result. Caching splits the input price but leaves the product shape intact.
Your worked estimate says $4,680 and the first real bill is $7,900. Which explanation should you check first?
Retries are billed and produce no answer, so they are invisible in every count taken from successful responses, which is the usual first cause of a gap this size. Traffic is worth checking but you can read it directly and would already know. A wrong token mix shifts the blended rate by a few percent, not seventy. A list-price change is rare and easy to rule out in one look.

Practice about 30 minutes, with your traces

Practice 1frontier

Size your own capstone at ten times its current traffic, and commit the estimate to the repository before you look at any bill.

Read four terms out of your traces rather than guessing them: requests a day, median model turns a request, median input and output tokens a turn, and your fan-out width, which is one unless module 5 changed your mind. Take the fifth, the price, from the published rate for the model you actually call, and write the rate into the estimate.

Then produce three numbers and one paragraph. The monthly bill at today’s traffic, the bill at ten times, and the bill at ten times with your stable prefix cached. The paragraph names the three upward risks from section 5 that apply to you and says which one you would notice first.

Hint

Take medians, not means. A single runaway trace moves a mean somewhere no request actually is, and that runaway is a separate finding worth its own line in the paragraph.

If you cannot separate input from output tokens in your spans, use the blended form and say so in the estimate. A stated approximation beats a precise-looking number built on a guess.

Show what a defensible estimate looks like
  1. Read the terms. Say 140 requests a day, median 2 turns, median 6,400 input and 700 output tokens, fan-out 1, on Claude Sonnet 5 at $2 and $10 per million.
  2. Price a turn. 6,400 × $2 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.0128, plus 700 × $10 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.007. A turn is $0.0198, call it $0.02.
  3. Today. 140 × 2 = 280 turns a day, × $0.0198 = $5.54 a day, × 30 = $166 a month. At ten times traffic, $1,663.
  4. With the prefix cached. If 4,800 of the 6,400 input tokens are a stable system and instruction prefix, input becomes 1,600 × $2 plus 4,800 × $0.20, per million, which is $0.0032 plus $0.00096. A turn falls to $0.0112 and the ten-times month falls to about $940.
  5. Name the risks. Retries are uncounted, the session grows, and the ten-times traffic is the assumption doing the most work in the whole estimate.

Sanity check. Your cached figure should land somewhere between 40% and 70% of the uncached one. Above 70% your prefix is not really stable or is not really the bulk of the input. Below 40% you have almost certainly applied the cache rate to output tokens as well, which it never touches.

Your teacher. If the sensitivity argument still feels like a trick, ask your agent to recompute the tornado at a fan-out of four and say which bar becomes widest. If your own estimate came out suspiciously round, ask it to re-derive the blended rate from your real input and output medians and to tell you what fraction of your bill is output tokens.
