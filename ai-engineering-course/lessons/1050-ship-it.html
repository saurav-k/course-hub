Capstone · Read after module 06 · Lesson 1050

Eight changes to one service, and now you can say what it costs

frontier 8 min The sixth capstone stop. Nothing new is built here; module 6's eight lessons are assembled into one deployment and one written budget. · The four request shapes

The one-minute version

  • Module 6 changed one service eight times. Assembled, they are a deployment: four shapes, three probes, two budgets, one pattern, one threshold.
  • The deliverable is not the deployment. It is four documents - the endpoint table, the latency budget, the cost budget and the containment note - because a deployment nobody can describe cannot be handed over.
  • The largest single number module 6 produced is almost always the prompt cache, and it is a prompt-ordering change rather than a feature.
  • Two of the eight lessons may correctly end in a decision not to ship something. A recorded refusal with its numbers is a pass.
  • The gate: your service survives a provider outage without restarting, and a bad prompt cannot reach main.

1. Four documents, not one deployment

The instinct at this stop is to point at a running service. That is the wrong deliverable, because a running service that only its author can describe is not handed over, is not on-called, and cannot be reasoned about when it misbehaves.

The four documents are small and each one already exists as a by-product of a module 6 lesson.

  • The endpoint table from 0600. Every endpoint that ends in a model call, its shape, and the one sentence that justified it.
  • The latency budget from 0620. Five segments at p50 and at p95, and the one change that would remove the most seconds.
  • The cost budget from 0630. Prefix tokens, requests per period, what the prompt cache saves, and whether the semantic cache from 0640 shipped.
  • The containment note from 0650. Which of the six patterns you applied, and - the part people omit - what an injection can still influence.

Together they are about two pages. They are also the input to module 7's design review, which asks six questions your own system has to answer, and every one of the six is answered faster if these four exist.

2. The two budgets, and which one moved

Latency and cost are separate budgets with separate levers, and module 6 found that the biggest lever in each is unglamorous.

On latency, the largest available improvement is streaming, and it changes no total. The work is unchanged; what changes is that the reader starts at the first token rather than the last. On the module's worked assumptions - 600 output tokens at 40 ms each with 1.2 s to first token - that moves perceived wait from about 25.2 seconds to about 1.2, and your total-duration dashboard reports nothing at all.

On cost, the largest lever is the provider's prompt cache, and it is bought by sorting the prompt so the stable part comes first. On the published multipliers a five-minute cached prefix is already cheaper at the second request. That is not a feature and it does not appear in any changelog.

3. Two lessons you may correctly have refused

Module 6 contains two decisions that a careful reader can finish by declining, and a recorded refusal is a pass rather than an incomplete.

The semantic cache. Lesson 0640 asks you to re-run the golden set through it and count the answers that changed. If your product is one where a plausible wrong answer is expensive, the right output of that lesson is a threshold sweep, a false-hit rate, and a written decision not to ship it. The prompt cache has already taken most of the cost saving with none of the correctness risk.

The strictest containment pattern. Lesson 0650's six patterns trade capability for a guarantee, and picking a weaker one deliberately - with a note saying what an injection can still influence - is a real answer. Picking none, or picking one without knowing what it gives up, is not.

This is the same principle module 5 states about the second agent: the arithmetic comes before the architecture, and a recorded no is a complete capstone increment. What is not acceptable is silence: a capstone that simply never mentions the semantic cache has not made a decision, it has skipped one.

4. The gate

Two things have to be true, and both are demonstrable rather than assertable.

Your service survives an outage it did not cause. Point it at a proxy returning 529 for five minutes and confirm the restart count stays at zero and at least one endpoint still serves. If every endpoint fails, the probes may be right and the degraded mode is missing, which is a product decision rather than a manifest edit.

A bad prompt cannot reach main. Break a prompt deliberately, push it, and watch the gate go red on a threshold you placed outside your own measured run-to-run spread. Then revert and watch it go green.

If both hold, the four documents are written, and the two refusals are recorded with their numbers, this stop is complete. Module 7 then prices the whole thing at ten times its traffic and reviews it against six questions, and both of those start from what you just wrote down.

Every figure on this page is carried forward from module 6's own worked assumptions or is a published multiplier from a vendor's documentation, and each is labelled where it appears. The dollar readings rest on an assumed base price that is nobody's published rate. None of them is a target. Your own budget is built from your own traces, and the reason module 1 made you write a baseline down is so that these numbers can be compared with something.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your capstone never mentions the semantic cache. Is that a complete module 6 increment?
The lesson's deliverable is a measurement and a decision, and either answer is acceptable so long as the numbers behind it are written down. Silence is the one outcome that is not, because it is indistinguishable from never having looked. The third option overcorrects: shipping it is not required and is often wrong. The first two treat skipping and deciding as the same act, which is exactly the distinction this stop is testing.
Which module 6 change is invisible in a total-duration dashboard yet is usually the largest improvement?
Streaming moves when the first token reaches the reader and leaves total duration unchanged, so it is both the largest latency improvement available and completely invisible unless the time-to-first-chunk metric is recorded separately. Sorting the prompt is the largest cost change rather than a latency one, and it is very visible on the bill. Probes change what happens during an outage. The CI threshold acts before a deploy and touches no request.

Practice about 45 minutes, mostly writing

Capstonefrontier

Almost all of this is assembly. The only new work is the two demonstrations at the end, and both are short.

Write the four documents into the repository, beside evals/baseline.md: the endpoint table, the latency budget at p50 and p95, the cost budget with the prompt-cache line item, and the containment note naming your pattern and what an injection can still influence.

Record the two decisions - the semantic cache and the containment pattern - each with the number that decided it. "Not shipped, false-hit rate 11% at the threshold that gave a 60% hit rate on golden-v1" is a decision. "Not used" is not.

Then demonstrate the gate twice. Five minutes of induced 529 with the restart count recorded, and one deliberately broken prompt pushed through CI and reverted.

Hint

If the four documents take more than two pages you are re-explaining module 6 rather than recording your own system. Each one is a table or a short list; the reasoning lives in the lessons and does not need repeating.

If your cost budget has no prompt-cache line, check the readback rather than the arithmetic: a prefix that still reports as ordinary input tokens is a prefix that moved between requests, and the usual cause is a value interpolated near the top of the prompt.

Show what a finished stop looks like
  1. The endpoint table has more than one shape in it. If every row still says synchronous, lesson 0600's sort was done on paper and not in code, and the queued and streamed rows are where the rest of the module's value was.
  2. The cost budget's largest line is the prompt cache, for nearly everyone, and it arrived from re-ordering rather than from new machinery. If yours is dominated by something else, that something else is worth a paragraph, because it is unusual.
  3. The containment note says what is still reachable. A note claiming the system is now safe is the failure lesson 0650 was written against; the paper's own patterns each give up something named, and yours should say what.
  4. Zero restarts through the induced outage, and something still served. Both halves matter. Zero restarts with nothing serving means the probes are right and the degraded mode is missing.
  5. The two refusals are recorded with numbers, wherever they landed. Modules 5 and 6 both allow a well-argued no, and the capstone that records two of them with arithmetic is stronger than one that shipped everything without measuring.

Sanity check. Your perceived wait should have fallen sharply while your total duration barely moved; if both fell together, something else changed at the same time - a shorter output cap or a different model - and you cannot attribute either improvement until you separate them. That is lesson 0100's one-change rule arriving in module 6, and it applies to deployment changes exactly as it applies to prompts.

Your teacher. If the four documents feel like paperwork, ask your agent to answer module 7's six design-review questions using only what you have written, and see which ones it cannot. Those are the gaps. If your induced-outage run restarted even once, hand it your probe manifest and ask which check reaches outside your own process.
