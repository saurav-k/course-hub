Module 02 · Retrieval quality and context engineering · Lesson 0270

Retrieve wide, rerank narrow, and price the shortlist

working 8 min Grounded in the cross-encoder reranking paper, read at arXiv:1901.04085v5, and in BEIR's zero-shot comparison, read at arXiv:2104.08663v4. · arXiv:1901.04085

The one-minute version

  • A retriever must score a whole corpus, so it scores query and document separately and precomputes half the work. That is what limits how good it can be.
  • A cross-encoder reads the query and the document together and cannot precompute anything, which is what makes it better and what makes it unaffordable on a corpus.
  • The funnel is the whole idea: retrieve a hundred cheaply, rescore them expensively, pass what fits.
  • Its cost is linear in the shortlist and it lands on the request path, so this is a latency decision before it is a quality one.
  • It sits on top of a real tension: one published result prefers top-20 over top-5, another says the middle of a long context is where answers are missed. Measure, do not pick.

1. Why the second scorer can be better

The retriever you have been improving is a bi-encoder. It turns a document into a vector without ever seeing a query, which is exactly what lets you compute every document's vector once at ingest and then answer a query with a nearest-neighbour search. The cost of that arrangement is that the document's representation cannot depend on the question: whatever the vector kept, it kept before anybody asked anything.

A cross-encoder does the opposite. It takes the query and one document as a single input and reads them together, so the model can attend from a word in the question to a word in the passage. Nothing can be precomputed, because the input does not exist until the question arrives. That is a strictly better position for judging relevance and a strictly worse position for scale.

The evidence that the second pass is worth having is old and clear. Nogueira and Cho's re-implementation of BERT as a passage reranker was "the top entry in the leaderboard of the MS MARCO passage retrieval task, outperforming the previous state of the art by 27% (relative) in MRR@10". BEIR's later comparison, over eighteen zero-shot datasets, finds that "re-ranking and late-interaction-based models on average achieve the best zero-shot performances, however, at high computational costs" - which is this lesson's whole argument in one sentence, including the second half.

2. The bill, which is linear and lands on the request

Reranking N documents is N cross-encoder passes, so the added latency is N times the per-pass cost. There is no batching trick that removes the linearity; batching improves the constant.

added_latency = N × per_pass_ms Read as: the milliseconds a reranker adds to one request is the number of documents in the shortlist, multiplied by the milliseconds one query-and-document pass takes on your hardware. Measure the second term rather than taking it from a benchmark.

3. How many to pass on, which is genuinely unsettled

Reranking gives you a better-ordered shortlist. How much of it to put in the prompt is a separate question, and the two best-evidenced answers this course could open point in opposite directions.

Anthropic's retrieval work reports that "passing the top-20 chunks to the model is more effective than just the top-10 or top-5", measured on their own corpora. Lesson 0240's sources say the recall of a long context degrades as it grows, and that the middle is where information is missed. Both can be true, because they are claims about different things - whether the answer is present, and whether it is used - and the balance between them depends on how long your passages are and how good your ordering now is.

What a good reranker changes about that argument is the ordering, and the ordering is what the position finding cares about. That is the reason to do the two together: a reranked top-20, packed with the strongest passage adjacent to the question, is a different experiment from an unreranked top-20 in retriever order.

Both of those results were measured by their authors on their own corpora with their own models, and neither is a setting for your system. This course cannot tell you whether 5, 10 or 20 is right for your questions, and any page that does is quoting somebody else's corpus at you. The experiment is four runs and you have the harness for it.

4. What this changed

Reranking is the last rung of the published ladder from lesson 0230, and the ladder is worth re-reading before you spend anything: the first two rungs took the failure rate from 5.7% to 2.9% at ingest-time cost, and the reranker took it from 2.9% to 1.9% at request-time cost. That is the smallest of the three gains, and it is the only one your users wait for.

So record two numbers together or the row is meaningless. Run the golden set with the reranker over your fused top 100, and record recall@10 and p95 latency, before and after. A reranker that adds four points of recall and 400 milliseconds is a decision for whoever owns the product's latency, and your job is to hand them both halves rather than the flattering one.

Then vary the shortlist. Reranking 100 and reranking 50 usually differ less than the arithmetic suggests, because the documents at ranks 51 to 100 rarely survive to the top 20 anyway - and if that is true on your corpus you have just halved the cost for nothing.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why can a cross-encoder not simply replace your retriever?
The cross-encoder reads query and document together, so nothing about a document can be computed before the question exists, and scoring a corpus would mean a model pass per document per query. It scores relevance better, which is the reason to want it. It needs no extra store, because it reads the text you already have. Both scorers produce a number you can sort by, so the output shape is not the obstacle.
Your reranker improves recall@10 by four points and adds 400 ms at p95. What belongs in the capstone table?
The whole point of the module's rule is that a change is recorded with what it cost, and this is the first change in the module whose cost lands on every request a user waits for. Deferring latency to a later module is how a system arrives at deployment already too slow. Four points on thirty questions is a real move, not noise. Sweeping the shortlist is the next experiment, not a precondition for recording this one.

Practice about 45 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

This is the module's last retrieval change and the first one whose cost your users feel.

Measure the per-pass cost first. Score one query against one document with your chosen cross-encoder, a hundred times, and take the median. That number replaces the placeholder in this page's arithmetic and decides everything below.

Rerank the fused top 100 from lesson 0260 down to 20, and pack those 20 with the strongest passage adjacent to the question, as lesson 0240 established.

Record recall@10 and p95 latency together, before and after, on the same thirty questions.

Then halve the shortlist to 50 and re-run. If the quality is unchanged, take the cheaper one and write down that you did.

Finally, sweep what you pass on: 5, 10 and 20 reranked passages, and see whether your own corpus prefers the larger set.

Hint

Measure the per-pass cost with the shortlist you will actually use, batched the way you will actually batch it. A single-pair timing on a cold model overstates it by a lot, and a fully batched timing on a warm one understates the tail your p95 will see.

Reranking and reordering are two changes. Run the reranker in retriever-packing order first, then change the packing order, so you can attribute whatever moves.

Show what a good result looks like
  1. The per-pass number is the one that makes the rest real. At 4 ms a document, a shortlist of 100 is 400 ms, which is a serious fraction of a two-second answer. At 0.5 ms it is 50 ms and the decision is easy. You cannot know which case you are in without measuring it.
  2. Expect the ladder's shape, not its numbers. The published ladder gained about a point of failure rate from reranking after gaining nearly three from ingest-time work. A reranker that transforms your results usually means the earlier stages were leaving something on the table, which is worth chasing before you pay per request.
  3. Compare 100 and 50 on the questions that changed. If the answer for every golden-set question came from the fused top 50, then reranking 100 is scoring 50 documents that could never have been packed, and halving it costs nothing at all.
  4. Write the row with both halves. "rerank top 100 to 20: recall@10 0.79 to 0.83, p95 620 ms to 1,010 ms" is a complete row and it is the one that lets somebody decide. The same row with the latency left out is the reason systems arrive at module 6 unshippable.

Sanity check. Your added latency should be close to the shortlist size times the per-pass cost you measured. If it is much larger, the reranker is being loaded or the passages are being re-fetched per pass; if it is much smaller, some of the shortlist is being skipped, and the recall number you just recorded is for a different configuration than you think.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to time one cross-encoder pass on your own hardware and to put that number into the arithmetic on this page rather than the placeholder. Then ask it to find, across your thirty questions, the deepest fused rank that any finally-packed passage came from. That single number tells you how much of your shortlist is doing work, and it is usually much smaller than the shortlist.
