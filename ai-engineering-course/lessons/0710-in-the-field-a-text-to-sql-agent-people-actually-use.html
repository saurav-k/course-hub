Module 07 · Agentic AI system design · Lesson 0710

Every stage before the expensive call exists to shrink what it must reason about

frontier 8 min Built from two organisations’ own engineering write-ups of products they shipped, and every figure below is their measurement of their own release. · QueryGPT · Musings on building a Generative AI product

The one-minute version

  • Two teams, different companies, different products, the same decomposition: narrow the space, gather what is needed, then generate.
  • Uber names four agents. LinkedIn names three steps and puts small models on the first two and a big one on the last.
  • The reason both arrived there is not taste. Every stage removes something the next stage would otherwise have to reason about, and the last stage is the one that costs.
  • The failure that nearly sank one of them was structured output, not reasoning: invalid parameters about 10% of the time, fixed with a parser rather than a prompt.
  • Both sets of adoption numbers are self-reported from a limited release. Read them as evidence that people use it, never as a target.

1. Four agents, and what each one removes

Uber’s QueryGPT turns a question in English into a SQL query over their own data platform, and the write-up names four stages in order.

An intent agent maps the question to a business domain, choosing among twelve system workspaces such as Mobility, Core Services, Platform Engineering, IT and Ads. That choice is not cosmetic: a workspace is a curated collection of tables, so picking one narrows what retrieval will search from everything to one domain. A table agent then proposes the specific tables and shows them to the user for confirmation or correction, which is a human-in-the-loop step placed exactly where a wrong guess is cheapest to fix. A column prune agent uses a model call to, in their words, “prune the irrelevant columns from the schemas” handed on, and the write-up is explicit that this is done to reduce token consumption and latency. Only then does the generation call write SQL, together with an explanation of how it got there.

2. Why both teams ended up here

LinkedIn describes a three-step retrieval-augmented pipeline: routing, which “decides if the query is in scope or not, and which AI agent to forward it to”; retrieval, which decides which internal APIs to call; and generation, which “filters it and produces the final response”.

Put beside Uber’s four agents, the convergence is not a coincidence and it is not fashion. A generation call is priced by what you put in front of it and is wrong in proportion to how much of that is irrelevant. A schema with four hundred columns is not merely expensive; it is four hundred chances to join on the wrong one. So each early stage is a filter whose job is to delete, and the design question at every stage is what does this remove rather than what does this add.

That question also tells you where to put a human. Uber puts the confirmation after the table agent, which is the last point at which a correction costs one cheap call rather than a wrong query somebody runs.

3. The numbers, and exactly what they are

Uber reports about 300 daily active users in a limited release, 78% of them “saying that the generated queries have reduced the amount of time” they spend authoring, and roughly three minutes to produce a query against around ten minutes by hand. For scale, they note the platform serves 1.2 million interactive queries a month, of which operations is about 36%.

Read those in two groups. The three-minutes-against-ten figure is the closest thing here to a measurement, and it is still that team’s own account. The 78% is self-reported satisfaction, which answers whether people feel faster and not whether they are. The 1.2 million is context for why the product exists rather than a result of it.

4. The failure was structured output, not reasoning

LinkedIn’s sharpest finding is not about quality at all. Their model had to emit parameters for internal API calls, and “~10% of the time the LLM would make mistakes and often output data that was invalid”. Ten percent of calls failing on syntax is not a system anyone can ship.

Their fix is the part worth copying: rather than re-prompting, which costs a whole round trip, they built an in-house defensive parser that detected and corrected the common errors, and the failure rate fell to about 0.01%.

There is a second lesson underneath the first, and LinkedIn state it as a timeline: they reached “80% of the basic experience” within one month and then spent four more months trying to pass 95%. Their answer to knowing where they stood was measurement at a scale most teams never attempt, with a linguist team evaluating up to 500 daily conversations.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What is the design question you should be asking at every stage before the generation call?
Every early stage in both systems is a filter, and its value is what it deletes: a workspace choice deletes domains, a prune deletes columns. Model choice follows from that decision rather than driving it, adding context is the failure these stages exist to prevent, and the confirmation step is one good answer to one stage rather than the question itself.
Which of the reported figures is closest to a measurement rather than a report of a feeling?
Elapsed time to produce a query is a quantity somebody timed, even though it is still the team’s own account of their own product. The 78% is self-reported satisfaction and answers whether people feel faster. Daily active users measures reach on a limited release, and the monthly query volume is platform context that existed before the product did.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Draw your own capstone as these three bands, on paper, in one page.

For each stage you draw, write two lines. The first names what the stage removes, in the units of your own system: documents, columns, tools, candidate answers, whole domains. The second names what the stage costs, as a model call or as latency or as a person’s attention.

Then find the stage you do not have. Almost every first-draft retrieval system goes straight from question to retrieval with nothing narrowing the space first. Name the narrowing stage you are missing, and what it would remove.

Hint

If a stage cannot name what it removes, it is not a stage in this sense. It is either part of the stage before it or a feature that belongs somewhere else in the system.

Your confirmation step, if you have one, should sit at the last point where a correction is cheap. Write down what a correction costs on either side of it.

Show a worked answer for a documentation assistant
  1. Band one, narrow. Route the question to one product area rather than the whole corpus. Removes: roughly four fifths of the index. Costs: one small model call, about 400 tokens.
  2. Band two, gather. Retrieve twenty candidates inside that area, then re-rank to six. Removes: fourteen passages that would otherwise have entered the window. Costs: one embedding call and one re-ranker pass.
  3. Band three, generate. One call over six passages instead of twenty. Removes nothing, because it is the stage everything else was protecting.
  4. Name the missing stage. The first draft had no band one, so every question searched everything. Adding it changed what reached the generation call before any prompt was rewritten.

Sanity check. Your generation call should be reading materially less than your retriever returns. If the packed context is the same size before and after your gather band, that band is sorting rather than filtering, and none of the cost argument on this page applies to it yet.

Your teacher. If the convergence still looks like coincidence, ask your agent to describe a third natural-language-to-query product from its own public write-up, and to place its stages in these three bands without being told the bands are there. If your own drawing has only two bands, ask it what a narrowing stage would look like for your corpus, and what it would have to know to choose correctly.
