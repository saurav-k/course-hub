Module 01 · Foundations · Lesson 0160

You can score the search without generating a single word

foundation 12 min Metric practice follows BEIR, read at v4, whose eighteen datasets report nDCG@10 and whose own finding is that BM25 is a robust baseline. · Thakur et al., arXiv:2104.08663

The one-minute version

  • The generator can only use what arrived. So the first number to build is one that does not involve the generator at all, and it is cheap, fast and deterministic.
  • A golden set is thirty questions you wrote by hand, each paired with the passage that answers it. Generated questions measure the generator, not the retriever.
  • Recall at k is the number that matters, because a passage that did not arrive cannot be used no matter what happens later.
  • BEIR reports nDCG@10 across eighteen datasets and finds BM25 a robust baseline. A dense retriever that does not beat plain keyword search on your corpus has not earned its cost.
  • This page carries a measurement taken on this hub's own content, and the interesting part of it is where the curve stops rising.

1. Thirty questions, by hand

A golden set is a list of rows, and a row is a question plus the identifier of the passage that answers it. That is the entire data structure and it lives in the repository as a small file.

Two rules make it worth having, and both are about how the questions are written.

Write them by hand and write them as a stranger would ask. The seductive shortcut is to hand a passage to a model and ask for a question it answers. That measures whether your retriever can find a passage from words lifted out of it, which it always can, so the score comes out high and means nothing. A real question uses the asker's vocabulary and not the author's, and the gap between those two vocabularies is the thing you are actually measuring.

Thirty is the target and ten is enough to start. Thirty hand-written questions is about ninety minutes of work. It is not a statistically comfortable sample, and it does not need to be: at this stage you are looking for changes large enough to see, and a change too small to show up in thirty questions is a change too small to spend a week on.

2. Recall at k, and reading its curve

Recall at k is the fraction of your questions whose answering passage appears in the top k retrieved. It is the right primary metric for this course for one blunt reason: the generator cannot use a passage that did not arrive, so a recall failure is an upper bound on everything downstream.

Anthropic's contextual-retrieval write-up reports the same quantity inverted, as a failure rate, "1 minus recall@20". That form is worth borrowing when you are reporting improvements, because a move from 0.94 to 0.97 sounds like polish while the same move framed as failures halving from 6% to 3% sounds like what it is.

Here is the curve, measured for this course on this repository's own content so that you can see what an unremarkable starting point looks like. The corpus is the 953 lesson pages of the twenty-one courses that are not this one, chunked at 900 characters with 150 of overlap, giving 12,961 chunks. The retriever is plain BM25 at k1 = 1.5 and b = 0.75. The golden set is twelve questions written in a colleague's voice rather than the pages'.

The flat tail is the whole reason to draw the curve rather than report one number. Five of the twelve questions never appeared at any k measured, and three of those failed badly: one sat at rank 718, one at 322, one at 187 out of nearly thirteen thousand chunks. Those three are pure vocabulary failures. The question about a customer being charged twice never uses the word idempotent, and the page never uses the word charged. No amount of increasing k fixes that, and it is exactly the failure the semantic half of module 2's hybrid search exists to catch.

3. When order matters, and the metric for it

Recall treats rank one and rank ten as the same success. Sometimes that is right, because the model reads all ten. Sometimes it is not: lesson 0240 will show that position inside the window changes whether a passage is used, and a reranker's whole job is order.

nDCG at 10 is the standard measure when order matters, and it is standard in a checkable sense: BEIR reports it as the headline metric across all eighteen of its datasets, which is why quoting it makes your number comparable with published work. It discounts each hit by how far down the list it sits and normalises against the best possible ordering, so a perfect ranking scores 1.

Use recall while you are fixing what arrives, which is modules 1 and 2, and add nDCG when you start changing the order, which is lesson 0270. Reporting both from the start costs one line of code and settles arguments later. This course does not derive the nDCG formula; BEIR is where its use is established here, and the original 2002 definition is worth reading if you want the derivation.

4. The baseline you have to beat

BEIR's finding is the one most easily forgotten when a vector database is already in the architecture diagram: across eighteen datasets in a zero-shot setting, BM25 is a robust baseline, and dense retrievers frequently underperform it while costing more. Its authors also report that reranking and late-interaction models achieve the best zero-shot performance on average, "however, at high computational costs".

The consequence for you is a rule and not an opinion. Measure keyword search on your corpus before you conclude anything about your embeddings. It takes an afternoon, it needs no API key, and it produces the number every later change is measured against. If your dense retriever does not beat it, you have learned something worth far more than the afternoon.

5. The reporting rule

One rule, and it is what makes a number in this project evidence rather than folklore. A number with no k, no named question set and no corpus version is not a number.

"Recall went up to 0.71" is unusable in four weeks. "recall@10 = 0.71 on golden-v2, corpus handbook-v7, BM25 baseline 0.58" is a claim somebody can reproduce or contradict. This is the same discipline lesson 0100 asked for when it made you version four artefacts: a measurement identifies which of the sixty possible systems it came from, or it identifies nothing.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Recall at ten and recall at twenty are identical on your golden set. What does that tell you?
A flat tail means the questions you are failing did not appear in ranks eleven to twenty either, so they are far down and enlarging k will not reach them. A reranker only reorders what was retrieved, so it cannot help these at all. The k might separately be too high for cost reasons, but that is not what the flatness shows. Close scores would show as a slow rise, not a flat line.
Why does BEIR's finding about BM25 matter before you tune your embedding model?
BEIR found BM25 a robust zero-shot baseline that dense retrievers often fail to beat, so it is the bar your more expensive option has to clear on your own corpus. BM25 needs chunking exactly as much as any retriever does. Its scores are not comparable across corpora, which is why recall rather than raw score is what you report. BEIR spans eighteen datasets, not languages, and the count is not why the finding matters.

Practice about 90 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1foundation

Build the golden set and the baseline that every remaining module of this course is measured against. This is the largest single piece of work in module 1 and it is the one that makes the rest of the course falsifiable.

Grow your ten questions from lesson 0000 to thirty, each with the identifier of the passage that answers it, in a file in the repository. Write them in a colleague's vocabulary, not the corpus's.

Then implement BM25 over your corpus with no embedding model and no API key, and report recall at 1, 3, 5, 10 and 20 and nDCG at 10. Plot the curve. Finally record the whole line in the charter: metric, k, set name, corpus version, retriever.

Hint

Write five questions, measure, and look at the three worst failures before writing the other twenty-five. The failures teach you what kind of question your corpus is bad at, and the remaining questions get much better once you know.

BM25 is about forty lines and needs no library. Implementing it yourself once is worth it, because the parameters stop being magic: Robertson and Zaragoza's own survey says the model gives no guidance on setting them and reports that values around 0.5 < b < 0.8 and 1.2 < k1 < 2 work well in many circumstances.

Show what a usable baseline looks like
  1. A recall at ten between about 0.4 and 0.7 is a healthy first baseline. The measurement on this page came out at 0.58. Much higher and your questions are probably echoing the passages; much lower and check for a bug before concluding anything about retrieval, because a broken tokenizer looks exactly like a hard corpus.
  2. Sort your failures by rank and read the worst three. A miss at rank 40 is a ranking problem that module 2's fusion and reranking will move. A miss at rank 700 is a vocabulary problem: the question and the passage share almost no terms, and only the semantic half of hybrid search can reach it. The two need different modules, and the rank tells you which.
  3. Keep every failing question in the set forever. The temptation is to rewrite a question that fails until it passes. That is deleting evidence. If a question is genuinely unfair, mark it and keep it; if it is fair, it is the most valuable row you have.
  4. Put the run in continuous integration now, while it is free. It needs no model call, so it costs seconds and no money, and it is the gate lesson 0250 turns into a rule about prompts. A measurement that runs only when somebody remembers is a measurement that stops running in week three.
  5. Record the losing configurations too. Your BM25 number is not a throwaway: it is the baseline the dense retriever has to beat, and in module 2 the two of them get fused rather than one replacing the other.

Sanity check. Recall must be non-decreasing as k grows, so if your recall at 5 exceeds your recall at 10 you have a bug in how you slice the ranked list. And if recall at 1 equals recall at 20, the retriever is either perfect or returning the same document every time; check that your query is actually being tokenized before you celebrate.

Your teacher. Hand your agent your three worst-ranked failures with the question and the answering passage, and ask it to list the terms the two have in common. It will usually be almost none, which is the concrete form of the vocabulary gap. Then ask it to rewrite each question the way somebody who had read the page would ask, and measure again: the difference between the two scores is the size of the gap you are asking retrieval to bridge.

Primary source to go deeper

The benchmark that established nDCG@10 as the reporting convention and found BM25 hard to beat zero-shot: BEIR: A Heterogenous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models, Thakur et al., at v4. For the parameters in the hint, The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond, Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009, whose section on using BM25 states plainly that the model itself offers no guidance on setting them. The concept-level treatment of recall and precision is Retrieval recall in AI System Design.
