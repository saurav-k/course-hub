Module 07 · Agentic AI system design · Lesson 0730

Six questions review any agentic system, including one you did not build

frontier 8 min Composed from the mechanisms of modules 1 to 6 rather than taken from one source, and every question below names the lesson that gave it its answer. · The whiteboard version lives in AI System Design

The one-minute version

  • A review that transfers is a set of questions, not a set of recommendations. Recommendations expire with the tools they were written about.
  • The six ask what the unit of work is, where untrusted text enters, what a request costs at the tail, what degrades when the provider is down, how you would learn the answers got worse, and how far one wrong answer travels.
  • Two of the six cannot be answered from a diagram. They need instrumentation, and a team that cannot answer them has found the review’s first finding.
  • Score it and fix the lowest-scoring answer. The band matters less than the row you fix this week.
  • The same six work on a system somebody else built, from its public write-up, which is what makes this the module’s transferable skill.

1. Questions transfer, recommendations expire

Most published design checklists for AI systems are lists of recommendations: use a vector database, add a re-ranker, cache aggressively, bound your agent’s turns. Each of those is a correct answer to a question, and the question is what survives when the recommendation stops being right. “Cache aggressively” is wrong for a system whose prefix changes every request. “Bound the turns” is already true of yours.

So the review is written as six questions with no correct answers attached. A system can score full marks on question two with an architecture that looks nothing like yours, and that is the point.

2. The two nobody can answer from a diagram

Questions one, two, four and six are answerable in a meeting by somebody who knows the design. Questions three and five are not, and that asymmetry is the most useful thing the review does.

Question three asks for the p95, not the average. A median request is what you budget against and a tail request is what falls over, and on an agentic system the tail is not close to the middle: one request that loops eight times costs eight times a normal one, and the distribution has a long right side by construction. Nobody can estimate their own p95 token count. It comes out of the traces you have had since module 1 or it does not exist.

Question five asks how long a regression would stay invisible. Not whether you would catch it, which everyone answers yes to, but the elapsed time between the answers getting worse and somebody knowing. If the evaluation runs nightly the answer is up to a day. If it runs in continuous integration on every change the answer is minutes for anything a change caused and still up to forever for anything a corpus drift or a provider update caused. Most teams discover, saying this out loud, that their honest answer is “when a user complains”.

3. What question four is really asking

Question four has a trap in it. Teams answer “we retry, and there is a circuit breaker”, which is a mechanism rather than a behaviour. The question asks what the system does, from the outside, and who finds out.

4. Running it on a system you did not build

The same six questions work on a published account, and that is the whole reason to learn them as questions rather than as a checklist for your own stack. Take either of the two systems from the lessons before this one and answer as much as their write-up allows. Uber names its unit of work precisely, so question one scores well from the outside. Neither post states a percentile of anything, so question three is unanswerable however carefully you read, and it stays unanswerable.

That gap is itself the finding. A write-up that describes an architecture in detail and never states a tail number is telling you what its authors found worth publishing, which is almost never the number a reviewer would ask for first. Question six is the one that usually separates two systems that look alike on a diagram: a query-writing assistant whose output a person reads before running it has a small blast radius, and the same assistant wired to execute has a large one. Nothing in either architecture drawing distinguishes those two, which is why the question is about the answer's onward journey rather than about the components.

A score is not a grade and there is nobody to award one. The failure mode of every readiness scorecard is a team that scores itself, feels reviewed, and changes nothing. The output of this review is one fix, made this week, on the lowest-scoring row, and everything else on the card is context for choosing it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is the review written as questions rather than as a list of recommended practices?
Recommendations encode one correct answer, and the answer stops being correct when the tools or the workload change, while the question behind it keeps working. Meeting speed is not the point and a thorough review is slow either way. Recommendations score perfectly well, which is how most maturity models are built. An outsider can answer either form, given the write-up.
Which pair of questions cannot be answered from an architecture diagram alone?
Both of those come out of instrumentation: a percentile is read from traces and a detection time is a property of a running schedule. Unit of work, entry points, blast radius and degraded behaviour are all design facts somebody who knows the system can state in a meeting, which is exactly why the two instrumented questions are the ones that find the gap.

Practice about 45 minutes, with your repository

Practice 1frontier

Run the review on your capstone, and then run it on somebody else’s.

Yours first. Answer all six in writing, in a file in the repository, one paragraph each. Two of them will need you to go and read traces. Score the card, note the band, and pick the lowest-scoring row.

Then fix that one row before you go on to module 8, and write one sentence saying what changed and how you know.

Then take one of the two systems from lessons 0700 and 0710 and answer as many of the six as their public write-up allows. Note which questions their write-up cannot answer, because that gap is itself a finding about what teams choose to publish.

Hint

If question three sends you to the traces and the token counts are not there, stop and add them. That is a smaller job than the review and it unblocks question five as well.

For the external system, answer question six from what the product does rather than from what the post says. A query-writing assistant whose output a person reviews before running has a much smaller blast radius than one that executes.

Show a worked answer for question six
  1. Name the wrong answer. The retriever returns a passage from a superseded policy and the model quotes it confidently.
  2. Trace it forward one hop. The answer is shown in a chat panel with a citation link to the source page. Nothing is written and nothing is triggered.
  3. Trace it forward two hops. A user pastes the answer into a ticket. Now it is durable, uncited and reads as a decision.
  4. Name the interception point. The citation link is the only place a person can catch it, and it is one click away from being ignored. The fix is a smaller radius rather than a better model: mark the superseded document at ingest so it cannot be retrieved at all.

Sanity check. A good answer to question six names a specific artefact that outlives the request and a specific person who could have stopped it. If your answer is “the user would probably notice”, you have described a hope rather than a radius, and the row scores zero.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to answer the six questions about your system from your repository alone, without asking you anything, and then compare its answers with yours. The questions where it guessed are the questions your repository cannot answer, which is exactly what module 8 is about. Then ask it to run the same six on one of the published systems from this module and to mark each answer as stated, inferred or unavailable.
