Module 05 · Multi-agent systems · Lesson 0550

Find the category before you reach for the prompt

working 9 min The three categories come from a study of over 1,600 annotated traces across seven frameworks; the span names come from conventions marked Development and are quoted with that status attached. · Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?

The one-minute version

  • A multi-agent run that goes wrong has three possible addresses, and rewording a prompt is a fix for at most one of them.
  • Fourteen observed failure modes cluster into three categories: system design, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification.
  • The trace tells them apart, and it needs one thing a single-agent trace does not: which agent did what, and whether it ran here or somewhere else.
  • The conventions distinguish an agent invocation over a remote service from one in the same process, which is exactly the A2A boundary from lesson 0540.
  • Verification failures are the ones a better model does not fix, so establishing the category first is what stops you paying for the wrong upgrade.

1. What a multi-agent trace needs that yours does not have

Module 1 gave you one span per stage of a request, and module 3 added a span per model call and per tool. That is enough to debug one agent. It is not enough to debug three, because it cannot answer "which of them did this".

The OpenTelemetry conventions for generative AI supply the missing vocabulary. An agent invocation gets a span whose operation is invoke_agent, carrying gen_ai.agent.name so you can see who acted, and gen_ai.conversation.id so turns of one exchange stay correlated. Planning gets its own plan operation, as lesson 0520 used.

The distinction that matters most here is one the conventions draw explicitly: an invoke_agent span is a client span when it "describes GenAI agent invocation over a remote service", and an internal span when it "describes GenAI agent invocation within the same process". That is the boundary lesson 0540 crossed, showing up in your telemetry - and it is what lets you tell "our worker was slow" from "their agent was slow".

Two things travel with every one of those names, and this course attaches them each time. The conventions are marked Status: Development and live in a repository that has published no release and no tag. What you commit to is the shape - one span per agent invocation, named, correlated, and marked remote or local - and a rename costs you one file of constants.

2. Making the trace cross the boundary

A trace that stops at your process edge is a trace that cannot answer the only question worth asking about a peer. Both protocols in this module have an answer.

MCP at revision 2026-07-28 documents OpenTelemetry trace-context propagation through its _meta field, naming the keys traceparent, tracestate and baggage. A2A names tracing among the enterprise concerns its design addresses. In both cases you are passing the W3C trace context you already have.

One caution, and it is the reason this course states a revision alongside a convention every time. The convention and the specification can disagree, and here they do: the OpenTelemetry conventions for MCP still show initialize and an mcp.session.id in their own examples, and MCP 2026-07-28 removed both the handshake and protocol-level sessions. Instrumenting from the convention's examples alone would have you record a field that no longer exists.

3. Three addresses, fourteen modes

Cemri et al. built their taxonomy from over 1,600 annotated traces across seven frameworks, developing it on 150 traces with expert annotators at an inter-annotator agreement of 0.88. Fourteen modes, and - this is the part to use - they cluster into three categories.

System design covers failures that show up at run time and come from decisions made before it: an agent that departs from its brief, one that repeats work already done, one that never recognises the task is finished. Five of the fourteen sit here, and every one is fixed in your specification rather than in a prompt.

Inter-agent misalignment covers coordination: work that drifts off the goal, an agent that never asks for the clarification it needs, one that keeps information another needed, one that ignores what a peer returned. Six of the fourteen, the largest group, and lesson 0530's sharing decisions are where most of them are made.

Task verification covers the end: stopping early, checking incompletely, checking wrongly. Three of the fourteen, and the smallest group by count is the one that most deserves your attention, because the study reports that verification remains a challenge regardless of the model used.

That finding is the practical payload of this page. If your failure is a verification failure, a better model is not the fix, and neither is a better prompt. Something has to check the result against the request, and nothing in the run currently does.

4. Score the system, not the agents

One last habit, and it follows from lesson 0370. Per-agent scores can all be green while the run fails, because each agent did its own job on the input it was given and the input was wrong.

So the top-level measurement stays what module 1 built: did this request produce the right end state, on the same golden set. The per-agent trajectory scores from lesson 0370 are diagnostics underneath it, not a substitute for it. A dashboard of five healthy agents above a failing system is the multi-agent version of a green build over a broken product.

When you move from debugging one run to watching many, module 6's Monitoring a system whose failures are quiet takes over: the same trace, read for alerting rather than for triage.

The fourteen modes, the three categories, the corpus size and the agreement figure are Cemri et al.'s own results on their own annotated traces, and the modes are paraphrased here rather than reproduced because the paper's licence does not permit redistribution on this hub. The span names are from conventions marked Status: Development in a repository with no release and no tag, quoted here beside MCP revision 2026-07-28 precisely because the two currently disagree.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What distinguishes an invoke_agent client span from an internal one in the GenAI conventions?
A client span describes an agent invocation over a remote service and an internal one describes an invocation in the same process, which is the A2A boundary from lesson 0540 showing up in telemetry. Tool use gets its own execute_tool operation regardless of kind. Being the root is a position in the tree, not a kind. Failure is recorded in the span status, which is a different field entirely.
A multi-agent run returned a wrong answer. What does this lesson say to establish first?
The category names the fix, and only one of the three is ever fixed by a prompt. Rewording first is the habit this page exists to break. A bigger model is specifically what the study says does not fix the verification category. Token count is lesson 0500's axis and tells you what the run cost, not why it was wrong.

Practice about 45 minutes, with your capstone and its traces

Practice 1working

This is module 5's last increment, and it closes the module by making its failures legible.

Instrument every agent invocation with the span shape from section 1: one span per agent, carrying the agent's name, correlated by conversation, and marked remote where it crossed a boundary. If your capstone stayed single-agent, instrument the one agent and stop - the span shape is the same.

Break a delegation on purpose. Narrow the history filter from lesson 0510 until the worker no longer receives something it needs, and run a question that depends on it.

Then triage before you fix. Score the run on the card above, name the category, and only then make the change. Record the category, the fix and whether it worked in docs/decision-multi-agent.md.

Hint

Pin the convention version you instrument against in a comment beside the constants, and put the attribute names in one module. That is what makes a rename a one-file change rather than a search across your codebase.

If the broken run still produces a plausible answer, you have found something better than the exercise asked for: a failure that no per-agent score would catch, which is exactly section 4's point.

Show what a good triage looks like
  1. The category is usually obvious from the trace and invisible in the transcript. A worker that never received a value produced a confident answer without it, and the reply reads as a reasoning failure. The span that shows a short payload is what tells you otherwise.
  2. Deliberately breaking it is what proves the instrumentation. If your spans cannot show you a failure you caused on purpose, they will not show you one you did not.
  3. Write the category down before the fix, not after. Recording it afterwards means recording the fix you chose, which is the thing you were trying to check. The order is the exercise.
  4. A single-agent capstone still finishes this module here. The span shape, the golden-set measurement and the recorded decision from lesson 0500 are the whole deliverable, and none of them requires a second agent.

Sanity check. Your broken run should score badly on row three and reasonably on the others - it is one clear inter-agent misalignment. If it scores badly on all five, you broke more than the filter, and the triage is not yet telling you anything a rerun would not.

Your teacher. Give your assistant the span list from your broken run - names, kinds, durations, and the size of what crossed each edge - with the transcript withheld. Ask it to name the category and say which span made it think so. Then show it the transcript and ask again. Where the two answers differ, the transcript was misleading you, and that gap is the reason this page asks for the trace first.
