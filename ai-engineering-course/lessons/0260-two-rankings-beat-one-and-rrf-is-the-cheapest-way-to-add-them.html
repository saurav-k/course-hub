Module 02 · Retrieval quality and context engineering · Lesson 0260

Add a second ranking before you add a second model

working 8 min Grounded in the reciprocal rank fusion paper, SIGIR'09, and in the BM25 survey, Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval 3(4), DOI 10.1561/1500000019. · Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher

The one-minute version

  • Dense retrieval misses exact strings - part numbers, error codes, rare proper nouns. Keyword retrieval misses paraphrase. The failures barely overlap.
  • BM25 is not a legacy fallback. Across eighteen zero-shot datasets, BEIR found "BM25 is a robust baseline".
  • Fusing two rankings needs no scores, no training and no tuning: reciprocal rank fusion adds one over a constant plus the rank, and sorts.
  • The constant is 60, and the paper says it "was near-optimal, but that the choice was not critical". You do not have a hyperparameter here.
  • It costs one extra query per request and no new database: Postgres full-text search beside your vectors is enough.

1. The two retrievers fail on different queries

An embedding is a lossy address, as lesson 0130 put it, and the loss is systematic. A rare token - a part number, an error code, a person's surname, a flag name - contributes almost nothing to a 1,536-dimension average, so a query built out of that token retrieves passages that are about the same topic and do not contain the string.

A keyword index has the opposite failure. It matches the string exactly and cannot match a paraphrase, so a reader who asks "when can a refund be reversed" against a document that says "chargebacks may be contested within 60 days" gets nothing at all.

2. BM25 is a baseline, not a legacy

It is worth being precise about what the keyword side actually is, because "keyword search" sounds like a downgrade. BM25 scores a document by how often the query's terms appear in it, with two corrections: term frequency saturates, so the twentieth occurrence of a word adds almost nothing over the tenth, and the score is normalised by document length, so a long document does not win by containing more of everything.

Those two corrections have two constants, and the survey that defines the model is unusually candid about them: "the model provides no guidance on how these should be set. This may be regarded as a limitation of the model." It then reports that experiments "suggest that in general values such as 0.5 < b < 0.8 and 1.2 < k1 < 2 are reasonably good in many circumstances", while noting optimal values depend on the documents and queries.

So take the defaults your database ships, and do not spend a week tuning two constants whose own authors give you a range. The reason to reach for BM25 at all is BEIR's result across eighteen zero-shot datasets: "BM25 is a robust baseline". Not a strong result on one benchmark - a result that survives being moved to a domain nobody trained on, which is exactly your situation.

3. Reciprocal rank fusion, exactly

You now have two ranked lists whose scores are not comparable: a cosine similarity and a BM25 score live on different scales and normalising them into agreement is a project of its own. Reciprocal rank fusion sidesteps that by throwing the scores away and using only the positions.

RRFscore(d) = Σr ∈ R 1 ÷ (k + r(d)) Read as: for one document d, take each ranking r in the set of rankings R, find the position that ranking gave the document, add the constant k to it, and take one over the result. Add those up across the rankings. A document absent from a ranking contributes nothing from it.

The paper fixes k = 60, and says so in the same sentence that defines the formula: it "was fixed during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation". Their reason for the shape is that "while highly-ranked documents are more important, the importance of lower-ranked documents does not vanish as it would were, say, an exponential function used", and the constant itself exists because it "mitigates the impact of high rankings by outlier systems".

The part that saves you a week is their pilot result: the experiments "indicated that k = 60 was near-optimal, but that the choice was not critical". Take them at their word and check it once on your own corpus rather than treating it as a knob.

You need no new infrastructure for this. The pgvector README, at tag v0.8.6, points at exactly this arrangement - "Use together with Postgres full-text search for hybrid search" - and names reciprocal rank fusion or a cross-encoder as the two ways to combine the results, which is this lesson and the next one.

4. What this changed

This is the lesson in the module most likely to move your number, and the size of the move is a property of your corpus rather than of the technique. Corpora full of identifiers, versions, error codes and product names gain a lot. Corpora of flowing prose gain little, because the dense retriever was already finding what there was to find.

Run it as one change: add the keyword ranking, fuse at k = 60, re-run the same thirty questions, record recall@10 against the lesson 0240 row. Then change k to 10 and to 200 and re-run twice more, and expect the number to barely move - which is the paper's own finding and is worth confirming yourself, because it is what tells you to stop touching it.

The whiteboard framing of the same decision is Hybrid search, with Keyword search beside it, in AI System Design for Staff+ Interviews.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does reciprocal rank fusion use ranks rather than the retrievers' own scores?
A cosine similarity and a BM25 score have no common scale, so combining them means inventing a normalisation, and the fusion exists to avoid that. Ranks are not cheaper: both retrievers already computed scores to produce the ranks. The constant makes the weighting flatter than a raw score would, not steeper. A keyword index does return scores, which is why this is a design choice rather than a workaround.
Your fused results are barely better than dense alone. What is the most likely reason?
Fusion pays when the two retrievers fail on different queries, so a corpus of flowing prose with paraphrased questions gives the keyword side little to contribute and a small gain is the honest result. The constant is the least likely cause, because the paper reports the choice was not critical. A misconfigured index is worth checking second, and weighting is a change you would make only after establishing there was something to weight.

Practice about 50 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

Hybrid search is the module's best ratio of gain to code, and it needs no component you do not already run.

Add a full-text index over the same chunk text you already store, in the database you already have. Retrieve the top 100 from each of the two rankings for one query and print both lists side by side.

Fuse them with the formula from section 3 at k = 60. Write the fusion yourself; it is about ten lines, and reading them is worth more than importing them.

Re-run the golden set and record recall@10 against the previous row.

Then re-run at k = 10 and k = 200 and record both. Three numbers that agree to within the noise is the result the paper predicts.

Finally, classify your thirty questions into paraphrase-shaped and exact-string-shaped, and look at which group moved.

Hint

A document that appears in only one of the two rankings still gets a score - from that ranking alone. Handle it as a sum over the rankings that contain it, not as a join that drops it, or you will silently throw away every document only one retriever found, which is the entire benefit.

Contextualised chunks from lesson 0230 help the keyword side too, because the generated context adds the document's real nouns to the row. If you ran that lesson, this one starts from a better keyword index than you might expect.

Show what a good result looks like
  1. Check one query by hand first. Print the two top-tens and the fused top-ten. A document that was fourth on one list and first on the other should rise; a document only one retriever found should still be present. If the second is not true, your fusion is an intersection.
  2. Work one score through the arithmetic. A document ranked 4th by dense and 1st by keyword scores 1 ÷ 64 plus 1 ÷ 61, which is 0.03202. A document ranked 1st by dense only scores 1 ÷ 61, or 0.01639. Appearing well on both lists beats topping one of them, which is the behaviour you wanted.
  3. Expect the three k values to agree. If recall@10 at k = 10, 60 and 200 differs by more than your run-to-run noise, you have found something the paper did not, and the first thing to check is whether one of your rankings is much shorter than the other.
  4. The split by question type is the real finding. "recall@10 0.74 to 0.79, and all five of the gained questions contain an identifier" tells you more than the delta does, and it tells you what to do next: if none of your questions contain identifiers, this lesson is not where your remaining loss is.

Sanity check. The fused list should never be shorter than the longer of the two inputs, and every document in either input should have a score. If your fused list is about the size of the overlap, you joined instead of summing, and the number you are about to record is measuring the wrong thing.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to write the fusion in ten lines with no library, then to construct a test where one document is first on one list and absent from the other, and to show that the document survives. Then ask it to tag each of your thirty golden-set questions as paraphrase-shaped or string-shaped and to report the delta separately for the two groups, which is the only way to see whether the gain came from where the theory says it should.

Primary source to go deeper
