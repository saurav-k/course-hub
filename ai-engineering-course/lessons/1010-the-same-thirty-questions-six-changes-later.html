A table where every change helped is a table nobody kept honestly

frontier 7 min The second capstone stop. Nine changes, one table, and the rule that at least one row has to read zero. · The eval harness

The one-minute version

  • Module 2 changed one pipeline nine times against the same thirty questions. The deliverable is not the pipeline, it is the table.
  • One row per change: what changed, the delta, and the k, the set and the corpus version that make the delta a number at all.
  • At least one row must read zero, and module 2 hands you three that will. Naming a prompt does not retrieve a better passage.
  • A flat row is a finding rather than a failure, and reading one correctly is a skill: below the noise, corpus-dependent, or a no-op by design are three different answers.
  • The published gains you were shown are somebody else's corpus. Yours is the only one that decides what you keep.

1. The table is the deliverable

Module 2's promise was never "your system is better". It was you can say what made it better, and by how much. Those are different claims and only the second survives somebody asking.

So each row carries four things: the change, the metric with its k, the before and after on the named set and corpus version, and one sentence on whether you keep it. That last column is the one people leave out, and it is the one a reader in four weeks actually needs.

Two rows are worth writing carefully. Chunk size is a curve rather than a winner: record recall@10 at each size you tried, because a flat region means the choice does not matter and a slope means the next person to touch the parser will move your retrieval without noticing. Hybrid search is corpus-dependent by construction: corpora full of identifiers, versions and error codes gain a lot, corpora of flowing prose gain little, and the size of your gain is a fact about your documents rather than about the technique.

2. How to read a row that did not move

A flat row is not one thing, and treating all three cases the same is how a module of careful work turns into a shrug.

It could not move. Ingestion identity, structured output and prompt naming change no retrieval behaviour, so a zero is the correct and predicted result. Write it down and move on; the change was still worth making for reasons that are not this metric.

It moved less than your noise. A thirty-item set carries real sampling error, and a two-point change is inside it. Re-run the identical system a few times, look at the spread, and if your delta sits inside it you have measured nothing yet.

It is corpus-dependent and yours is the wrong shape for it. This is the interesting case, because the technique works and your documents are not the kind it works on. That is a finding worth a sentence, and it will save the next person a week.

3. Somebody else's ladder is not your ladder

Module 2 showed you a published ladder: a failure rate falling from 5.7% to 2.9% at ingest-time cost, and then to 1.9% once a reranker was added at request-time cost. Those are Anthropic's measurements on Anthropic's own corpora, and lesson 0270 is careful about what they license.

Two things transfer and one does not. What transfers is the shape - each rung buys less than the one before it - and the cost structure, where the first two rungs are paid once at ingest and the last is paid on every request your users wait for. What does not transfer is the magnitude. Your rungs will be different sizes and one of them may be flat.

So the table's last column matters more than its middle one. A change that bought 0.01 at ingest-time cost is probably worth keeping; the same 0.01 bought at request-time latency probably is not, and only your table can tell the two apart.

The 5.7%, 2.9% and 1.9% figures are Anthropic's published measurements on their own corpora and are evidence that the effect is real, never a target for yours. The six deltas in the chart are a worked example of a shape, not a measurement of any system. Your six numbers come from your thirty questions on your corpus, and they are the only ones that decide what you keep.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Naming and versioning your prompt produced a zero delta on recall@10. What should the table say?
Naming a prompt changes no retrieval behaviour, so a zero is the correct and predicted result and belongs in the table with that reading beside it. Omitting the row is what turns a record into a list of wins. Reverting throws away a change that buys an owner for the next regression, which was never measured by recall. Enlarging the set is the answer for a move you cannot resolve, not for one that could not exist.
Hybrid search gained 0.01 on your corpus and much more in the published write-up. What follows?
The size of the hybrid gain is a property of the documents: corpora dense in identifiers and error codes gain a lot, flowing prose gains little because the dense retriever was already finding what there was. A bug is worth ruling out and is not the first hypothesis. Calling the published figure optimistic misreads it: it was a measurement on a different corpus and was never a claim about yours. A larger set sharpens the estimate and does not change the underlying fact.

Practice about 30 minutes, mostly assembling

Capstonefrontier

Module 2 already produced these numbers one lesson at a time. This stop puts them in one place and checks that the place is honest.

Write evals/module-2-deltas.md with one row per change: what changed, metric and k, before, after, delta, the corpus version, whether you keep it, and whether the cost is paid at ingest or per request.

Confirm at least one row reads zero and carries its reading - predicted, below noise, or corpus-dependent. If no row is zero, go back and check that each measurement moved exactly one artefact.

Then measure your spread if you have not: five runs of the identical system, min, max and median recorded beside the table. Every delta smaller than that spread is provisional and should say so.

Hint

If your deltas do not add up to the difference between your module 1 baseline and your current number, something changed that is not in the table - usually the corpus, quietly re-exported by whoever maintains it.

Keep the chunk-size curve rather than only the winning size. It is the row most likely to be re-litigated by the next person who touches the parser.

Show what a finished table looks like
  1. Nine rows, not six. Every lesson gets a row even when the answer was "this cannot move retrieval", because a missing row reads as an untried idea rather than a predicted zero.
  2. The zeros are annotated, not just present. "0.00, predicted: no retrieval behaviour changed" and "0.00, below our measured spread of 0.03" are different rows and the difference is the whole point.
  3. The last column separates ingest cost from request cost. A gain paid once at ingest and the same gain paid on every request are different products, and the reranker row is where this bites.
  4. The deltas roughly reconcile with the baseline. If module 1 said 0.61 and you are now at 0.83, your rows should account for about 0.22. A large unexplained residual is a corpus that moved.
  5. The poisoned chunk from lesson 0280 is in the set permanently, with its expected behaviour being that the instruction is not followed. It is a regression test rather than a delta row, and it never comes out.

Sanity check. Your largest single delta should be smaller than the sum of the rest, and at least three rows should read zero. If one change accounts for nearly all your gain and everything else is flat, either that change was really several changes committed together, or the others were measured against a corpus that had already moved.

Your teacher. If your table has no zero rows, ask your agent to take each of your nine changes and say whether it could have moved a retrieval metric at all, before you look at the numbers again. If your deltas do not reconcile with your module 1 baseline, hand it the baseline record and the table and ask what artefact must have changed that neither document mentions.
