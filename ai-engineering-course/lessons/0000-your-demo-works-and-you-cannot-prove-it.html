Module 00 · Start here · Lesson 0000

Until you can see the search, you are tuning the writer

foundation 11 min Framed against the span vocabulary OpenTelemetry publishes for generative AI, read at commit fee465db and marked Development. · GenAI spans

The one-minute version

  • Your demo answered five questions and failed the sixth, and the sentence you reached for was "the model hallucinated". It almost never is.
  • A retrieval system is a search engine with a writer attached. The search decides which facts exist; the writer only decides the wording.
  • The reason you cannot tell them apart is that you have no trace and no golden set. This course installs both on the first day rather than the last.
  • Everything you send is dominated by retrieved text. The prompt you keep rewriting is a rounding error in the request, and this page shows the arithmetic.
  • Pick one corpus you actually care about before module 1. Pick it while reading this page.

1. The sixth question

The demo works. You asked it five things about your own documents and it answered all five, and you showed somebody and they were pleased. Then you asked a sixth, and the answer was fluent, confident and wrong, and the sentence you reached for was that the model hallucinated.

That sentence is a diagnosis, and it is almost always the wrong one. There are three ways to get a wrong answer out of a retrieval system and only the last of them is about the model: the passage that answers the question was never retrieved, or it was retrieved and the model did not use it, or it was retrieved and something else in the window contradicted it. The three have different fixes in different modules, and the only thing they have in common is that you cannot tell which one happened by reading the answer.

2. The same request, seen from inside

One question through your pipeline is not one event. It is at least three: the question is embedded, the index is searched, and a model is called with whatever came back. A print statement was enough while there was one of those. There are three now, and by module 4 there will be a loop around them.

The vocabulary for writing them down already exists and you do not have to invent it. OpenTelemetry publishes semantic conventions for generative AI, and they name six kinds of span: Inference, Embeddings, Retrievals, Fetch response, Memory and Execute tool. The one that matters most on this page is Retrievals, because it is the only place the search becomes visible: it carries the query text, the top_k that was asked for, and every document that came back with its own id and its own score.

Two things about that convention have to be said in the same breath as its attribute names, and this course says them every time. It is marked Status: Development, and it lives in a repository that has published no release and no tag at all. What you are committing to is not the spelling of gen_ai.retrieval.top_k; it is the shape underneath it, which is one span per stage with the query and the document scores recorded on the retrieval one. A rename costs you one file of constants.

3. The first number, and where it comes from

Now the arithmetic that makes the point sharp enough to act on. Take a request with a system prompt of about 200 tokens, an instruction of about 60, a question of about 20, and ten retrieved passages of about 400 tokens each. Those four numbers are assumptions and they are the only assumptions here: substitute your own and the conclusion does not move.

Ten passages at 400 tokens is 4,000 tokens. The other three slots together are 280. So 93% of what leaves your machine was chosen by the retrieval you have never inspected, and 7% is the prompt you have spent a week rewriting.

There is one honest question to ask before any of this matters, and it is the cheapest question in the course. Anthropic's own write-up of contextual retrieval says that a knowledge base under about 200,000 tokens, roughly 500 pages, can simply be put in the prompt with no retrieval at all. If your corpus is that small, you do not have a retrieval problem and this course will teach you to solve one you do not have. Measure your corpus before module 1 rather than after it.

4. Eight modules, read as edits to that picture

The order is a dependency chain and not a list. Module 1 gives you a pipeline, a trace and a thirty-question golden set, in that order, because a reader who reaches module 2 with no baseline cannot tell whether anything in module 2 worked. Module 2 then changes one thing at a time and makes you say what moved, which is the module's whole promise and the reason it is nine lessons rather than six.

Module 3 replaces the fixed sequence with a loop that decides its own path, and module 4 joins the two: retrieval becomes a tool the model calls, with a grader that can refuse and a pause a human can resume. Module 5 asks whether a second agent is worth its bill, and the correct answer is often no; a recorded refusal with the arithmetic behind it finishes that module. Module 6 is where it stops being a script: request shapes, three probes, four caches, one security pattern, a threshold in CI. Modules 7 and 8 price the whole thing at ten times its traffic, review it against six questions, and turn it into something a stranger can run.

The capstone is not a final project. It is one application you carry from here to the end, with a stop at the foot of most modules, and it is always measured on the same thirty questions you write in module 1.

5. What this course is not

Six neighbours in this hub already own material that would otherwise leak onto these pages. Every one of them is a link rather than a summary.

  • What a language model is belongs to LLM Papers, In Order and Mathematics for Machine Learning. Nothing here explains attention.
  • How a model is served, quantized and priced per token belongs to LLM Inference Optimization.
  • The whiteboard version of every design question here belongs to AI System Design for Staff+ Interviews, which this course links from almost every foundations page: they frame it, we do it.
  • Agents at the keyboard, agent-ready repositories and AI code review belong to AI Software Developer. No example in this course is a coding agent.
  • Context engineering across the turns of a loop, durable state and inter-agent security belong to Production Agent Engineering. This course's context engineering is one turn's retrieval payload.
  • Retries, circuit breakers and queues belong to Production Systems.

Four things this course owns that no sibling does: the agent-to-agent protocol, the host side of the Model Context Protocol, agent memory outside the context window, and the arithmetic for refusing a second agent.

Every published number in this course is somebody else's measurement on somebody else's corpus, and it is written that way on the page. A vendor's retrieval improvement, a research system's token multiplier and a benchmark's score are all evidence that an effect is real. None of them is a target for your corpus, and a course that printed one as a target would be teaching you to stop measuring on the day you started.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your system answers a question wrongly. Which single artefact tells you fastest whether the right passage was even a candidate?
The retrieval span carries the query, the top_k and every returned document with its id and score, so it answers "was it a candidate, and where did it rank" directly. The system prompt tells you what you asked for and nothing about what came back. A finish reason says how generation ended, which is a different failure. Latency says the request was slow, which is a third one.
On the arithmetic in section 3, which change makes the largest difference to what the model actually reads?
Retrieved passages are 4,000 of the 4,280 tokens, so the selection of those ten is by far the largest term and the only one that changes which facts are present at all. The other three options together move under seven percent of the request, and none of them can add a fact the retriever did not return. That is the whole argument of this page in one question.

Practice about 30 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1foundation

This is the capstone's first move, and doing it now costs half an hour and saves you a module.

Choose a corpus you actually care about: documentation you maintain, a policy set you are asked about, a research folder, anything where you would notice a wrong answer. If you have nothing to hand, this repository's own course content is the fallback, and it is licensed so you may redistribute it.

Measure two things before you build anything. First, the size of the corpus in tokens. Second, write ten questions you genuinely want answered, and for each one open the document and note the passage that answers it. Fifteen minutes, by hand, no generation.

Then run any retrieval you like over it and record, for those ten questions, how many times the passage you noted appeared in the top ten results. That fraction is your first number.

Hint

You do not need a vector database for this. Ten questions against a few hundred passages is small enough for exact search in a loop, and using the crude version first is the point: it is the baseline that everything in module 2 is measured against.

Count tokens rather than words for the corpus size, because the 200,000 figure in section 3 is in tokens.

Show what a good first measurement looks like
  1. The corpus size decides whether you have a problem. Under roughly 200,000 tokens, the honest answer is to put the whole thing in the prompt and skip retrieval entirely. Write the number down either way, because it is also the input to every cost estimate you will make in module 6.
  2. Ten hand-written questions beat a hundred generated ones. A generated question is answerable by the passage it was generated from, which measures nothing. A question you actually wanted answered is the only kind that can embarrass the system.
  3. The fraction is recall at ten. Six out of ten means four questions cannot be answered correctly no matter what you do to the prompt, because the evidence never arrived. That is the number module 1 turns into a proper thirty-question set and module 2 spends nine lessons moving.
  4. Write all three numbers into the repository, not into a notebook. Corpus size, recall at ten, and the date-free version of whatever retrieval you used. A baseline nobody can find later is a baseline nobody will argue with.

Sanity check. If your recall at ten came out at ten out of ten, the questions were too close to the wording of the passages. Rewrite three of them the way a colleague who has not read the documents would ask, and measure again. A first number that is comfortable is a first number that measured nothing.

Your teacher. If the three failure modes in section 1 still feel like one thing, ask your agent to take a question your system got wrong and, for each of the three, say what evidence would prove that mode and what evidence would rule it out. If the arithmetic in section 3 felt like a trick, ask it to redo the table with your own passage size and your own top-k, and to say at what top-k the retrieved share drops below half.
