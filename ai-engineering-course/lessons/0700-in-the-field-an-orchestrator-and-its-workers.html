Module 07 · Agentic AI system design · Lesson 0700

A fan-out buys quality with tokens, and one team published the exchange rate

frontier 8 min Built only from Anthropic’s own public account of the system, and every number in it is that team’s measurement on that team’s own work. · How we built our multi-agent research system

The one-minute version

  • A fan-out is a lead agent that spawns workers, and then synthesises what they bring back. Nothing else about the shape is load-bearing.
  • The published price: agents use about 4x more tokens than a chat, and multi-agent systems about 15x more. Both figures are approximate in the source and workload-dependent by construction.
  • The published gain: a lead-and-workers system outperformed a single agent by 90.2% on that team’s own internal research eval. That is not a benchmark result and cannot be quoted as one.
  • The running cost is the part nobody budgets for: long-lived state, non-determinism between runs, and deploying without killing agents mid-flight.
  • You already have the traces to compute your own multiplier. Do it before you build a second agent, not after.

1. The shape, before the price

Orchestrator-workers is one of five workflow patterns Anthropic names in Building effective agents, alongside prompt chaining, routing, parallelization and evaluator-optimizer. Their definition of it is short: a central model “dynamically breaks down tasks, delegates them to worker LLMs, and synthesizes their results”, and the case for it is that the subtasks “aren’t pre-defined, but determined by the orchestrator based on the specific input”.

That last clause is the whole test. If you can write the subtasks down in advance, you want a workflow and not a fan-out, and you have just saved yourself the multiplier below.

2. The price, as its builders state it

The sentence to carry out of the whole post is this one: “agents typically use about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats”.

Read the hedges. About is in the source, and both figures are workload-dependent by construction, so they are an order of magnitude rather than a coefficient. They are also that organisation’s measurement of that organisation’s traffic, which means they are evidence that the effect is real and they are not a number your finance team should be given. What transfers is the shape: the step from a chat to an agent is large, and the step from an agent to several agents is larger than the count of agents suggests, because each worker carries its own instructions, its own tool definitions and its own accumulated results.

3. What the tokens bought, and what that licenses

The other half of the exchange rate is stated just as plainly: a “multi-agent system with Claude Opus 4 as the lead agent and Claude Sonnet 4 subagents outperformed single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2% on our internal research eval”.

That is a large number and it is doing less work than it looks like it is doing. It is an internal eval: the questions are not published, the scoring is not published, and the comparison is between that vendor’s own models on that vendor’s own task. It is a legitimate primary source for what that team measured, and it is not a benchmark result, so it cannot be compared with anybody else’s number and it cannot be quoted as a figure your system would also reach.

The honest reading is directional and it is still useful: on open-ended research, where the subtasks genuinely cannot be written down in advance, a fan-out was worth a large multiple to the team that measured it. That is exactly the condition their own workflow-pattern guidance names, which is why the two documents agree.

A published gain and a published cost from the same organisation are the strongest evidence you will get, and they are still one organisation’s account of one workload. The one number in this lesson that is allowed to decide anything is the multiplier you compute from your own traces in the practice problem below.

4. The cost that is not in the token bill

Three operational problems are named in the post, and each of them is a thing your single-agent system does not have.

Agents hold state for a long time. They “can run for long periods of time, maintaining state across many tool calls”, and the consequence is stated bluntly: “minor system failures can be catastrophic for agents”. A retry that is free for a stateless request is not free for a worker forty tool calls into a task.

Runs do not repeat. Agents “make dynamic decisions and are non-deterministic between runs, even with identical prompts”, which the post says directly “makes debugging harder”. Every reproduction step you rely on today assumes the opposite.

Deploys kill work in flight. Their answer is rainbow deployments, “gradually shifting traffic from old to new versions while keeping both running simultaneously”, which is a deployment strategy you adopt because a request now outlives a pod. The general mechanism belongs to Production Systems; what is new here is that the unit you must not interrupt is a half-finished agent rather than a half-finished HTTP request.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which condition, from the same team’s workflow guidance, actually argues for an orchestrator-workers shape?
The source draws the line at subtasks that are “determined by the orchestrator based on the specific input”, so unpredictable decomposition is the condition. A slow task is a request-shape problem from module 6 and parallelism inside one agent is cheaper. A long prompt is a context problem. A large corpus is a retrieval problem, and sharding an index needs no second agent at all.
A colleague quotes the 90.2% figure in a design document. What is the single most important correction?
The source says “our internal research eval”: unpublished questions, unpublished scoring, so it cannot be compared with anything or promised to anybody. It does compare a lead-and-workers system against a single agent, so the architectures are the thing varied. Nothing suggests the measurements were taken at different times, and the figure is explicitly about research quality rather than speed.

Practice about 25 minutes, with your traces

Practice 1frontier

Your capstone has been traced since module 1 and deployed since module 6, so the numbers below are already on disk.

Compute your own multiplier. Take thirty served requests. For each, sum the input and output tokens across every model call in the trace. Take the median, and call it your agent cost. Then take the same thirty questions and answer each with a single model call over the packed context, with no tools and no loop, and take that median too.

Then write the paragraph. One paragraph, in the repository, stating your measured ratio, the fan-out width you were considering, the projected cost at that width, and the decision. A decision against is a complete answer.

Hint

Use the median rather than the mean. One runaway trace with forty tool calls will drag a mean somewhere no request actually is, and the runaway is a separate finding worth its own line.

If your traces do not carry token counts per span, that is the finding, and fixing it is a smaller job than this measurement.

Show what a good answer looks like
  1. Compute the single-call baseline. Suppose the median single call is 5,200 tokens: 4,600 in and 600 out. That is one number and it is yours.
  2. Compute the agent median. Suppose the median traced request is 21,000 tokens across four model calls. The ratio is 21,000 ÷ 5,200 = 4.0, which happens to land near the published figure for one agent and would be just as informative if it landed at 2 or at 9.
  3. Project the fan-out, do not measure it. Four workers at the same per-worker cost, plus a lead that reads every worker’s summary, is roughly 4 × 21,000 plus a synthesis pass. Call it 95,000 tokens, and say out loud that the synthesis term is the one you guessed.
  4. Divide, and then decide. 95,000 ÷ 5,200 is about 18. Now ask the only question that matters: on your own thirty questions, is the answer 18 times better, or is it slightly better and much slower to debug.

Sanity check. Your measured single-agent ratio should land somewhere between about 2 and about 10. Below 2 you are probably counting only one call in the trace, and above 10 with no fan-out means the loop is not terminating, which is a module 3 defect and not a pricing finding.

Your teacher. If the difference between a workflow and an agent still feels like a matter of degree, ask your agent to take three tasks your system handles and, for each one, write the subtask list in advance. The ones it can write are workflows. If the 90.2% figure still feels quotable, ask it to write the sentence you would put in a design document, then to strike every clause that is not in the source.
