Module 01 · Foundations · Lesson 0130

Nearby addresses are a hypothesis, and it is model-specific

foundation 10 min Grounded in the benchmark whose own conclusion is that no embedding model wins everywhere, read at v3. · Muennighoff et al., arXiv:2210.07316

The one-minute version

  • An embedding is an address, and cosine similarity is a claim that things stored near each other mean similar things. That claim is a property of the model, not of language.
  • MTEB benchmarked 33 models over 8 tasks, 58 datasets and 112 languages and concluded that no method dominates across tasks. Leaderboard rank is therefore not a selection method.
  • Four criteria that are not rank: dimension count against index cost, context length against chunk size, licence and hosting, and whether you can afford to re-embed everything.
  • Changing the embedding model invalidates the whole index. It is the most expensive reversible decision in the system, and the arithmetic is on this page.
  • A question and a document are different kinds of text, and exploiting that asymmetry is a real technique rather than a trick.

1. What the number actually is

An embedding model reads a passage and emits a fixed-length list of floats. That list is an address in a space the model constructed while it was trained, and its only useful property is relative: passages the model considers similar land near each other.

Two consequences follow and both matter more than they sound. The first is that the space is lossy on purpose. Several hundred to a few thousand numbers cannot preserve a paragraph, so the model keeps whatever its training objective rewarded and discards the rest. A distinction your corpus depends on and its training data did not may simply not be represented. The second is that cosine similarity is a choice, not a fact. It is the comparison the model was trained under, so using it is honouring the model's own contract, and the number it returns has no meaning outside that model's space. A score of 0.83 from one model and 0.83 from another are not comparable quantities.

2. Why the leaderboard is not a method

The reflex when choosing an embedding model is to open a leaderboard and take the top row. The benchmark that leaderboard comes from disagrees with the reflex, in its own abstract.

MTEB was built because embeddings were being evaluated on a narrow slice of tasks and the results were being generalised. It spans 8 embedding tasks over 58 datasets and 112 languages, and its authors benchmarked 33 models across all of it. Their stated finding is that no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks, and they draw the conclusion explicitly: the field has not converged on a universal text embedding.

So a rank is an average over tasks that are not your task, on corpora that are not your corpus, in a mix of languages that is not your mix. It is a shortlist, and it is a good one. It is not a decision, and the decision costs one afternoon: embed a few hundred of your own chunks with two candidates and count.

3. Four criteria that are not rank

Dimensions against index cost. The address length is the single largest term in what your index costs to hold and to scan, and it is chosen for you by the model. The calculator below turns that into a number for your corpus.

Context length against chunk size. A model with a 512-token input truncates anything longer, silently, and a truncated chunk is a chunk whose second half is unsearchable. This constrains module 2's chunking decision before module 2 starts.

Licence and hosting. Whether you may run the model where your documents are is a legal and architectural question that no benchmark scores, and it eliminates candidates faster than quality does.

Whether you can afford to change your mind. This is the criterion people discover last and it deserves its own section.

4. The most expensive reversible decision

Changing embedding model does not migrate. There is no conversion between two models' spaces, so every vector in the index has to be recomputed from the original text, which means the original text has to still exist, keyed by the identifier lesson 0120 made you keep.

Work the cost with the sliders above: a corpus of 13,000 chunks at 400 tokens each is about 5.2 million tokens to re-embed, plus the time to rebuild the index, plus a full re-run of the evaluation because every score has moved. It is reversible, which is why it is a two-way door and not a one-way one, and it is expensive enough that people avoid making it and then live for two years with a model they picked in an afternoon.

The practical consequence is a rule: keep the raw text, keyed, forever. An index you cannot rebuild from source is an index you can never change your mind about, and that is a decision you will have made by accident.

5. A question is not a document

One more property of the space is worth knowing now because module 2 uses it. A question and the passage that answers it are different kinds of text. The question is short, interrogative and uses the asker's vocabulary; the passage is long, declarative and uses the author's. Embedding both with the same model and expecting them to land near each other asks the model to bridge that gap on its own.

HyDE is the published exploitation of this. Given a query, it asks a language model to write a hypothetical document answering it, embeds that, and searches with it, so the comparison is document-to-document rather than question-to-document. The paper's own account of why it works is worth quoting properly: the generated document "captures relevance patterns but is unreal and may contain false details", and the encoder's dense bottleneck is what filters the false details out on the way to a real neighbourhood. It is not on your critical path today. It is the reason lesson 0260's hybrid search is not the only answer to a paraphrase failure.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

MTEB benchmarked 33 models over 8 tasks. What does its abstract conclude about ranking them?
The paper states plainly that no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks, and concludes the field has not converged. Size correlating with quality is a common belief the benchmark does not assert. Transfer between tasks is exactly what MTEB was built to test and exactly what it found is unreliable. Language coverage is one of its dimensions and is not the finding.
You want to switch embedding model on a live index. What makes this expensive?
There is no mapping between two models' spaces, so the only route is re-embedding every chunk from the source text, which is why keeping the raw text keyed is a rule. Differing dimensions are a symptom rather than the cost. Converting scores is impossible rather than merely expensive, so it is not a step anybody performs. The index format is usually unchanged and is the cheapest part.

Practice about 40 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1foundation

Choose your embedding model by measuring rather than by reading, on your own corpus.

Take 200 to 500 chunks from the loader you wrote in lesson 0120 and the ten questions you wrote in lesson 0000. Embed the chunks with two candidate models. For each question, retrieve the top ten under each model and record whether the passage you noted as the answer appears.

Report two fractions, one per model, and the footprint each index would need at your full corpus size using the calculator above. Then write one sentence in the charter naming the model you chose and the number that chose it.

Hint

Pick two models that differ on something you care about rather than two that sit next to each other on a leaderboard: a small one against a large one, or a hosted one against one you can run where the documents are. If the two you compare are near-identical, the comparison cannot change your decision and the afternoon is wasted.

Ten questions is a small sample and the difference you measure has real uncertainty. That is fine at this stage: you are looking for a model that is obviously worse, not for a statistically significant winner.

Show what the comparison should tell you
  1. A tie is the most likely outcome and it is a useful one. If two models retrieve the same passages for your ten questions, the decision is settled by the other three criteria: dimensions, context length, and where you may run it. Choose the cheaper one and record that the quality difference was not measurable at this sample size.
  2. A large gap usually means a vocabulary mismatch rather than a quality difference. A model trained mostly on English prose does poorly on a corpus full of part numbers, error codes and internal acronyms. That is not a defect; it is the hypothesis in the page title failing on your corpus, and it is an argument for the lexical half of the hybrid search in lesson 0260.
  3. Compute the footprint before you commit, not after. A 3072-dimension model over 100,000 chunks is 1.2 GB of raw vectors at four bytes a number, which changes what infrastructure you need. That is a design constraint discovered in an afternoon or in production.
  4. Write the losing number down too. In four months somebody will propose the model you rejected, and a recorded measurement ends that conversation in a minute.
  5. Keep the raw text, keyed, before you embed anything. If this experiment convinces you to switch later, the raw text is what makes switching possible at all.

Sanity check. Both models should get most of your ten questions right, because ten hand-written questions over a few hundred chunks is an easy task. If either scores near zero, you have a bug rather than a result: check that you embedded the chunks and the questions with the same model, and that you did not compare a query vector against a document vector from a different run.

Your teacher. Take the question your two models disagreed on most and ask your agent to write, in the author's voice, the passage it thinks would answer it. Compare that with the passage that actually answers it. The gap between the two is the gap the embedding has to bridge, and it is also the intuition behind the hypothetical-document technique in section 5.
