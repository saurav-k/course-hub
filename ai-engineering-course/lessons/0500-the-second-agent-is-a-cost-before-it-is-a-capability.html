Module 05 · Multi-agent systems · Lesson 0500

Price the second agent before you draw it

working 8 min Two organisations that both ship these systems have published opposite advice. Both are quoted here at their own ceiling, and the numbers are theirs rather than this course's. · How we built our multi-agent research system

The one-minute version

  • The pressure is real: one window holding a system prompt, twenty tool schemas, retrieved passages and a growing trace gets worse as it gets longer.
  • The relief is expensive. Anthropic reports that agents use about 4× the tokens of a chat and multi-agent systems about 15×, on its own systems.
  • The same post reports a large win on an internal evaluation set that is not published, so it is a company's claim about its own measurement rather than a result you can check.
  • Cognition, shipping the same class of system, published the opposite advice: share full traces, and prefer one agent with a compression step.
  • This module does not resolve that. It gives you the arithmetic, and a recorded decision not to split is a complete answer.

1. Why the idea arrives

It arrives from the window. By the end of module 3 one request carries a system prompt, a tool catalogue, retrieved passages, a growing trace and possibly a memory file, and Anthropic's own write-up on context engineering names what happens next: as the number of tokens in the window rises, "the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases".

So the instinct is to divide. Give retrieval to one agent, comparison to another, drafting to a third, and let each keep a small clean window. That instinct is sound and it is also the most expensive instinct in this course.

2. The case for, at its own numbers

Anthropic published an account of a research system built as an orchestrator with parallel subagents, and reports that the multi-agent configuration "outperformed single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2%" on an internal evaluation.

Read that sentence twice, because how you read it decides whether this page taught you anything. It is a company's measurement of its own system on an evaluation set that is not published. It is good evidence that the effect can be real on research-shaped work. It is not a number you can reproduce, compare against, or promise to anybody, and this course will not print it as if it were.

The same post is unusually candid about the bill, and those figures are the useful part: agents "typically use about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats", and "token usage by itself explains 80% of the variance" in performance.

That last clause is the one to carry. If most of the gain is explained by spending more tokens, then a fair comparison is not one agent against five - it is one agent against one agent allowed to spend the same tokens.

3. The case against, from somebody shipping the same thing

Cognition published the opposite recommendation, and it is worth taking as seriously as the first because it comes from the same kind of work rather than from a critic. Walden Yan's write-up sets out two principles: share context, and share full agent traces rather than individual messages; and actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results.

The argument behind the second is the interesting one. When two subagents work in parallel on pieces of one task, each makes unstated assumptions the other cannot see, and the orchestrator gets back two outputs built on incompatible premises. Nothing errored. The work simply does not fit together.

Their recommended alternative is a single-threaded linear agent, and where the task is too long for one window, a dedicated model that compresses the history into the decisions and events that matter rather than a team that divides it.

4. The decision rule that survives both

You do not have to pick a side, because both sources agree on more than the headlines suggest. Anthropic's own post states the boundary: domains "requiring all agents to share the same context or involve many dependencies between agents are not a good fit", most coding work "involves fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research", and the value of the task has to justify the cost.

That is Cognition's argument, written by the company making the case for multi-agent systems. So the rule is:

Split when the subtasks are genuinely independent and mostly read-only, so a worker cannot invalidate another worker's assumption. Do not split when the agents need the same context, or when one depends on the other's output. And only split when the task is worth the multiplier, which is the part you can compute rather than argue.

The 90.2% figure is Anthropic's claim about its own system on an internal evaluation set that has not been published. It cannot be reproduced, compared, or used as a target, and this course cites it only as evidence that the effect can exist on research-shaped work. The token multipliers in section 2 are from the same post and are also Anthropic's measurement of its own systems. Your multiplier is whatever your trace says, and module 3 already taught you how to read it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Anthropic reports that token usage alone explains 80% of the performance variance. What does that imply for a comparison?
If spending explains most of the variance, the honest control is one agent allowed to spend what the team spends, otherwise you are measuring the budget rather than the architecture. It cannot mean more agents is always worth it - that is the claim the variance figure undercuts. Token counts matter enormously here, which is why the figure exists. Model choice is named in the same sentence as one of two lesser factors, not the only one.
On the rule in section 4, which task is the best candidate for splitting across agents?
Independent and read-only is exactly the case where a worker cannot invalidate another worker's assumption, which is the failure Cognition's second principle describes. Drafting then checking is sequential and belongs in one agent. A task whose second step needs the first has a dependency, which Anthropic's own post names as a poor fit. A shared context is the other case that post rules out.

Practice about 40 minutes, with your capstone and its traces

Practice 1working

This is module 5's first increment, and for many readers it is the only one they will need.

Read three numbers out of your own traces, not out of this page: the average prompt tokens one request spends today, the average number of turns it takes, and the fraction of requests that hit the turn ceiling you set in lesson 0310.

Then project the team. Take the one feature you would actually split, name the workers, and use the calculator above with your own tokens-per-turn to get a per-thousand-request figure for both shapes.

Write docs/decision-multi-agent.md in your repository: the measured single-agent cost, the projected multi-agent cost, which of section 4's three conditions the feature meets, and the go or no-go. If the answer is no, say so in one sentence and stop - that file is the deliverable.

Hint

The tokens-per-turn figure is not the tokens in your first turn. Lesson 0310's arithmetic applies: each turn re-sends the history, so take the total prompt tokens for a run and divide by its model calls.

If you cannot name a feature whose subtasks are independent and read-only, that is the finding, and it is the most common one. Write it down rather than inventing a feature that fits.

Show what a good decision record looks like
  1. Three measured numbers, one projection, one sentence. The record is short by design. Its value is that in six months somebody can see what was true when the decision was made, and re-run the arithmetic when it stops being true.
  2. The honest comparison gives the single agent the same budget. Before you conclude that three workers beat one, raise the one agent's turn ceiling until it spends what the three would, and re-run your module 1 golden set. This is what the 80% variance figure demands, and it is often where the case collapses.
  3. Name which of the three conditions fails. "Independent and read-only: no, the comparison step needs the retrieval step's output" is a reason somebody can argue with. "It felt like overkill" is not, and it will be re-litigated.
  4. A no is a finished module. The course's mission records this: a capstone whose recorded decision is single-agent, with the arithmetic written down, is complete. Read the four lessons that follow anyway - you cannot defend the refusal without knowing what you refused.

Sanity check. Your projected multi-agent figure should land somewhere near a small multiple of the single-agent one - roughly three to five times for a three-worker design. If you got a number close to 1x you multiplied the workers into the turns rather than on top of them, and if you got 15x you have reproduced Anthropic's figure rather than computed your own.

Your teacher. Describe your candidate feature to your assistant and ask it to argue both sides explicitly: first as Anthropic's post would, then as Cognition's would, naming which assumption each worker would have to make and where the two could conflict. Then ask which of the two arguments applies to your feature and why. If it cannot name a concrete conflicting assumption, your subtasks are probably independent enough to split, and that is the useful outcome.
