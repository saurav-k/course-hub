Module 02 · Retrieval quality and context engineering · Lesson 0220

Sweep the chunk size rather than arguing about it

working 9 min Grounded in a controlled evaluation of semantic chunking, read at arXiv:2410.13070v1, and in Anthropic's own implementation notes. · arXiv:2410.13070

The one-minute version

  • The cut is the one decision in the pipeline that is irreversible at query time. Nothing downstream can retrieve half a chunk or join two.
  • Two forces pull against each other: small chunks embed precisely, and large chunks carry enough to answer. The first is a retrieval constraint and the second is a generation one.
  • Semantic chunking is sold as the free upgrade and a controlled study says its computational costs are not justified by consistent gains.
  • The cost half is arithmetic you can do before you run anything: halving the chunk size doubles the rows and doubles the embedding bill.
  • The quality half is not arithmetic. Sweep four sizes on your own golden set and you leave with a curve rather than an opinion.

1. The two forces, and why they are different problems

An embedding is one vector for a whole passage. Put four topics in a passage and the vector sits somewhere between all four, close to none of them, which is why small chunks retrieve precisely. That is the retrieval force, and on its own it argues for cutting small.

The generation force argues the other way, and it is the one that gets forgotten. The model can only use what arrives. A chunk cut so tightly that the sentence answering the question is in it but the subject of that sentence is in the previous chunk has retrieved successfully and answered nothing. A reader chasing recall alone will drive the cut down until this starts happening and will see it as a generation failure.

So the two forces are not two settings of one dial that you balance by taste. They are two different failure modes, they show up in two different metrics, and you can tell them apart: retrieval recall falls when the cut is too big, faithfulness falls when the cut is too small.

2. Four families, and the one that is oversold

Fixed-size cuts every N tokens and is the baseline. Fixed-with-overlap repeats the last slice of each chunk at the head of the next, which buys back the straddling answer above at the price of storing the overlap twice. Structural cuts on the document's own boundaries - headings, sections, list items, code blocks - which is why lesson 0200 insisted the parser keep them. Semantic cuts where the meaning changes, by embedding sentences and looking for the seam.

Semantic chunking is the one everybody wants to try, and it is the one with a controlled result against it. Qu, Tu and Bao evaluated it across document retrieval, evidence retrieval and retrieval-based answer generation, and concluded that "the computational costs associated with semantic chunking are not justified by consistent performance gains".

Read that carefully, because it is routinely confused with a different claim. It says nothing about adding words to a chunk, which is what lesson 0230 is about and which has evidence in its favour. Moving where the cut falls and writing context into the chunk are two different interventions with two different bills, and hearing them as one finding is how a reader ends up believing that the expensive option always wins.

3. The half you can compute before you run anything

Cost is arithmetic. Take a corpus of two million tokens, which is a mid-sized documentation set. The number of chunks is the corpus divided by the chunk size, so halving the size doubles everything downstream: rows in the index, vectors to store, embedding calls at ingest, and rows to re-embed the day you change model.

chunks = corpus_tokens ÷ chunk_tokens Read as: the number of chunks is the size of the whole corpus in tokens, divided by the number of tokens you put in each chunk. Overlap adds to this in proportion to the fraction repeated.

4. The sweep is the method

Everything above narrows the decision and none of it makes it. What settles it is running your own thirty questions at four chunk sizes and reading the curve, because the right size depends on how your documents are written and nobody has measured your documents.

Anthropic's own implementation notes say only that chunks are "usually no more than a few hundred tokens" and that "The choice of chunk size, chunk boundary, and chunk overlap can affect retrieval performance". That is a range and a warning, not a number, and it is the honest shape of the published advice.

Four points is enough for a first curve: 200, 400, 800 and 1,600 tokens, each re-ingested from scratch - which lesson 0200 made cheap - and each scored on the same golden set with nothing else changed.

5. What this changed

This is the first lesson in the module whose number can genuinely move, and the size of the move is the point of running it. Record recall@10 at each of the four sizes and keep the curve, not just the winner: the shape tells you whether you are near a flat region, where the choice does not matter and you should pick the cheaper one, or on a slope, where the next reader who changes a parser will move your retrieval without noticing.

Record the faithfulness score alongside it. If recall rises as the chunks get smaller while faithfulness falls, you have found the two forces in your own data, and the crossing point is your answer rather than anybody else's.

The whiteboard framing of the same trade-off, stated as a design question rather than as a sweep, is Chunking and Chunk overlap in AI System Design for Staff+ Interviews.

The paper's conclusion is about the cost not being justified by consistent gains, which is weaker than saying fixed-size wins and much weaker than saying semantic wins. Practice about 45 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

This is the module's first real measurement, and the idempotent pipeline from lesson 0200 is what makes it affordable.

Re-ingest your corpus at four chunk sizes: 200, 400, 800 and 1,600 tokens, fixed-size with no overlap, changing nothing else. Keep each run's row count.

Score every run on the same golden set. Record recall@10 and, if you built the judge in lesson 0170, faithfulness as well.

Plot recall against chunk size and look at the shape before you look at the winner.

Then add overlap at your best size - a fifth of the chunk is a reasonable first try - and record what it cost in rows and what it bought in recall.

Hint

Four full re-ingests is four embedding bills. Run the sweep on a subset of the corpus that still covers every golden-set question, then repeat only the winning size on the whole thing.

If two sizes score within the noise of each other, they are the same answer and you should take the larger one, because it costs fewer rows.

Show what a good result looks like
  1. Expect a curve with a knee, not a straight line. Recall usually improves as the cut gets smaller and then stops improving, because past some point the answer no longer fits in a chunk at all. The knee is the number you want and it is a property of your documents.
  2. Check the row counts against the arithmetic. Corpus tokens divided by chunk size should predict each run's row count to within the ragged ends of documents. If run four has more rows than a quarter of run one, something is splitting on structure as well as size and you are sweeping two variables.
  3. Read the questions that changed, not only the total. Thirty questions and a move from 0.61 to 0.68 is two questions. Open those two and confirm the reason is the cut rather than a coincidence of the retriever's ordering.
  4. Overlap is a cost you can state exactly. A fifth of each chunk repeated is a fifth more rows and a fifth more storage, paid forever, for whatever recall it bought on those thirty questions. Write both halves in the table.

Sanity check. Halving the chunk size should roughly double the row count and leave the total tokens embedded almost unchanged, because it is the same corpus. If your embedding bill doubled as well, you are counting a per-call overhead rather than tokens, and the per-call overhead is a real cost that belongs in the table under its own name.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to run the four-point sweep as one script that re-ingests, scores and writes a row per size, so the whole thing is one command you can repeat after any parser change. Then ask it to find the golden-set questions whose result changed between the best and second-best size, and to print the retrieved passages for both, side by side. That comparison is where you learn what your documents are actually like.
