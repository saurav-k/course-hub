A second pass helps when it carries evidence the first pass did not have

working 9 min Two peer-reviewed results disagree here. Both are quoted at their own ceiling, and the reconciliation offered in section 4 is this course's reading rather than either paper's claim. · Reflexion

The one-minute version

  • Reflection is the evaluator-optimizer shape from lesson 0300: one call generates, another criticises, and the loop goes round again.
  • One well-known result reports a large gain: 91% pass@1 against a stated 80% baseline, on a benchmark that runs the code and reports whether it passed.
  • Another reports the opposite: without external feedback, models "struggle to self-correct" and performance sometimes degrades.
  • Both papers are careful and neither is wrong. They differ in whether the critic had anything to look at besides the answer.
  • The rule this course takes from that: name your external signal before you add the pass. No signal, no pass.

1. The mechanism, in two published forms

Madaan et al.'s Self-Refine (arXiv 2303.17651v2) is the simplest statement of it: one model produces an output, produces feedback on its own output, and revises, with no extra training and no separate critic model.

Shinn et al.'s Reflexion (arXiv 2303.11366v4) adds the part that makes it an agent technique rather than a prompting one. Agents "verbally reflect on task feedback signals, then maintain their own reflective text in an episodic memory buffer to induce better decision-making in subsequent trials", and the paper is explicit that this reinforces the agent "not by updating weights, but instead through linguistic feedback".

Read the phrase task feedback signals carefully, because it is the whole lesson. The paper says the framework is flexible about the type of signal - scalar or free-form language - and about the source, external or internally simulated. It does not say the source does not matter.

2. The result people quote

Reflexion's headline is worth quoting exactly, at its own ceiling and no further. The paper reports that Reflexion "achieves a 91% pass@1 accuracy on the HumanEval coding benchmark, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art GPT-4 that achieves 80%". Both figures are the authors' own, measured on that benchmark and on the models available to them.

Now notice what HumanEval is. It is a set of programming problems with unit tests, so a candidate answer can be executed and the result is a fact rather than an opinion. The reflection step is being handed a test outcome.

That is the single most important thing about the number, and it is also the reason it does not transfer to your service unchanged. Your question-answering system has no compiler. Whatever plays the part of the unit test has to be something you built - and module 1 already made you build one.

3. The result people do not quote

Huang et al. (arXiv 2310.01798v2) examined the same idea and reached the opposite conclusion under a narrower condition. Their subject is intrinsic self-correction, defined as an attempt to correct an initial response "based solely on its inherent capabilities, without the crutch of external feedback".

Their finding, in their own words: in the context of reasoning, models "struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction".

Take that seriously rather than as a caveat. It says a reflection pass with no external evidence is not merely a wasted turn - it can be a worse answer produced at a higher price, and both papers appeared in the same period and were reviewed.

4. Reading the two together

This section is this course's reading of the two papers, not a claim either of them makes. Both were opened, both are quoted above at their own ceilings, and neither one addresses the other's setup directly.

The reading is this. The two experiments differ in whether the critique had access to information the original answer did not contain. Reflexion's coding results are reflection over an execution outcome. Huang et al. deliberately removed exactly that, and named its absence in their definition. Under that reading the papers are not in conflict at all: they are a measurement of the same loop with and without one edge, and they disagree about the loop only if you drop the edge from the description.

What makes this a reading rather than a fact is that neither paper ran the other's experiment. Treat it as a hypothesis that decides your design and that you can test on your own golden set in about an hour, which is what the practice below asks for.

5. Name the signal first

The practical rule follows directly and it is short: before you add a reflection pass, name the thing outside the model that will tell it it was wrong. If you cannot name one, you are building the upper loop.

Four candidates are usually already sitting in a system like yours. A schema validator, which module 2 gave you when structured outputs arrived. A retrieval check: did the answer cite a passage that was actually returned. Your module 1 evaluator, run inline on one answer rather than in a batch. And a person, which is module 4's subject and is the strongest signal of the four.

One caution about the third of those. Module 1 establishes that a judge is a model with its own biases and needs its own evaluation, so a judge used as a reflection signal is external in the sense that matters here - it saw something the draft did not - and is not ground truth. Both things are true at once, and the page that argues otherwise is the page that turns a judge score into a target.

Section 4 is this course's reading and not a finding. Shinn et al. report their result on an executable benchmark; Huang et al. report theirs on intrinsic self-correction in reasoning tasks. Neither paper ran the other's experiment, so the claim that the presence of an external signal is what separates them is an inference drawn here from two sources that were opened and quoted. It is written down as a hypothesis to test on your own golden set rather than as a settled result, and if your measurement disagrees with it, your measurement is the better evidence.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

This page argues that one difference explains why the two published results point in opposite directions. Which?
Reflexion's coding result reflects over an execution outcome, and Huang et al. explicitly removed external feedback from their definition, so the presence of an outside signal is the difference this page points at - and section 4 labels that as this course's reading rather than either paper's claim. Model size varies in both and neither paper attributes the effect to it. More retries without a signal is more of the loop that does not work. Difficulty would change the scores, not their direction.
You are about to add a reflection pass to your answer path. What does this lesson say to do first?
If you cannot name the thing outside the model that will say the answer is wrong, you are building the loop that Huang et al. measured degrading. Raising the ceiling buys more turns of whichever loop you built. A larger model still cannot see evidence that is not in front of it. A second critic model is another model with the same view of the same answer, which is the upper loop with a bigger bill.

Practice about 45 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

Run the experiment this page could not find in a paper, on your own golden set.

Build one reflection pass twice. Path A critiques the draft with the draft alone. Path B runs your module 1 groundedness check first and gives the critic its output. Everything else identical: same model, same prompt skeleton, same one revision.

Run all three variants - no reflection, path A, path B - over your thirty golden questions, and record for each the score your module 1 harness gives, and the prompt tokens spent.

Write the three rows into docs/shape.md. Then write one sentence saying which variant you are shipping and why, including the case where the answer is "none of them".

Hint

Do not build a second harness for this. The point of the module 1 golden set is that every later module measures on it, and a reflection experiment scored by a fresh metric measures nothing you can compare next month.

Thirty questions is small, so expect the difference between path A and no reflection to be inside the noise. That is a result, and it is the one to write down honestly rather than to fix by adding questions until it moves.

Show what a good three-row table looks like
  1. Three rows, one metric, one token column. Anything wider invites you to pick the column that flatters the variant you already liked. If your harness gives several scores, choose the one you would defend in a review and record only that.
  2. Path A costing more and scoring the same is the expected outcome, not a bug in your setup. That is the shape Huang et al. reported, and reproducing it on thirty questions of your own is worth more to you than the paper is.
  3. Path B's gain, if any, will be concentrated in a few questions. Look at which ones. If they are the questions where the draft asserted something no passage supported, the signal is doing exactly what section 5 said it would, and you can now say so with your own numbers.
  4. Shipping none of them is a complete answer. A reflection pass adds a turn to every request, and lesson 0310's arithmetic says what a turn costs. A gain inside the noise does not pay for that, and recording the decision is what stops somebody adding it again next quarter.

Sanity check. Path B should cost more than path A, because it runs the check as well as the critique. If path B came out cheaper, you probably skipped the revision when the check passed - which is a sensible optimisation and a different experiment, so record it as its own row rather than as path B.

Your teacher. Take three answers your system produced and ask your assistant to critique each one twice: once given only the answer, and once given the answer plus the passages that were retrieved. Compare the two critiques sentence by sentence. The second set will name specific unsupported claims and the first will mostly suggest rewording - and seeing that on your own text is more convincing than either paper.

Primary source to go deeper

Read both, in this order, and notice that they are describing the same loop. Reflexion, Shinn et al., v4, for verbal reinforcement and the episodic reflection buffer, with the 91% figure in its own context. Then Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet, Huang et al., v2, for the definition of intrinsic self-correction and what happens without a signal. Self-Refine, Madaan et al., v2, is the simplest statement of the mechanism itself.
