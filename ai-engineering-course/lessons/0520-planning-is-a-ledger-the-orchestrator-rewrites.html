Module 05 · Multi-agent systems · Lesson 0520

Write the plan down, or you cannot tell why the team stalled

working 9 min The two-ledger design is read from Magentic-One, arXiv 2411.04468v1, which publishes its orchestrator's own structure rather than describing it in general terms. · Magentic-One

The one-minute version

  • An orchestrator that keeps its plan only in its context window has a plan nobody can read, resume or correct.
  • The published design that works keeps two documents: an outer task ledger of facts and a plan, and an inner progress ledger of who is doing what right now.
  • The inner loop asks the same short list of questions every iteration, starting with whether the request is already satisfied. That is lesson 0310's stop condition, at the level of a team.
  • When the inner loop stalls, the outer loop revises the facts and the plan - and in that design, every agent's context is cleared.
  • That reset is expensive and deliberate. Knowing what it costs is what lets you argue with it.

1. Two loops, two documents

Magentic-One publishes its orchestrator's structure, which is unusual and useful: most descriptions of "planning" stop at the word. The design is two nested loops, and each owns a document.

The outer loop maintains a task ledger, which the paper describes as containing "facts, guesses, and plan" - and the facts compartment is itself split, into what has been given or verified, and what still needs looking up. The outer loop is triggered by the initial request, and it runs again whenever the plan needs revising.

The inner loop maintains a progress ledger, holding current progress and the assignment of work to agents. The paper is explicit that this inner ledger "directs and evaluates the individual steps that contain instructions to the specialized agents".

Read the split as a lifetime question, exactly as lesson 0340 taught you to read memory. The task ledger changes rarely and survives the whole request. The progress ledger changes every step and is worthless afterwards. Two lifetimes, two documents, and confusing them is what produces an orchestrator that forgets the goal while it remembers who is busy.

2. The stop condition, at team level

On every iteration of the inner loop, that orchestrator answers a short fixed list of questions to build the progress ledger, and the first one is whether the request is already fully satisfied.

That is lesson 0310 again, one level up. A single agent's loop needs a stop condition or it runs until a ceiling; a team needs one or it keeps assigning work after the answer exists. The difference is that a team's stop condition can be written down and inspected, because it is a question asked against a document rather than a feeling in a context window.

The other questions in that list are the ones that detect a stall: is the team making progress, and is the same thing happening repeatedly. If your orchestrator does not ask something equivalent every iteration, it has no way to notice a loop, and MAST's catalogue of observed multi-agent failures names both an agent that does not recognise stopping criteria and one that unnecessarily repeats completed steps.

3. The reset, and what it costs

One design decision in that paper deserves an argument rather than adoption. On a re-plan, the authors state that they "force all agents to clear their contexts and reset their states after each plan update".

The reasoning is sound: agents that carry context from a plan that turned out to be wrong will keep acting on its assumptions, which is Cognition's conflicting-decisions problem arriving from the past instead of from a sibling. Clearing is the surgical fix.

It is also the most expensive operation in the system, and this is where lesson 0310's arithmetic lets you price a design choice from a paper. Take three workers that had each reached six turns on a conversation adding about 550 tokens a turn. Re-establishing that context after a reset means re-sending what they had built: roughly 3,300 tokens each of history that the ledger now has to replace, three times over, plus the outer loop's own planning call.

What makes it payable is the ledger. If the facts a worker needs are in a document, a reset costs one re-read of that document rather than a re-derivation. The reset is only affordable because the ledger exists, and a design that clears contexts without writing anything down has bought the cost and none of the benefit.

4. A plan you can see in a trace

The OpenTelemetry semantic conventions for generative AI register plan as an operation in its own right, described as an agent's planning or task-decomposition phase, with the model call that produces the plan as a child of that span and the resulting work as siblings under it.

Two things travel with that name every time this course quotes one, and both are true here. Those conventions are marked Status: Development and live in a repository that publishes no release and no tag, so the spelling can change. And what you commit to is the shape rather than the spelling: one span per plan, one per re-plan, on the same trace as the work they caused. Lesson 0550 is where that shape earns its keep.

The two-ledger design, the compartments and the context reset are Magentic-One's own account of its own system, in arXiv 2411.04468v1. The paper reports its benchmark results as statistically competitive with the state of the art on three agentic benchmarks; this page does not quote those figures, because the mechanism is what transfers and the scores were measured on their harness with their models. The token arithmetic in section 3 is this course's, over lesson 0310's stated assumptions.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does the outer loop's task ledger hold that the inner loop's progress ledger does not?
The task ledger holds facts, guesses and the plan - the things that outlive any one step. Which agent is busy, the last result and the step number all change every iteration and belong to the progress ledger, which is worthless once the request is answered. The split is a lifetime question, the same one lesson 0340 asked about memory.
Why is clearing every agent's context on a re-plan affordable in that design?
The ledger survives the reset, so a worker re-reads a few hundred tokens instead of re-deriving thousands. Contexts are not cheap to refill - section 3's arithmetic is that a three-worker reset discards nearly ten thousand tokens. Idleness is irrelevant, since the cost is the discarded context rather than the workers' time. Caching changes the price of re-sent tokens, not the fact that the reasoning behind them is gone.

Practice about 40 minutes, with your capstone

Practice 1working

Give your orchestrator a written plan, then break it on purpose.

Write the task ledger as a real document - a dict you serialise, a row, a file - with the four compartments from section 1 kept separate. Build it once at the start of a request and pass it to every worker instead of the raw conversation.

Log every rewrite. Each time the orchestrator revises the plan, append the old version, the new one, and the one-line reason. This is a small append-only log, not a state machine.

Then cause a stall. Point one worker at a corpus that cannot answer its step, run the request, and read the rewrite log afterwards. Record in docs/decision-multi-agent.md how many rewrites it took and whether the last plan was better than the first.

Hint

Keep guesses in their own compartment even if it feels pedantic. The value shows up only on the re-plan, when you need to know which assumptions to throw away and which facts to keep.

If your orchestrator never rewrites the plan, it has no stall detection. Add the first question from section 2 - is the request already satisfied - and the second - is the same step repeating - before you conclude that re-planning is unnecessary.

Show what a good ledger looks like
  1. The plan is derived, not authored twice. If a fact moves from "to look up" to "verified", the plan should be regenerated from the compartments rather than edited in place. Otherwise the plan and the facts drift, and the plan wins because it is what workers read.
  2. The rewrite log is the artefact, not the ledger. The current ledger tells you where the run ended up. The log tells you what it believed at each point, which is the only thing that explains a wrong answer after the fact.
  3. Most stalls are one missing fact. Reading the log usually shows the same lookup being re-attempted with different wording. That is the repeated-step failure mode, and the fix is in the ledger - record the lookup as attempted and failed - rather than in the prompt.
  4. A plan that never changes is a workflow. If your run completes without a rewrite every time, you wrote a sequential pipeline in lesson 0510's third shape, and you should say so and enjoy the lower bill.

Sanity check. Your ledger should be a few hundred tokens, not a few thousand. If it is approaching the size of the conversation it replaced, you are copying the history into it rather than distilling facts from it, and the reset in section 3 will cost you as much as it saves.

Your teacher. Give your assistant the rewrite log from your stalled run and ask it, for each rewrite, which compartment changed and whether the change was warranted by what the workers had actually returned. Rewrites that changed the plan without any new fact arriving are the interesting ones: that is an orchestrator re-planning on impatience rather than on evidence, and it is a cost with no cause.
