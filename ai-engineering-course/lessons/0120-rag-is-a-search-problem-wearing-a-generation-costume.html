Module 01 · Foundations · Lesson 0120

The retriever chooses the facts and the model only chooses the words

foundation 10 min Grounded in the paper that named retrieval-augmented generation, read at v4. · Lewis et al., arXiv:2005.11401

The one-minute version

  • The paper that named RAG framed it as two memories: the parametric one inside the weights, and an explicit non-parametric one you can inspect, update and cite.
  • The second is yours. Everything you can change about correctness lives there, and it is a search problem.
  • One query has five stages and only the last involves a model. Four of the five are yours.
  • The packed prompt is an object you can print. Print it once and the argument on this page becomes obvious rather than persuasive.
  • The cheapest question in the course, asked before you build anything: is the corpus small enough that there is no retrieval problem at all?

1. What the paper actually named

Lewis and colleagues introduced the term in 2020, and the framing they used is more useful than the acronym that survived. A pre-trained model stores factual knowledge in its parameters. That store is parametric memory: large, implicit, and impossible to update or cite. Their contribution was to pair it with non-parametric memory, in their case a dense vector index over Wikipedia reached by a learned retriever, and their abstract names exactly the two problems this solves: providing provenance for a decision, and updating the model's world knowledge.

Those two are still why you are here. You are not adding intelligence; you are adding an inspectable, updatable, citable store, and then arguing about which parts of it reach the model. The paper compared two formulations, one conditioning on the same passages for the whole generated sequence and one able to use different passages per token, and reported state-of-the-art results on three open-domain question-answering tasks. The version you are building is the first of those, because the second requires control over decoding that an API does not give you.

2. Print the prompt once

The most useful thirty seconds in this module is printing the exact bytes you send. Not the template. The rendered request, with the retrieved passages in it, to a file.

Two things happen when people do this. The first is that the request turns out to be mostly somebody else's writing, which is the arithmetic lesson 0000 worked through. The second is subtler and lands harder: the passages are visibly wrong for the question, and they were wrong before the model was involved, and you have been editing the instruction above them for a week.

3. The cheapest question, asked first

Before any of this is worth building, measure the corpus. Anthropic's own write-up of contextual retrieval states the threshold plainly: a knowledge base smaller than about 200,000 tokens, roughly 500 pages, can simply be placed in the prompt, with no retrieval at all. That is the vendor's guidance rather than a measured law, and it is the cheapest possible test of whether you have the problem this course solves.

It is worth being concrete about what fails the test. This repository's own course content, which is the fallback corpus for a reader with nothing of their own, is 953 lesson pages and about 2.57 million tokens after the chrome, figures and code blocks are stripped out, counting the twenty-one courses that are not this one. That is roughly thirteen times the threshold, so it is genuinely a retrieval problem. A single team's runbook folder frequently is not.

4. The three ways to be wrong, made actionable

Lesson 0000 named the taxonomy: the passage was never retrieved, it was retrieved and unused, or it was retrieved and contradicted. It is worth attaching the fix to each now, because the rest of the course is organised by that attachment.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What did Lewis et al. call the explicit, inspectable store their retriever searched?
The paper's framing pairs parametric memory in the weights with non-parametric memory as a dense vector index you can update and cite. Parametric memory is the other half of that pair and is precisely the part you cannot inspect. Contextual and episodic memory are terms from agent memory design, which module 3 covers, and neither appears in this paper.
Your corpus is about 90,000 tokens. What does this page say to do first?
Ninety thousand is well under the roughly 200,000-token threshold Anthropic publishes, so the whole corpus fits in one prompt and there is no retrieval problem to solve. The other three all begin by building a retriever, which is the week this section exists to save you. Note that the threshold is vendor guidance rather than a measured law, so it is a first test rather than a proof.

Practice about 20 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1foundation

Build the corpus loader you will use for the rest of the course, and measure two numbers with it.

Write a function that walks your chosen corpus, strips whatever is not prose, and returns the text of each document with a stable identifier. If you have no corpus of your own, point it at this repository's *-course/lessons/*.html files, strip the script, style, svg, figure, nav and footer elements, and take the text inside main.

Report the total token count and the number of documents. Then print one full packed prompt for one question, with whatever crude retrieval you have, into a file, and read it.

Hint

Strip the figures. A hub lesson carries several hundred words of figure captions and SVG label text that would otherwise dominate every chunk, and they are not the prose a reader is asking about. The measurement in section 3 was taken with figures removed for exactly that reason.

Multiply words by about 1.3 for a token estimate if you do not want to run a tokenizer. The threshold you are comparing against has one significant figure.

Show what the loader owes you
  1. A stable identifier per document is not optional, and it is the thing people leave out. Use the path, not an array index: the index changes when a file is added and every score you recorded against it silently means something else. Lesson 0200 turns this into a full chunk record.
  2. Stripping is a decision with consequences you should write down. Removing code blocks makes a documentation corpus much smaller and much more answerable in prose; keeping them makes it larger and better at "show me the invocation". Neither is wrong. What is wrong is not knowing which you did.
  3. The token count decides whether you continue. Under about 200,000, stop and put the corpus in the prompt. This repository's lessons come to roughly 2.6 million with figures stripped, which is thirteen times over, so it continues.
  4. Reading the packed prompt is the point of the exercise. Count what fraction of it is retrieved text, and check whether the passages would let a careful human answer the question. If a human could not answer from those passages, no model can, and every prompt change you make is decoration.
  5. Keep the loader deterministic. Sort the file list. An unsorted directory walk gives a different chunk order on a different machine, and chunk order becomes chunk identity the moment you build an index.

Sanity check. Run the loader twice and diff the two outputs; they must be byte-identical. Then check the total against your intuition: if a corpus you think of as large comes out under 200,000 tokens, either your strip is too aggressive or you do not have a retrieval problem, and both are worth knowing before lesson 0130.

Your teacher. Hand your agent the packed prompt you printed and the question it was for, and ask it to answer using only the passages, then to say which passage it used. If it cannot answer, you have a recall problem and lesson 0160 will measure it. If it answers from a passage you did not expect, you have a ranking problem and module 2 is about to be useful.

Primary source to go deeper

The paper that named the technique and framed it as two memories: Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, Lewis et al., at v4. Read the abstract's two stated motivations, provenance and updatable knowledge, and notice that neither of them is answer quality. The whiteboard framing of the same pipeline, topic by topic, is RAG and Retrieval.
