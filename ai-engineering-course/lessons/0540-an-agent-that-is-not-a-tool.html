Module 05 · Multi-agent systems · Lesson 0540

A peer you cannot see inside needs a contract, not a schema

working 9 min Written against the A2A specification at protocol version 1.0, read in the repository at tag v1.0.1; the protocol version is the major and minor part only. · A2A specification

The one-minute version

  • MCP connects your agent downward to tools and data. A2A connects it sideways to a peer you do not run.
  • The design principle is Opaque Execution: agents collaborate "without needing access to each other's internal state, memory, or tools".
  • Discovery is one HTTP GET for an Agent Card, and its eight required fields are eight promises you are making to strangers.
  • A request comes back as a Message or a Task, and a Task has nine states of which four are terminal and two are interruptions waiting on you.
  • The trap is not the protocol. It is that handing a peer your conversation hands it whatever credentials are in your conversation.

1. Down to tools, across to peers

The A2A specification opens by naming what it is for: letting agents "securely exchange information to achieve user goals without needing access to each other's internal state, memory, or tools", and it lists Opaque Execution among its guiding principles - agents collaborate on declared capabilities rather than by sharing their thoughts, plans or tool implementations.

Set that beside MCP, which module 3 used and module 4 develops. MCP's unit is a tool call: you list a server's tools, you call one, you get a result, and your client drives every step. A2A's unit is a task with a lifecycle, handed to something that decides for itself how to do it.

The project's own argument for why a tool wrapper is not enough is worth quoting: encapsulating an agent as a simple tool "is fundamentally limiting, as it fails to capture the agent's full capabilities". A tool answers. A peer can ask you a question back, work for ten minutes, need authorization halfway, and produce artefacts rather than a string.

Both protocols in one system is the ordinary case, not an exotic one: your agent speaks MCP to its own tools and A2A to somebody else's agent.

2. Eight required fields, and eight promises

Discovery is one HTTP GET. A client fetches https://{domain}/.well-known/agent-card.json and gets a self-describing manifest.

That path is a version trap worth knowing about before you follow a tutorial: it was /.well-known/agent.json up to v0.2.6 and became agent-card.json at v0.3.0. Anything older than that describes a path that is no longer served.

Reading the protobuf at tag v1.0.1, eight fields are required: name, description, supported_interfaces, version, capabilities, default_input_modes, default_output_modes and skills. Two of those are easy to misread. The version is your agent's version, not the protocol's; the protocol version lives on each entry in supported_interfaces, beside that interface's URL and binding. And capabilities is a commitment: declare streaming false or leave it out, and the specification requires that streaming calls to you return an unsupported-operation error.

3. A Message back, or a Task

Send work with SendMessage and the specification says the agent "MAY create a new Task to process the provided message asynchronously or MAY return a direct Message response for simple interactions", returning immediately either way.

A client that assumes one shape is broken by the other, and this is the first thing to get right in your own client. A quick question comes back as a Message. Anything the peer decides is long-running comes back as a Task with an id you hold.

The Task state machine has nine values. Four are terminal - completed, failed, canceled and rejected - and two are interruptions that are waiting on you: input-required, where the peer needs something more, and auth-required, where it needs a credential. That second one is the interesting design decision in the whole protocol: authorization is a task state rather than an error, so a peer can pause mid-task and ask you to go and get permission.

4. Three ways to find out it finished

Because a task can run for minutes, the protocol offers three ways to learn the outcome, and they cost very different amounts.

Poll with GetTask on a timer. Simple, works everywhere, and the round trips grow with the task's duration. Stream with SendStreamingMessage or SubscribeToTask, which uses Server-Sent Events: first a Task or Message, then status and artefact update events until a terminal or interrupted state closes the stream. Register a webhook, and the peer POSTs you one payload carrying exactly one of a task, a message, a status update or an artefact update. Delivery is at-least-once, so your handler must be idempotent - which is lesson 0330's annotation question arriving from the other side.

5. Handing a peer your history hands it your secrets

Here is the part the specification's prose will not teach you, and a production implementation will.

Google's ADK, at 2.8.0, ships a client that speaks A2A to a remote peer. Before it forwards session events, it removes credential-bearing ones, and its own comment says why: a credential-request call "carries a serialized AuthConfig in its arguments, including raw_auth_credential (an OAuth2 client secret or a service account key)", and that event "is in the history that the next request is rebuilt from and would otherwise be sent to the remote peer".

Read that against lesson 0510. There, handing the whole history to a worker was a cost and a coherence decision. Here the worker is somebody else's process, in somebody else's organisation, and the same history may contain a credential your own flow put there. The history filter stops being an optimisation and becomes a security boundary.

The general rule is the one module 6 states for prompt injection and it applies unchanged: decide what the other side is allowed to reach, rather than trying to judge each message. For a peer, that means an allow-list of what may cross, not a deny-list of what may not.

A2A's protocol version is 1.0 - the major and minor part only. The specification states that patch numbers "do not affect protocol compatibility" and MUST NOT be considered when clients and servers negotiate, so this page reads the repository at tag v1.0.1 and cites the protocol as 1.0. Separately: ADK's A2A support is marked experimental by its own authors, whose warning says the implementation "is in experimental mode and is subject to breaking changes" while noting in the same sentence that "A2A protocol and SDK are themselves not experimental". Both halves matter - the protocol is stable and one binding to it is not.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does A2A give you that wrapping the same agent as one of your tools does not?
Opaque Execution is the guiding principle: a peer collaborates on declared capabilities and keeps its state, memory and tools to itself, which is what lets it be a task-taking agent rather than a function. It is not cheaper - a task lifecycle costs more than one call. Access to its tools and its history are the opposite of what the protocol provides, and offering them would defeat the purpose.
At protocol version 1.0, where does a client fetch an Agent Card?
The current path is /.well-known/agent-card.json. The second option is the real trap: it was correct up to v0.2.6 and was renamed at v0.3.0, so any tutorial older than that sends you to a path nobody serves. The other two were never the path. This is why a protocol claim carries its version in the sentence.

Practice about 50 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

Publish your capstone as a peer, then drive it from outside as a stranger would.

Write the Agent Card first, by hand, before any server code. Five required blocks, honestly filled: what your agent does, what it will accept and return, and one skill it genuinely has. Declare streaming only if you will implement it.

Serve it and the endpoint, then fetch the card with curl from a second terminal and read it as somebody who has never seen your repository. Drive one task from a second process to a terminal state.

Then write the boundary. Before anything crosses to a peer, run it through an allow-list, and add one test that puts a fake credential into the session and asserts it does not appear in the outgoing payload. Record both in docs/decision-multi-agent.md.

Hint

Start with the JSON-RPC binding and polling. Streaming and webhooks are more interesting and neither is needed to see a task move through its states, and declaring a capability you have not built is the one card mistake that will actually break a client.

For the credential test, do not search the payload for your real secret. Inject a distinctive fake one at the start of the session and assert on that, so the test is safe to commit and safe to fail loudly.

Show what a good first peer looks like
  1. The card is harder to write than the server, and that is the lesson. Describing your agent to a stranger forces you to decide what it actually promises. Most first drafts describe the implementation rather than the capability, and a skill nobody outside your team can recognise is not a skill.
  2. Do not conflate the two versions. The version at the top is your agent's, and it should change when your behaviour changes. The protocol_version inside an interface entry is A2A's, and it is 1.0. Getting these the wrong way round produces a card that parses and negotiates nothing.
  3. Drive it from a genuinely separate process. Calling your own handler in-process proves nothing about the boundary, and the boundary is the entire subject. A second terminal and curl is enough.
  4. The allow-list is the deliverable, not the server. Naming what may cross - the question, the ledger's verified facts, nothing else - is a decision you can defend. Filtering out what must not cross is a list that will always be one item short of the next incident.

Sanity check. Your outgoing payload should be small - a question and a little context, not a conversation. If it is the size of the message lists from lesson 0510, you have exported your whole session to another organisation, and the credential test you just wrote is the only thing standing between that and an incident.

Your teacher. Hand your assistant only your Agent Card - not your code - and ask it to describe what your agent does, what it will accept, and what it promises to return. Then ask it what it cannot tell from the card and would need to know before calling you. That list is what a real caller will email you about, and adding those answers to the description is cheaper than answering the emails.
