Module 01 · Foundations · Lesson 0170

Your scorer is a model, so it is a component you evaluate too

foundation 11 min Metric names from Ragas 0.4.x; the judge's biases and the agreement figure from the MT-Bench paper at v4. · Zheng et al., arXiv:2306.05685

The one-minute version

  • Lesson 0160 scored the half of the pipeline with no model in it. This page scores the other half, and the scorer is itself a model.
  • Reference-free evaluation exists because nobody has ground-truth answers for a private corpus. Ragas is the framework that named the metrics everyone now uses.
  • The names worth adopting: faithfulness, response relevancy, context precision, context recall, noise sensitivity. Two of those are retrieval metrics wearing generation names.
  • The judge has named, measured biases: position, verbosity and self-enhancement, plus limited reasoning. Not "might be unreliable" - specific failure modes with specific mitigations.
  • The rule that closes the thread: your judge's agreement with your own hand-scoring is the confidence interval on every automated number for the rest of this course.

1. Why reference-free, and what it costs

Classical evaluation compares an output against a reference answer somebody wrote. For a private corpus nobody has written those, and writing them is the expense the whole approach was meant to avoid.

Ragas introduced the framework that stuck: a suite of metrics that score a retrieval-augmented answer without ground-truth human annotations, by decomposing the question into parts a model can check. Its paper names the three dimensions plainly: whether retrieval found relevant and focused passages, whether the model used them faithfully, and whether the generation itself is any good.

What this buys is speed: you can score a hundred answers in minutes rather than commissioning annotation. What it costs is that the measuring instrument is made of the same material as the thing measured, which is the rest of this page.

2. The names worth adopting

Adopt the vocabulary rather than the library. Metric names travel between tools and survive version bumps; a 0.x API does not, and this course pins Ragas at 0.4.x for its names rather than its call signatures.

Faithfulness asks whether the answer's claims are supported by the retrieved passages. It is the hallucination metric and it is the one to add first. Response relevancy asks whether the answer addresses the question rather than an adjacent one. Context precision and context recall score the retrieved set itself, and it is worth noticing that these two are retrieval metrics living in a generation framework; they overlap what lesson 0160 already measures, and where they disagree the lesson 0160 number is the one with no model in it. Noise sensitivity asks how much an irrelevant retrieved passage degrades the answer, which is the metric module 4's retrieval grader is built to move.

Two of the five are therefore about the retriever, and only faithfulness and response relevancy are genuinely new here. That is the honest shape and it is worth saying out loud, because a dashboard with five metrics on it looks like five independent readings and is not.

3. The biases, named and measured

The MT-Bench paper is the one to read because it examines the method rather than advertising it. Its abstract names four limitations explicitly: position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and limited reasoning ability.

Position bias is the one that will affect you today: present the same two answers in the opposite order and the judge can prefer whichever came first. It is cheap to defend against and expensive to ignore. Verbosity bias is a preference for longer answers, which matters because it means a prompt change that only made answers longer will show as a quality improvement. Self-enhancement bias is a preference for text produced by the same model family, which is a direct problem if you judge your Claude-generated answers with a Claude judge.

The same paper supplies the anchor for how good the method can be: GPT-4 judgments reached over 80% agreement with human preferences, which the authors note is the same level of agreement humans reach with each other. That number is the ceiling and it is somebody else's measurement on their benchmark, so it is evidence that the method can work and is not a score you may assume for your own judge on your own corpus.

4. Four cheap defences

Swap the order and score twice. Present the pair in both orders and keep the result only when the two agree; a disagreement is a tie rather than a coin flip. This directly cancels position bias and costs one extra call.

Fix the rubric before you see any outputs. A rubric written after reading the answers is a description of those answers. Write the criteria, then run.

Judge with a different model family than you generate with, where you can, which removes self-enhancement bias by construction rather than by hoping.

Score length as its own column. If mean answer length moved with your quality score, verbosity bias is a live hypothesis and you can see it rather than argue about it.

5. The rule that closes the thread

Module 1 has built three things: a pipeline, a trace, and two numbers. This is the sentence that governs the second of those numbers for the rest of the course.

Report the judge's agreement with your hand-scoring beside every score it produces, and treat it as the confidence interval on that score. If your judge agrees with you on 17 of 20 answers, a quality move from 0.78 to 0.81 is not a result. If it agrees on 19 of 20, the same move might be. The difference is knowable and it costs one hour.

No later page in this course may quote a judge score as though it were a measurement, and modules 5 to 8 re-run this same harness rather than building a second one. A course with two evaluation harnesses has none, because the reader will maintain neither.

The over-80% agreement figure is Zheng et al.'s measurement of GPT-4 against human preference on MT-Bench, published at v4. It is evidence that judging with a model can reach human-level agreement on that benchmark. It is not a property of judges in general, not a property of your judge, and not a number to assume in place of measuring. This course also does not name the tasks behind it beyond what the paper states.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A prompt change raised your judge's quality score and also made answers 40% longer. What is the risk?
Verbosity bias is a measured preference for longer answers, so a change that only added length can raise the score without improving anything, which is why length is reported as its own column. Position bias affects which of two answers wins, not the score of one. Context precision is a retrieval metric and is unaffected by how the answer is worded. Self-enhancement is about model family and did not change here.
Your judge agrees with your hand-scoring on 16 of 20 answers. What follows?
Eighty percent agreement is the resolution of your instrument, so a change smaller than that error is indistinguishable from noise and must not be reported as an improvement. Swapping models might help and is a guess until you measure the replacement the same way. Golden-set size affects retrieval measurement, which has no judge in it. Changing which metric you report does nothing about the scorer's error, since both metrics come from the same judge.

Practice about 60 minutes, half of it reading

Practice 1foundation

Measure your own judge, and finish module 1 by writing both numbers into the repository.

Run your pipeline over twenty questions from the golden set and keep the answers. Score all twenty by hand first, on a rubric you write before you read them: two levels are enough, supported by the passages or not. Then score the same twenty with a judge, and compute the agreement.

Report three things in the charter: your recall at 10 from lesson 0160, your faithfulness score, and the judge agreement that qualifies it. Then add the judge's rubric to the repository with a version string.

Hint

Score by hand before you look at the judge's output, not after. Reading the judge first anchors you to it, and the agreement you then measure is between the judge and a version of yourself that has already been persuaded.

Two levels beat five. A five-point scale produces disagreement about whether something is a 3 or a 4, which is noise rather than signal, and the binary question is the one your gate will actually ask.

Show what finishing module 1 looks like
  1. Agreement in the high teens out of twenty is workable and anything near twelve is not. At 16 of 20 your instrument resolves differences of about a fifth, so it can detect a large regression and cannot referee a close call. Write the number down rather than a verdict, because the next person needs the number.
  2. Read the disagreements, they are the most informative artefact of the exercise. Usually one of two things: the judge accepted an answer that was fluent and unsupported, or it rejected a correct answer phrased differently from the passage. The first is a faithfulness rubric that is too loose, the second one that is too literal, and both are fixed in the rubric rather than in the model.
  3. Now you have the pair of numbers module 1 exists to produce. One from lesson 0160 with no model in it, and one from this page with two. They fail differently, they move for different reasons, and every later module reports both.
  4. Version the rubric like a prompt. It is a prompt. A rubric that changes silently makes two runs incomparable in exactly the way lesson 0100's four artefacts do, and lesson 0250 formalises the mechanism.
  5. Put the judge behind the same gate as everything else. The judge is a model call, so it costs money and it is non-deterministic, which is why the cheap deterministic recall number stays your first gate and the judge is the second.

Sanity check. Score the same twenty answers with the judge twice and compare. If the judge disagrees with itself on more answers than it disagrees with you on, its temperature is too high or the rubric is ambiguous, and the agreement number you just computed is measuring noise rather than bias. Fix that before you trust either figure.

Your teacher. Give your agent the answers where you and the judge disagreed, with your rubric, and ask it to argue the judge's side of each one. Where it makes a case you accept, your rubric is ambiguous and needs a sentence. Where it cannot, you have found a genuine judge failure worth recording. Ask it also to rewrite one correct answer to be twice as long without adding information, then judge both, and see whether verbosity bias is live in your setup.

Primary source to go deeper

The paper that examines the method rather than selling it, including the four named limitations and the agreement figure: Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, Zheng et al., at v4. For the metric vocabulary, Ragas: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation, Es et al., at v2, and the current metric list in the Ragas documentation at 0.4.x. The whiteboard framing of evaluation, including who owns the gate, is LLM-as-judge in AI System Design, and the organisational question of who may override a red eval belongs to Proving It Works.
