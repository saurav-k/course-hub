Capstone · Read after module 07 · Lesson 1070

Two numbers about your own system, before anybody writes them up

frontier 7 min The fifth capstone stop. Module 7 turned outward to two other organisations and then back to your system; this is the turning back. · The six-question review

The one-minute version

  • Module 7 read two systems from their owners' public write-ups and then asked the same six questions of yours. This stop records the answers.
  • Two of them are numbers: what one request costs at the tail, and what the whole thing costs at ten times the traffic.
  • Two of the six cannot be answered from a diagram. If you cannot answer them, that is the review's first finding rather than a gap in the review.
  • The output is short - a score, the two numbers, and the lowest-scoring answer - and lesson 0820 turns it into prose without new thinking.
  • The gate: you fixed the lowest-scoring row before moving on, or wrote down why you did not.

1. Six questions, and the two that need instrumentation

The review transfers because it is a set of questions rather than a set of recommendations. Recommendations expire with the tools they were written about; the questions do not.

Four of the six can be answered from a whiteboard by somebody who knows the system: what the unit of work is, where untrusted text enters, what degrades when the provider is down, and how far one wrong answer travels. Two cannot. What a request costs at the tail, and how you would learn that the answers got worse, are both readings rather than opinions - and a team that cannot produce them has found the review's first finding without scoring anything.

You are in an unusually good position on both, because modules 1 and 6 built exactly the instruments they need. The tail cost comes from the token histograms; the "how would you know" answer is the nightly evaluation on the golden set. If either is missing, this is where you notice.

2. The bill is one multiplication, and one term can move by ten

Everything an agentic system costs is the same product of five terms, and the useful discipline is writing it once rather than re-deriving it per feature.

Because it is a product, a one per cent change anywhere moves the bill by one per cent - so sensitivity is not about the terms, it is about how far each one can travel. Traffic can move by a factor of ten. Model choice moves the price a token by about five. Prompt wording moves nothing at all, which is worth knowing before another week goes into it.

Caching is the odd one out: it changes the shape rather than the size, because it splits input tokens into two prices. That is the module 6 work arriving in the module 7 arithmetic, and it is why the cost budget from Ship it is an input to this stop rather than a separate document.

Write the estimate down before you look at a bill. The gap between the two is the only thing that teaches you anything about your own estimating, and it disappears the moment you calibrate after the fact.

3. What this stop actually produces

Four short things, and none of them is prose.

  • A score from the six-question review, with the band it lands in.
  • The lowest-scoring row, named, and either fixed this week or explained.
  • The tail cost of one request, read from the histogram rather than estimated.
  • The monthly bill at ten times the traffic, written down before you look at an actual bill.

That is the whole deliverable. Lesson 0820 is explicit that the design record "is not new writing" and is the six questions answered in prose with two of them already answered by numbers - these two. Producing them here is what makes module 8's writing a transcription rather than a research project.

The travel ranges in the chart are lesson 0720's own framing of how far each term can move, drawn to scale against one another rather than measured on any system. They are a claim about which terms deserve attention, not a prediction of your bill. Your two numbers come from your histogram and your own five terms, and the whole point of writing the estimate before the bill is that the two will differ.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your team cannot answer "what does a request cost at the tail". What is that?
Two of the six questions are readings rather than opinions, and being unable to produce one is exactly the sort of thing the review exists to surface - so it is a finding, recorded as such. The p50 answers a different question and hides the tail, which is where the cost lives. Deferring inverts the purpose: the review is most useful precisely when it cannot be answered, and the first option blames the instrument for the reading.
Which of the five cost terms deserves the least of your attention, and why?
Wording changes no term in the product, so however long you spend on it the bill is unchanged - which is the point of ranking terms by how far they travel rather than by how much they are discussed. Traffic travels furthest and must be planned for even though you do not set it. Price per token is the vendor's and still moves the bill about fivefold through model choice. The turn ceiling is yours and is one of the terms you can actually move.

Practice about 45 minutes, mostly reading your own dashboards

Capstonefrontier

Almost all of this is reading instruments you already built. The writing is four lines.

Run the six-question review on your own system and record the score and the band. Answer every question even where the answer is "we cannot say".

Read the two numbers. The tail cost of one request from your token histogram, and the monthly bill at ten times your current traffic from the five terms. Write the second one down before you open any billing page.

Name the lowest-scoring row and fix it, or write one sentence saying why it is not this week's work. Both are acceptable and silence is not.

Then compare the estimate with the bill if you have one. The gap, in either direction, is the finding.

Hint

If your tail cost and your median are close together, check that you are reading a histogram rather than an average of averages. A generating system's cost distribution has a long right tail by construction, and a narrow one usually means the metric is pre-aggregated.

Ten times the traffic is the right multiplier even if you think growth is unlikely; it is the term with the widest travel, and the estimate is cheap.

Show what a finished stop looks like
  1. Six answers, including the ones that read "we cannot say". A review with six confident answers on a system with no token histogram has not been run honestly.
  2. The two numbers come from instruments, not from memory. If either was estimated rather than read, say so beside it; lesson 0820 will quote them and the provenance travels with them.
  3. The lowest row is named. For most readers at this point it is question five - how you would learn the answers got worse - because a nightly evaluation is the last thing teams wire up.
  4. The estimate is written before the bill. If you looked first, say so, because a calibrated-after-the-fact estimate teaches nothing about your estimating and you will need that skill on the next system.
  5. All four outputs fit on one screen. This stop is deliberately short: the writing up is module 8's, and duplicating it here is how two documents start disagreeing.

Sanity check. Your ten-times estimate should be close to ten times your current bill, and if it is not, one of your five terms is not linear in traffic - usually a fixed cost such as an index rebuild that does not scale per request. Finding that term is worth more than the estimate, because it is the one that makes the system cheaper per user as it grows.

Your teacher. If your six answers all came out confident, ask your agent which of them you could support with a number from an instrument you actually have, and treat the rest as opinions. If your ten-times estimate feels arbitrary, hand it your five terms and ask which one you are least sure of and what it would cost to measure that one properly.
