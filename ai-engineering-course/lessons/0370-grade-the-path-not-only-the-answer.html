Module 03 · Agents and agentic systems · Lesson 0370

A right answer reached the wrong way is a bill you pay every day

working 9 min Trajectory matching is read from a shipped implementation, google-adk 2.8.0, as evidence that the rule set is settled; the reliability figures are Yao et al.'s own. · tau-bench

The one-minute version

  • Your module 1 harness scores answers. An agent can reach a right answer through four redundant calls, and that score will not move.
  • A trajectory score compares the tool calls the agent actually made against the ones you expected, under a named match rule.
  • Three rules are enough and a shipped implementation uses exactly three: exact, in order, and any order.
  • Reliability is a second axis. Yao et al.'s pass^k asks whether all k trials of the same task succeeded, and their best agent scored 61.2% once and under 25% eight times running.
  • Both go into the golden set module 1 built. This course does not get a second harness.

1. Three failures a right answer hides

Your golden set from module 1 asks one question of every run: was the answer right. That is the correct first question and it is blind to three things an agent can now do.

The agent can reach the right answer through the wrong tool - answering from what the model already knew, calling retrieval afterwards, and citing whatever came back. The answer scores well and the citation is decorative.

It can reach the right answer in nine turns that should have been two, which lesson 0310 already priced: nine turns is roughly five times the prompt tokens of two, every time that question is asked.

And it can reach the right answer once in eight tries. A harness that runs each question once will record that as a pass, and a user asking the same thing on Tuesday will not get it.

2. Comparing the path, under a named rule

The mechanism is simpler than it sounds: write down the tool calls you expected, compare them with the ones that happened, and score the run one or zero. The only real design question is what counts as a match, and a shipped implementation has settled on three rules that are worth borrowing whatever you build with.

Reading google-adk at 2.8.0, its trajectory evaluator compares "the sequence of tools called by the agent against a list of expected calls" under EXACT, which requires a perfect match with no extra or missing calls; IN_ORDER, which requires every expected call to be present in the same order and allows others in between; and ANY_ORDER, which requires them present in any order and also allows others in between. Each invocation scores 1.0 or 0.0, and the metric is the average across the eval case.

The library is a fixture here and the three rules are the subject. Fifteen lines in your own harness gives you the same thing, and the reason to know the names is that they encode three different promises: EXACT says this path is the specification, IN_ORDER says these steps must happen and in this sequence, and ANY_ORDER says these steps must happen.

The same package ships a wider set of agent metrics, which is worth knowing only as evidence that this is now ordinary practice rather than an invention: alongside tool_trajectory_avg_score there are multi-turn trajectory and tool-use quality metrics, a hallucination metric, rubric-based variants, and a simulated user. You do not need any of them to start. You need one expected trajectory on five of your thirty questions.

3. The second axis, which is not accuracy

Yao et al.'s tau-bench (arXiv 2406.12045v1) contributes two things this course uses. The first is a grading method: rather than comparing text, it compares the database state at the end of a conversation with an annotated goal state, which is the same instinct as checking whether the answer cited a passage that was actually returned.

The second is the metric. They define pass^k as the chance that all k independent trials of a task succeed, averaged across tasks - deliberately not pass@k, which asks whether any of k succeeded. One is a reliability question and the other is a best-of question, and agent products live on the first.

Their headline figures are worth quoting exactly. The strongest agent they measured, gpt-4o with function calling, scored 61.2% on the retail domain and 35.2% on airline at pass^1, and the paper reports pass^8 falling below 25% in retail. Those are their measurements, on their benchmark, with the models available to them.

One derived observation, because it changes what you conclude. If the eight trials were independent coin flips at 61.2%, pass^8 would be 0.612 to the eighth power, which is about 2%. The measured figure is far higher than that, which means failures are concentrated: some tasks succeed nearly every time and others fail nearly every time. That is good news for debugging - the unreliable questions are a short list you can go and look at - and it is arithmetic done here rather than a claim of theirs.

4. What goes in the set, and when

Two columns are added to the harness module 1 built, and nothing is replaced. For each question that has an expected path, record the trajectory verdict and the match rule you promised. For a small subset - five questions is enough to start - run each one several times and record how many of the runs succeeded.

The discipline that makes this useful is an ordering rule: the eval case is written before the fix. A trajectory case added after you fixed the behaviour records what you did. One added when you first saw the wrong path records what you promised, and only the second kind fails again later.

Reliability costs real money, so pick the subset deliberately. Five questions run eight times is forty runs, and lesson 0310's arithmetic says what a run costs. Run it nightly rather than per commit, and keep the trajectory column, which is one run each, in the fast gate.

Every figure in section 3 is Yao et al.'s measurement on their own benchmark, with the model ids they used, and none is a target for your corpus. The 2% independence figure is this page's arithmetic over their published pass^1, shown so you can check it. The three match rules are read from a shipped implementation at a pinned version and would be worth the same if that package disappeared tomorrow.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You expected search then summarise. The run did summarise, then search. Which match rules pass it?
Both expected calls are present, so ANY_ORDER passes. IN_ORDER requires the expected sequence to appear in order and it did not, so it fails, and EXACT is stricter still. That is the point of the run B example: this is the answer-then-cite failure, and only the most permissive rule lets it through.
What does pass^k tell you that pass@1 does not?
pass^k is defined as the chance that all k trials of a task succeed, which is what a user asking the same question twice experiences. Whether any of k passes is pass@k, the best-of metric, and it moves in the opposite direction as k grows. Cost is lesson 0310's axis and is not in this metric at all. Judge accuracy is module 1's concern and applies to whichever metric you use.

Practice about 45 minutes, with a terminal

Practice 1working

This is module 3's last increment, and it closes the module by measuring what the module built.

Add two columns to your module 1 harness. A trajectory verdict, with the match rule recorded beside it, and a repeat count. Do not build a second harness, and do not invent a new score for the answer.

Write expected trajectories for five of your thirty questions, choosing the five where the path matters: a comparison that needs two retrievals, a question that should not need retrieval at all, and three in between. Promise the strictest rule you are willing to keep.

Run five questions eight times each and record how many of the eight succeeded. Then score one recorded run on the card above and put the resulting table in docs/shape.md.

Hint

The expected trajectory comes out of your trace, not out of your head. Run the question once, read the tool sequence, and decide whether that sequence is the one you would defend. Often it is not, and that is the finding.

Set the temperature you actually ship with for the repeat runs. Measuring reliability at temperature zero measures your retriever's determinism and not your agent's.

Show what a good two-column addition looks like
  1. The question that should need no retrieval is the most valuable of the five. An expected trajectory of no tool calls catches the agent that searches reflexively, which is a cost on every request and invisible to an answer score.
  2. Record the match rule in the row, not in your head. Six months later, a trajectory failure with no rule beside it cannot be judged: nobody knows whether an extra call was ever allowed. The rule is half the assertion.
  3. Expect the repeat column to be worse than the single-run column, and by a lot. That is what Yao et al. measured on their benchmark and it is the ordinary shape. A question that passes eight times out of eight is genuinely done; one that passes five times is a defect that your single-run harness has been calling a pass.
  4. Put the nightly and the per-commit runs in different places. Trajectory verdicts are one run each and belong in the fast gate. Forty runs for reliability belong on a schedule, and module 6 is where that becomes a threshold in CI.

Sanity check. If all five questions pass eight out of eight on the first attempt, the five you chose are too easy, or you ran them at temperature zero. Pick a question your system got wrong last week and add it, because a reliability column that starts perfect will never move and will quietly stop being read.

Your teacher. Hand your assistant one recorded trace and its expected trajectory and ask it to give the verdict under each of the three rules, with the reason. Then ask which rule you should promise for that question and what mistake each of the other two would let through. The disagreements are worth having, and the answer for a question that should need no retrieval at all is usually stricter than you first want it to be.
