Module 14 · Resilience and migration · Lesson 1402

Every service replicates on its own terms, so ordering the cutover is the only thing left to design

working 10 min Capability area resilience-migration, matrix rows cross-region-replication and backup-service.

The one-minute version

  • There is no single platform-wide replication switch. A bucket has a replication policy, a block volume has a replica, a file system has its own replication, and a key-value table has global tables.
  • All of them are asynchronous, which means the standby is behind by an amount that depends on the service and the load.
  • Backup policies are the other half: scheduled copies with predefined tiers, and cross-region copies of those backups.
  • A volume group is what makes a multi-volume snapshot consistent, and without one a machine with two volumes has two unrelated point-in-time copies.
  • The recovery plan of lesson 1400 is what orders these mechanisms into one cutover. On their own they are several independent copies with no agreed moment.

The mental model: one mechanism per service

The mechanism: asynchronous means the standby is behind

The mechanism: what makes a multi-volume copy consistent

The reframing Replication gives you copies. A recovery needs a moment: a point at which every part of the workload agrees about what happened. Volume groups supply that moment within a machine, and the recovery plan supplies the ordering across the workload. Neither comes from the replication itself.
Lesson 0404 recorded that a restore forks rather than rewinds: recovering from a backup produces a new volume or a new instance rather than returning the original to an earlier state. That property matters more here than it did there, because a failover plan that assumes resources are restored in place will find itself with new resources, new identifiers and anything that referenced the old ones pointing at nothing.

The trade-off: synchronous safety is not on this menu

Every mechanism on this page is asynchronous, which is what makes writes fast and what makes cross-region replication practical at all. The price is a recovery point that is not zero and not fixed: it is however far behind the standby happens to be when the primary stops.

Where a workload genuinely cannot lose recent writes, the answer is at the database layer with its own standby arrangement rather than at the storage layer, and the honest design step is to state the acceptable loss as a number before choosing mechanisms rather than after. A drill will not reveal this, because a drill is orderly and an unplanned failover is not.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

How is cross-region replication turned on for a workload?
Each service replicates through its own mechanism and each is configured on its own. The first invents a platform-wide switch. The third and fourth give the recovery plan a job it does not have: it orders and validates rather than configuring the copies.
Two volumes on one machine are backed up without a volume group. What is the risk?
Without a group, each snapshot is taken at its own moment and a restore may mix two points in time. The first and second invent limitations that do not exist. The third invents a failure that would at least be obvious, which this one is not.
What does asynchronous replication mean for an unplanned failover?
Asynchronous means acknowledged before copied, so the unreplicated tail is lost. The first invents a blocking behaviour. The second describes synchronous replication. The fourth invents a replay that would require the primary to be reachable, which a failover assumes it is not.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A workload has a bucket of customer uploads, four instances each with a data volume and a log volume, a shared file system, and a database. The team has enabled bucket replication and configured volume backups on a schedule, and believes the workload is protected across regions. They have not created a volume group and have not built a protection group.

List what is protected, what is not, and what a restore of the current backups would actually give them.

Hint

Go component by component and ask which mechanism covers it. Then look separately at the two volumes per machine and ask what moment each snapshot represents. Finally, remember what a restore produces rather than what it repairs.

Show the worked solution
  1. The bucket. Protected across regions by its replication policy, asynchronously, so the destination is behind by some amount.
  2. The volumes. Backed up on a schedule, but backups are not the same as a cross-region copy unless the copy is configured. Check whether the schedule includes copying to another region.
  3. The file system and the database. Not covered by either mechanism mentioned. Each has its own replication, and neither was configured. These are the gaps.
  4. What a restore gives them. New volumes rather than repaired ones, and for each machine two snapshots taken at slightly different moments because there is no volume group. A database restored from a data volume and a log volume seconds apart may not start.
  5. The first three fixes, in order. Put each machine's volumes in a volume group so a snapshot covers one moment. Configure the file system and database replication. Then build the protection group, so that the membership check names anything still missing.

Sanity check. At least two components should turn out to be unprotected, and the volume backups should turn out to be inconsistent rather than absent. If everything looked covered, count the components against the mechanisms they configured.

Your teacher. Ask an agent which instances have more than one attached volume and whether those volumes sit in a volume group. Every machine on that list without a group has backups that look complete and restore to a moment that never happened.
