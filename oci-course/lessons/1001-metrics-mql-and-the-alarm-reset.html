The destination you choose decides how many alarm messages survive a storm

working 11 min Capability area observability, matrix rows metrics-store and alerting.

The one-minute version

  • Service metrics arrive in namespaces at roughly one-minute cadence, and you can publish your own alongside them.
  • They are queried in MQL, a small language whose statements read like CpuUtilization[1m].mean() > 80.
  • An alarm is an MQL statement plus a destination, and it can fire on a threshold or on a metric being absent.
  • The destinations differ enormously in capacity: a notification topic accepts about sixty alarm messages per evaluation while a stream accepts up to one hundred thousand.
  • When a metric stops arriving, the alarm resets after about thirteen minutes, which is a ten-minute internal window plus a three-minute default allowance.

The mental model: a series, a query, a destination

MQL · the shape of a monitoring query
CpuUtilization[1m].mean() > 80

CpuUtilization[5m]{resourceDisplayName = "web-01"}.max() > 90

The metric name comes first, the window in square brackets, an optional filter on dimensions, then the statistic and the comparison. Custom series are published through the metrics posting operation, and a Grafana plugin reads the same store.

The mechanism: the two destinations are not the same size

The mechanism: what happens when the metric stops

The reframing A threshold alarm answers "is this number too high". It cannot answer "is this thing still there", because a missing metric is not a high number. That second question needs an absence alarm, written deliberately, and it is the alarm most estates discover they never wrote.
Alarms carry a trigger delay, a repeat-notification interval and suppression controls, and all three change how loud an incident is. They are also the settings most often left at their defaults and then blamed during a review. Decide the repeat interval for a page in the night deliberately, because the default was chosen for a general case and not for yours.

The trade-off: one minute of resolution, and what it hides

A one-minute cadence is enough for capacity questions, saturation questions and almost every alarm worth waking somebody for. It is not enough to see a burst that begins and ends inside a minute, and no setting on this service makes it finer.

Where sub-minute behaviour matters, the answer is a different family: traces from the next lesson show the individual request, and logs show the individual event. A design that tries to answer a per-request question from a per-minute series will keep finding an average that looks healthy.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An alarm may match thousands of resources at once. Which destination fits?
The stream is the destination sized for that volume. The second names the destination that accepts about sixty per evaluation. The third invents a summarisation step. The fourth invents a cap on matching.
An instance dies while its processor alarm is firing. What does the alarm do?
A missing metric resets the alarm after roughly thirteen minutes. The first assumes acknowledgement semantics the service does not have. The second would be helpful and is exactly what an absence alarm must be written to do. The fourth invents a state.
What does the square bracket in an MQL statement carry?
The bracket holds the window. The first is the brace filter, the third follows the comparison operator, and the fourth is chosen when the metric is published. All three are real parts of the model, which is what makes them plausible.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A fleet of four hundred instances runs behind a load balancer. The team wants to be paged when processor use is sustained above eighty per cent, and to know when an instance stops reporting at all. They write one threshold alarm across the whole fleet, point it at a notification topic that emails the on-call engineer, and leave every other setting at its default.

Find the three problems with that arrangement, and say what you would change for each.

Hint

Count how many messages one evaluation could generate and compare it with the destination's capacity. Then ask which of the team's two stated wishes the alarm they wrote actually covers. The third problem is in the phrase "left at their defaults".

Show the worked solution
  1. The destination is too small. A fleet-wide event could match up to four hundred resources in one evaluation, against a notification capacity of about sixty. Route it to a stream and have something downstream summarise before it reaches a person.
  2. The second wish is not covered. A threshold alarm cannot see a missing metric, so an instance that stops reporting produces nothing. Add a separate absence alarm.
  3. The first alarm will also lie. If a firing instance dies, the alarm resets after about thirteen minutes and reads as recovery. The absence alarm is what turns that silence back into a signal.
  4. The defaults. The trigger delay, repeat interval and suppression were never chosen. Decide the repeat interval against how often the on-call engineer should be reminded, and use the trigger delay to keep a single noisy minute from paging anyone.
  5. What the fixed design looks like. Two alarms rather than one, a stream for the fleet-wide threshold, a notification for the summarised output, and three settings chosen on purpose.

Sanity check. Your answer should end with two alarms and two destinations. If it still has one alarm, ask what fires when an instance goes quiet.

Your teacher. Ask an agent how many alarms in a tenancy fire on absence rather than on a threshold. In most estates the answer is zero, and that number is the difference between noticing a broken thing and noticing a busy one.
