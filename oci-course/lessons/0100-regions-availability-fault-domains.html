OCI nests three failure boundaries, and the middle one is not always there

foundation 9 min Capability area resilience-migration, matrix row region-az-model.

The one-minute version

  • OCI's physical model nests three levels: a region holds one or more availability domains, and every availability domain holds fault domains.
  • The count that never varies is the innermost one. Every availability domain contains exactly three fault domains, in every region, without exception.
  • The count that does vary is the middle one. Many OCI regions carry a single availability domain, so a design that assumes three of them will not deploy.
  • Availability domain names are randomised per tenancy, so the label in your account and the label in someone else's account are not the same building.
  • In a single-availability-domain region, intra-region resilience means fault-domain spread, and genuine site redundancy means a second region.

The mental model: three boxes inside each other

Each level answers a different question about what can fail at once. A fault domain is a grouping of hardware inside one availability domain, so placing two instances in different fault domains keeps them off the same physical host and away from the same local power and network. An availability domain is the data-centre boundary. A region is the geographic one. All of this is stated on Oracle's Regions and Availability Domains page, which is the authority for everything on this lesson.

The reframing On OCI the anti-affinity unit you can always rely on is the fault domain, not the availability domain. Three of them exist in every availability domain of every region, which makes fault-domain spread the one resilience move that is portable across the whole estate.

The mechanism: counting what a region actually gives you

Oracle markets 41 commercial cloud regions across 26 countries on its public cloud regions page, and the documentation is explicit that many of them are single-availability-domain regions. That is the single most consequential fact on this page, because it inverts the habit an engineer brings from another cloud: on OCI, multi-availability-domain deployment is a property of the region you happened to pick, not a property of the platform.

Traffic between availability domains and between regions is encrypted by the platform, so spreading a workload wider does not put plaintext on a wire you do not control.

The trap: the label is not the building

The randomisation trap is quiet rather than loud. A hard-coded availability domain name usually still resolves in the next tenancy, because the same three labels exist there - it just points at different hardware. Nothing fails, and the two halves of a supposedly spread deployment can end up in one building.

The trade-off: what a second region actually costs

Fault-domain spread is free: you choose a fault domain when you place a resource and there is nothing extra to buy. An availability domain spread is free too, where the region has more than one. A second region is a different order of commitment - a separate subscription, replicated data and an orchestrated cutover - and this course reaches the machinery for it in a later module. The judgement this page asks for is narrower: know, before you design, how many of the three boundaries your chosen region actually offers.

What this certification tests Resilience is the lightest weighted area of the OCI Architect Professional blueprint, at 15 per cent for Architect High Availability and Disaster Recovery Solutions, and it is the area where scenario questions most often turn on a single-availability-domain region rather than on a service. The region model itself is one of the four objective areas named in the Architect Associate course.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A tenancy is subscribed to one OCI region. Which count is guaranteed by the documentation regardless of which region it is?
Three fault domains per availability domain is the invariant. The first option is the common wrong assumption and the whole point of the page, because many regions have one availability domain. The third inverts the nesting, and the fourth borrows a word from a level above the region: a realm is a whole separate cloud boundary and nothing counts three of them per region.
A Terraform module launches two instances into the availability domain named in a variable, and the variable is set once for the whole company. What goes wrong?
Availability domain names are randomised per tenancy, so the same label points somewhere else in the next account and the deployment silently loses the spread it thinks it has. The first option is wrong because the label does resolve. The second confuses the level - fault domain choice is a separate placement input. The third invents a failure the model does not produce.
A workload runs in a single-availability-domain region and must survive the loss of a whole data centre. What does the region model say?
The availability domain is the data-centre boundary, so where a region holds one, surviving its loss means leaving the region. The second option confuses the levels: fault domains are inside the one data centre, so all three fall with it. The third and fourth change properties that have nothing to do with the failure boundary at all.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A team is told to run six identical application servers so that no single hardware failure takes out more than a sixth of the capacity, and so that no single data-centre failure takes out more than a third.

Work out whether that is possible in a single-availability-domain OCI region, and what the best achievable spread is in a three-availability-domain region. Show the arithmetic for both.

Hint

Count the isolation slots at each level first. The fault-domain count is fixed per availability domain, so the total is a multiplication, not a lookup.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the slots in a single-availability-domain region. One availability domain gives exactly 3 fault domains, so six servers spread evenly means two per fault domain. Losing one fault domain removes 2 of 6, which is a third, not a sixth. The hardware requirement is not met.
  2. Check the data-centre requirement there. There is one availability domain, so losing it removes 6 of 6. The data-centre requirement is not met either, and no arrangement inside the region can meet it.
  3. Count the slots in a three-availability-domain region. Three availability domains times three fault domains each gives 3 × 3 = 9 distinct hardware slots. Six servers fit in six of them, one each, so losing one fault domain removes 1 of 6 - exactly a sixth.
  4. Check the data-centre requirement there. Place two servers in each availability domain, in different fault domains. Losing one availability domain removes 2 of 6, which is a third. Both requirements are met.

Sanity check. The slot count in the second region should be nine, and six is smaller than nine, so a one-per-slot answer must exist. If your single-availability-domain answer came out able to survive a data-centre loss, you counted fault domains as data centres.

Your teacher. If the three levels still blur together, ask an agent to list the availability domains in a real tenancy and then, for one of them, the fault domains - and to say which of the two lists it could have predicted without calling the API. Only one of them is predictable, and noticing which is the lesson.
