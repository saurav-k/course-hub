Two Kafka products share one platform, and partition count is what separates them

working 10 min Capability area analytics, matrix row stream-ingest.

The one-minute version

  • Streaming is the serverless one: an Apache Kafka compatible event log with partitions, a seven-day replay window and Kafka-protocol access authenticated by IAM.
  • Streaming with Apache Kafka is the managed-cluster one: fully managed Kafka clusters, complete Kafka API compatibility, no partition ceiling and a stated availability commitment.
  • The second is managed but not serverless. You size a cluster, and that sizing is the work the first service removes.
  • Oracle's guidance draws the line at scale: the classic service suits application-to-application messaging up to roughly five hundred partitions per tenancy per region.
  • Two services with almost the same name is a naming trap. Read the documentation title, not the word "streaming" in a colleague's message.

The mental model: the same protocol, two operating models

Streaming is the serverless service: partitions, a seven-day replay window, and Kafka-protocol access authenticated through IAM. Streaming with Apache Kafka is the managed-cluster service, fully Kafka API compatible with no partition ceiling and a stated availability commitment, and its own page carries a comparison table between the two.

The mechanism: where the line is drawn

The reframing Two products with nearly the same name is a documentation problem before it is an architecture problem. When somebody says "we are using OCI Streaming", the useful follow-up is which documentation page they had open, because the two services differ in whether a cluster exists and in how far they scale.

The trade-off: serverless removes sizing and adds a boundary

The serverless service takes cluster sizing off the table entirely, which is genuinely most of the operational work of running Kafka. In exchange it comes with guidance about the scale it suits, and crossing that guidance means changing service rather than changing a setting.

The managed cluster service scales past that boundary and is the one Oracle points at for change-data-capture pipelines, device telemetry and stream analytics at scale. Its cost is that a cluster exists: it has a size, it is paid for whether busy or idle, and somebody has to decide what that size should be. Its superuser credentials live in Vault, which module 08 covers.

Neither of these is the answer to every message-shaped problem on this platform. The verified inventory records five services that all look like messaging from a distance, and picking by name rather than by behaviour is a documented way to get it wrong. Module 12 is where the five are separated properly; this page is only about the two that speak Kafka.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What separates the two Kafka-speaking services on this platform?
Both speak Kafka, and the difference is whether a cluster exists to size and pay for. The first denies the compatibility both services claim. The second invents a regional split. The fourth invents a retention figure for the managed service that no page on this platform states.
A design needs several thousand partitions. Which service does the guidance point to?
Partition count is the documented dividing line and the managed cluster service is the one with no stated ceiling. The second invents a support path. The third denies the criterion. The fourth is true about the protocol and irrelevant to the question, which is what makes it the strongest distractor.
A consumer is down for nine days against the classic Streaming service. What happens?
Retention is seven days, so two days of records have expired. The first assumes an unbounded log. The third invents back pressure onto producers. The fourth invents an archive behaviour that this service does not have.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A team plans one topic per customer for a hundred customers, with twelve partitions each, in a single region. They also plan a nightly consumer that reads the day's records into the lake, and that consumer has historically been down for as long as four days during a holiday period.

Work out the partition count, decide which service the guidance points to, and say what the four-day outage means for the design.

Hint

Multiply before you judge. Then compare the total with the documented guidance figure rather than with your sense of how much data a hundred customers produce. Handle the outage question separately, against the retention window.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the partitions. 100 topics × 12 partitions = 1,200 partitions in one region.
  2. Compare with the guidance. The classic service is recommended up to roughly 500 partitions per tenancy per region, and 1,200 is well past that, so the guidance points at Streaming with Apache Kafka.
  3. Notice what did not decide it. Nothing in the question said how much data a hundred customers produce. The partition count crossed the line on its own, which is the trap in the topic-per-customer pattern.
  4. Judge the outage. Four days is inside the seven-day window, so a historical outage of that length loses nothing. It also leaves only three days of margin, so the design should alarm on consumer lag rather than trust the margin.

Sanity check. The partition total should be well over the guidance figure while the data volume is never mentioned, and the outage should be inside the retention window rather than outside it. If your answer turned on throughput, re-read what the guidance is actually measured in.

Your teacher. Ask an agent for the total partition count across every stream in a region, not the count per topic. Topic-per-tenant designs grow that total quietly, and the number nobody has added up is the one that decides which of these two services the design belongs on.
