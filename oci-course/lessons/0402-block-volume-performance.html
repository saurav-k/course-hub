A volume's speed is a number you buy per gigabyte, and it can move while the volume is live

working 10 min Capability area storage, matrix row block-storage.

The one-minute version

  • Block Volume performance is bought as volume performance units per gigabyte, so speed and size are one purchase rather than two.
  • The ladder has four named rungs: Lower Cost at 0, Balanced at 10 which is the default, Higher Performance at 20, and Ultra High Performance from 30 to 120.
  • Ultra High Performance reaches up to 300,000 operations per second on one volume.
  • The setting can be changed on an existing volume, and an auto-tune option moves it for you: up quickly, down slowly.
  • A separate auto-tune applies when a volume is detached, dropping it to the lowest rung so an idle disk stops paying for speed.

The mental model: one dial, four named positions

Two details on the ends of the ladder matter in practice. Lower Cost at zero units is available for block volumes but not for boot volumes, so a machine's own disk cannot be taken to the bottom rung. And Ultra High Performance is where the 300,000 operations per second figure lives, on Oracle's Block Volume performance reference.

The reframing Because performance is bought per gigabyte, size and speed are the same purchase. A small volume at a high rung and a large volume at a low rung are different points on one line, and a workload that needs operations rather than capacity is buying gigabytes to get them.

The mechanism: auto-tune is deliberately asymmetric

Oracle's own engineering blog on dynamic performance scaling describes the upward steps as arriving within seconds and the downward ones as deliberately gradual. The setting itself is changed on a live volume through the change performance path, so none of this requires a new volume.

Detached auto-tune is the one that quietly saves money and quietly costs it. A volume detached for a week stops paying for performance it is not using - which is right. A volume detached and reattached during a maintenance window comes back at the bottom rung and climbs from there, so the first minutes after a reattach are slower than the last minutes before the detach, with nothing in the configuration to explain it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A volume is created and nothing is said about performance. What rung does it get?
Balanced at ten units is the documented default, so an unconsidered volume already sits on a middle rung. The first would be the cheapest and is not the default, the third is a deliberate step above it, and the fourth describes auto-tune rather than a starting point.
Why does auto-tune raise performance quickly and lower it slowly?
The asymmetry is deliberate: it errs towards the cheap mistake, paying a little longer rather than throttling a workload that has not finished. The first invents a pricing rule, the second invents a schedule, and the fourth confuses attached auto-tune with the separate detached behaviour.
A database spans four volumes. What captures them consistently?
A volume group gives a crash-consistent capture across every member at one instant. The second sounds close and is not the same thing - four independent snapshots describe four different moments. The third and fourth are useful and neither of them makes the four captures simultaneous.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A reporting database sits on a 2 TB block volume at the Balanced rung. It is idle for twenty-two hours a day and then, for two hours each night, drives as much input and output as it can get. The team asks whether to move it permanently to Ultra High Performance.

Compare the permanent move against enabling auto-tune, say what fraction of the day each one pays a premium for, and name the one operational habit that would undo the auto-tune answer.

Hint

Work out the share of the day the workload is actually demanding, then ask what each option is paying for during the rest of it. The habit to look for is anything that returns the volume to a rung it did not choose.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the demanding share. Two hours out of twenty-four is 2 ÷ 24 = 8.3 per cent of the day. The other 91.7 per cent needs nothing above the default.
  2. Price the permanent move. Ultra High Performance starts at 30 units per gigabyte against the Balanced 10, so the premium is paid for the whole day to serve 8.3 per cent of it - roughly twelve hours of premium for every hour of use.
  3. Price auto-tune. Auto-tune raises the units within seconds of the demand arriving and lowers them gradually afterwards, so the premium is paid for the two hours plus a tail. The exact tail is not published, but the shape is clear: a little over 8.3 per cent rather than 100 per cent.
  4. Name the habit that undoes it. Detaching the volume. Detached auto-tune drops it to the lowest rung, so a maintenance window that detaches and reattaches leaves the volume climbing from the bottom at the moment the nightly job starts - and nothing in the volume's configuration will explain the slow night.

Sanity check. Auto-tune should come out cheaper by roughly an order of magnitude in premium-hours, because the workload is demanding for less than a tenth of the day. If the two options came out close, the idle period was priced as though it needed the higher rung.

Your teacher. Ask an agent for the performance rung of every block volume in a compartment, and separately for which of them are detached. Any detached volume above the lowest rung is paying for speed nothing is using, and that list is usually longer than expected.
