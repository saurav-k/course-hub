Module 11 · Security services · Lesson 1101

A packet must satisfy four separate things, and the newest one is written about intent

frontier 10 min Capability area security-services, matrix row zero-trust-routing.

The one-minute version

  • Zero Trust Packet Routing expresses network access as a policy over attributes attached to resources, rather than over addresses and rule lists.
  • It is an additional check. A packet must pass the route table, the subnet and card rule sets, and this policy.
  • A resource may carry at most three security attributes, which forces the vocabulary to stay small.
  • It does not police traffic arriving from the internet or from on premises. That is a documented boundary rather than a gap to work around.
  • Its intended use is defence in depth beside private service endpoints and the deny statements of lesson 0801, not a replacement for the rule sets of lesson 0700.

The mental model: four checks in series

The mechanism: attributes rather than addresses

The reframing This layer is where a network policy becomes reviewable by somebody who does not read address ranges. "The reporting tier may reach the customer database" is a sentence a risk owner can approve or reject. Nothing else in module 07 produces an artefact that reads like that.
The documented boundary matters as much as the mechanism. This layer does not police ingress paths from the internet or from on premises. Traffic arriving through those doors is governed by the gateways, the rule sets and the firewalls of the previous page. Assuming otherwise would leave the most exposed path in an estate unprotected while a policy document says it is covered.

The mechanism: what it is designed to sit beside

The trade-off: a small vocabulary is a real constraint

Three attributes per resource keeps policies readable and keeps the number of possible sentences small enough to review. It also means the layer cannot express a fine-grained matrix, and any attempt to encode one will run out of attributes on the first resource that belongs to several intents at once.

The intended division of labour follows from that. Coarse intent lives here, where it is auditable. Ports, protocols and tier-to-tier relationships stay in the network security groups of lesson 0700, where the vocabulary is unbounded and the review is an engineering one.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A packet is allowed by this policy but denied by a security list. What happens?
The checks are additive, so a denial anywhere stops the packet. The first and second both assume this layer can override an existing control, which it cannot. The fourth invents a conflict state that the service does not report.
Which traffic does this policy layer deliberately not police?
The documented boundary is ingress from the internet and from on premises. The other three describe paths inside the platform, which are exactly what the layer is for, and assuming it covers the external ones is the error this page exists to prevent.
Why is the three-attribute limit useful rather than merely restrictive?
A small vocabulary is what makes the policy readable by a non-engineer, which is the layer's whole purpose. The first invents a performance rationale. The third is a coincidence of numbers from lesson 0100. The fourth misreads an attribute as a reachability quota.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

An estate has grown to several hundred network security group rules across six networks. A risk owner asks for a written statement of which systems may reach the customer database, and an engineer offers to export the rule set. The customer database is also reachable from a partner over the private circuit.

Design an attribute policy that answers the risk owner's question, say what it leaves to the existing rules, and state plainly what it does not cover.

Hint

Ask what the risk owner is actually asking: a sentence, not a rule dump. Then check the attribute budget against the number of distinct intents. The last part of the question is about the documented boundary rather than about your design.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the intents. Give the customer database an attribute meaning "customer data", and give the systems that may reach it an attribute naming their role, such as "reporting" or "application tier". Keep the list short: three attributes per resource is the ceiling.
  2. Write the sentences. The policy then reads as a small number of statements about which attribute may reach which. That is the artefact the risk owner can approve, and it is stable when addresses change.
  3. Leave the detail below. Ports, protocols and the tier-to-tier detail stay in the network security groups. The attribute layer says who may talk to whom; the groups say how.
  4. Say what is not covered. The partner arriving over the private circuit is on an on-premises ingress path, which this layer does not police. That path is governed by the routing gateway's attachment tables, the rule sets, and the network firewall of the previous lesson.
  5. Answer the engineer. Exporting several hundred rules answers a different question. A rule export shows what is configured; the risk owner asked what is intended, and only the attribute policy states that.

Sanity check. Your policy should be a handful of sentences, not hundreds, and it should explicitly exclude the partner path. If it grew past a few attributes per resource, the fine-grained detail has leaked up from where it belongs.

Your teacher. Ask an agent to write, as plain sentences, which systems may currently reach your most sensitive data store. If that takes several hundred rules to answer, the answer is the argument for this layer, and the sentences you end up writing are the policy.
