Module 10 · Observability and audit · Lesson 1000

Three log families feed one store, and only the control-plane one is on without asking

working 11 min Capability area audit-telemetry, matrix rows control-plane-audit-log and log-store.

The one-minute version

  • Three families land in one place: audit logs from the control plane, service logs enabled per resource, and custom logs from your own applications.
  • They live in log groups, which are ordinary resources that policy statements can be written against, and one search language reads all of them.
  • The audit stream is always on, cannot be disabled, and costs nothing. Its retention is fixed at three hundred and sixty-five days for the whole tenancy and cannot be changed.
  • Audit records API calls. It does not record data-plane actions such as reading an object, or anything that happens inside a database.
  • Its listing API is not a bulk export. Build the export on Connector Hub from the first day rather than reconstructing history by paging through calls.

The mental model: three sources, one destination

Oracle indexes twenty-nine service log categories in its reference, so the second family is a long list rather than a handful. The next lesson but one walks the streams that matter most; the reference is the authority for the tail.

The mechanism: the audit stream, and its fixed clock

What audit does not capture is as important as what it does. It records the control plane, so creating a bucket appears and reading an object from it does not. Object access is a separate service log that is off until enabled, network packets are never in it, and what happens inside a database is not in it either. A question that begins "who read" almost always needs a different stream from a question that begins "who created".

The mechanism: the export path, and the one that looks like it

The reframing Treat the audit stream as a rolling window with a hard edge, not as an archive. It is complete, free and untouchable for a year, and then it is gone. Whether you have five years of history is decided by a connector somebody configured, not by the audit service.

The trade-off: an unchangeable default is a guarantee and a ceiling

Nobody can disable the audit stream, shorten its retention, or exclude a noisy service from it. That is unusual and genuinely valuable: an investigation can assume the record exists without first proving that somebody left it turned on.

The same rigidity means the retention cannot be extended either, and there is no tier to buy that changes it. The only lever is the export, and the storage tiers of module 04 are then what decide how much a decade of audit history costs to keep.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which question can the audit stream alone answer?
Audit records control-plane calls, so creating a bucket is in it. The first is a data-plane action needing a service log. The third happens inside a database. The fourth needs flow logs, which are off by default.
A rule requires five years of API history. What has to be built?
Retention is fixed and the export path is Connector Hub. The first names a setting the documentation says cannot be changed. The second is exactly the approach the vendor documentation warns against. The third invents a per-compartment trail that does not exist.
What decides who can read a particular set of logs?
Log groups are ordinary resources, so the policy language scopes access to them. The first confuses lifetime with access. The second invents a parallel model. The fourth ties access to whoever configured the log, which is not how any resource on this platform works.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An incident review asks four questions about something that happened eight months ago. Who deleted a production instance. Who downloaded a particular object from a bucket. Which application errors preceded the deletion. And whether any of this will still be answerable in two years.

Say, for each question, which family the answer lives in and whether it was available by default, then answer the fourth question about what is needed to make all of it durable.

Hint

Sort the first three by whether they describe a control-plane call, a data-plane action or an application's own output. Only one of the three is available without anyone having turned anything on.

Show the worked solution
  1. Who deleted the instance. The audit family, and it is available: the stream is always on, and eight months is inside the fixed retention.
  2. Who downloaded the object. A service log for bucket access, which is off until somebody enabled it for that bucket. If it was not enabled eight months ago, the answer does not exist anywhere.
  3. The application errors. The custom family, arriving through the agent or the ingestion API. Available only if the application was shipping its logs, and retained for whatever period was configured on that log.
  4. Two years from now. Nothing survives on its own. Audit reaches a year and then stops, and service and custom logs keep whatever retention was set. Durability means a connector delivering all three into Object Storage, and then the tier decisions of module 04.
  5. The lesson to draw. Two of the three questions depended on a decision made before the incident, and the platform gave no warning at the time that the decision had been made by default.

Sanity check. Exactly one of the three questions should be answerable with no prior configuration. If you answered all three from audit alone, re-read which family a bucket download belongs to.

Your teacher. Ask an agent which log categories are enabled anywhere in a tenancy, and compare that list against the resources that matter. The gap is what the next incident review will not be able to answer, and it is far cheaper to close now than during the review.
