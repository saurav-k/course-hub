A cheaper tier charges for time you have not used yet

working 10 min Capability area storage, matrix rows object-storage and archive-storage.

The one-minute version

  • Object Storage has two live tiers - Standard and Infrequent Access - and Archive beside them as the cold one.
  • The cheaper tiers charge a minimum retention: thirty-one days for Infrequent Access, ninety for Archive. Deleting early does not save the money, it bills the remainder.
  • Infrequent Access also charges a retrieval fee, so a tier chosen for storage price can lose the saving on reads.
  • Auto-tiering moves objects larger than one mebibyte between Standard and Infrequent Access at no cost, which removes the guesswork.
  • It also conflicts with lifecycle rules that target Infrequent Access, so the two mechanisms are alternatives rather than layers.

The mental model: three tiers and one clock each

Infrequent Access also carries a retrieval fee, which is the second half of the trade. A tier is cheaper per gigabyte-month and dearer per read, so the break-even is a ratio between how much you keep and how often you touch it rather than a property of the data.

The reframing A storage tier is a bet on access frequency, and OCI makes you pay the bet in advance as a minimum retention. That is why guessing badly is expensive in both directions: too cold and you pay for reads and for days you did not use, too warm and you pay for storage you did not need.

The mechanism: auto-tiering takes the bet for you

Auto-tiering conflicts with lifecycle rules that target Infrequent Access. The two are alternative ways to answer the same question, and enabling both means two mechanisms deciding one object's tier. Pick one per bucket: auto-tiering when access is unpredictable, lifecycle rules when the cooling schedule is known in advance.

The trade-off: what a bucket does not do until asked

The events point is the one that catches designs. A pattern like "an upload triggers a function" needs the bucket to emit object events, and the Events rule alone is not enough. Module 12 works the event machinery properly; what matters here is that the switch lives on the bucket.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An object is written to the Infrequent Access tier and deleted after four days. What is charged?
Infrequent Access carries a thirty-one-day minimum retention, so deleting early bills the remainder rather than saving it. The first option ignores the commitment, the second invents a grace period, and the fourth applies Archive's ninety-day minimum to the wrong tier.
A bucket holds millions of files of about 200 KB each. What does auto-tiering do for it?
Auto-tiering only considers objects larger than one mebibyte, so a bucket of small files is untouched. The second and third describe behaviours it would have if the threshold did not exist, and the fourth inverts the real interaction: the conflict is with lifecycle rules targeting Infrequent Access, and it is a reason to choose one, not a block.
An upload should trigger a function, the Events rule is correct, and nothing fires. What is the most likely cause?
A bucket must opt in to emitting object events before any rule can match them. The first would break the function's own calls rather than the trigger, the second is legal and would not stop matching, and the third names a different switch on the same bucket that feeds logs rather than events.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A media pipeline writes 40 TB of intermediate renders a month. Each render is a single file of about 4 GB, is read two or three times in its first week, and is deleted after twenty days. A cost review proposes moving the bucket to Infrequent Access to save money.

Evaluate the proposal against the minimum retention and the retrieval fee, say what the twenty-day lifetime does to it, and give the change that would actually save money.

Hint

Compare the object's lifetime against the tier's minimum before comparing any prices. Then ask whether the reads happen during the period the cheaper tier charges for retrieval.

Show the worked solution
  1. Compare lifetime to commitment. The objects live 20 days and Infrequent Access commits 31. Every object is therefore billed for 11 days it does not exist, which is 55 per cent more storage-days than the workload actually uses.
  2. Add the reads. The two or three reads all fall in the first week, so they land inside the tier that charges a retrieval fee. The proposal moves the reads into the fee and the storage into a commitment, which is the worst of both halves of the trade.
  3. Check whether auto-tiering rescues it. The files are 4 GB, comfortably over the one-mebibyte threshold, so they are eligible - but auto-tiering moves cold objects, and these are read in their first week and deleted in their third. There is no cold period for it to find.
  4. Give the change that saves money. Leave the bucket in Standard and shorten the lifetime. Deleting at fourteen days instead of twenty removes 30 per cent of the storage-days at no retrieval cost, which is a real saving where the tier change was a loss.

Sanity check. The tier proposal should come out worse rather than merely neutral, because it adds both a commitment and a fee. If it came out cheaper, the eleven days of unused minimum retention were not counted.

Your teacher. Ask an agent for the average lifetime of the objects in one bucket. If that number is under thirty-one days, no cheaper tier can save money on it, and the conversation moves from tiering to lifecycle in one step.
