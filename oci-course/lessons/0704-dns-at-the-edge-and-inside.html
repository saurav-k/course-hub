Module 07 · Networking and delivery · Lesson 0704

Two resolution worlds share one service, and only the public one can be steered

working 10 min Capability area dns-domains, matrix rows authoritative-dns, private-dns and dns-routing-policies.

The one-minute version

  • OCI DNS serves authoritative public zones from anycast addresses and supports DNSSEC. It also serves private zones inside a virtual network.
  • OCI does not sell domain registrations. You register the name at a registrar and delegate the zone here, which is a normal arrangement rather than a limitation.
  • Inside a network, the default resolver answers internal names, and resolver endpoints with forwarders extend that to on-premises name servers.
  • Steering policies apply to public zones only. A private zone gets no failover, weighting or geographic answer.
  • Six policy kinds exist, evaluated as a pipeline, and the record's time to live is what decides how quickly a failover reaches users.

The mental model: three names, three resolution paths

OCI DNS serves the public side from anycast addresses with DNSSEC available. Because Oracle does not sell registrations, the usual arrangement is to register the name elsewhere and delegate the zone here, which is also why almost every public zone on this platform starts life as a delegation rather than as a transfer.

The mechanism: a steering policy is a pipeline of rules

The reframing Steering is a property of the public zone, not of the workload. That is why it cannot help a private name: there is no public record for a policy to attach to. Resilience for an internal endpoint is built from the load balancers and the routing of the previous two pages instead.

The mechanism: the time to live is the failover clock

The trade-off: quiet records fail over slowly

A short life makes a failover reach clients quickly and asks the resolvers far more questions, which costs query volume. A long life is cheap and quiet, and it means a proportion of users keep trying a region that Health Checks marked unhealthy some time ago. There is no setting that is good at both, and the honest move is to pick the number from the recovery objective rather than from habit.

One asymmetry between the two halves is worth carrying forward. Query logging exists for the private resolver and does not exist for public zones, and even on the private side an answer served from cache is not logged until the cached entry expires, so counts undercount by the cache hit rate. Module 10 covers this properly. The consequence here is that a question like "who looked up this public name" has no answer from DNS on this platform, and has to be asked of the load balancer or firewall logs instead.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A team wants failover steering for a name used only inside their network. Possible?
Steering attaches to public zones, so an internal name gets its resilience from the balancers and routing instead. The first and third both invent a way to attach a policy to a private zone. The fourth invents a dependency on a signing feature that has nothing to do with steering.
Where is a domain name registered for use with OCI DNS?
Oracle states that it is not a registrar, so registration happens elsewhere and the zone is delegated here. The first assumes a service that is not offered. The second confuses identity domains with domain names. The third misses that delegation has to be recorded at the registrar for anyone to find the zone.
A steering policy has no health checks attached. What happens on an outage?
Health is a stage in the pipeline, so with nothing feeding it the failing answer is never dropped. The second and fourth invent fallback behaviours the documentation does not describe. The third would be a safe design and is not what happens, which is exactly why this configuration is worth checking.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A service is published as a public name steered across two regions, with a recovery objective stating that no more than two minutes of user traffic may go to a failed region after it is detected. The same service is also reached internally by a private name that the application uses, and by an on-premises reporting job that resolves a third name held on the company's own name servers.

Set the record's time to live from the objective, say what carries the internal name's resilience, and name what makes the on-premises name resolvable from an instance.

Hint

Deal with the three names separately. Only one of them has a steering policy available. For the first, read the objective as a ceiling on the worst case rather than as an average.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the objective as a worst case. A client that cached the old answer may reuse it for the whole stated life, so the life must be at most the allowed window: 120 seconds or less.
  2. Pick a value and accept the cost. Something at or under two minutes, knowing that shorter values mean more queries. Attach Health Checks so that the health stage of the pipeline has something to act on.
  3. The internal name. No steering is available for it. Its resilience comes from a load balancer in front of the backends and from the routing of the previous lessons, not from DNS.
  4. The on-premises name. A private resolver endpoint with a forwarder pointing at the company's own name servers, so the instance's single resolver reaches a zone this platform does not host.

Sanity check. The time to live must be at or below the two-minute window rather than near it, and exactly one of the three names should have a steering policy. If the internal name got one, re-read which zone kind policies attach to.

Your teacher. Ask an agent whether every steering policy in a tenancy has health checks attached to it. A policy without them looks configured, reports no error, and quietly sends users to a region that stopped answering.
