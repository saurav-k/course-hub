Module 10 · Observability and audit · Lesson 1002

Most streams are off, and every one of them has a documented blind spot

frontier 12 min Capability area audit-telemetry, matrix rows network-flow-log, load-balancer-access-log, data-plane-access-log, dns-query-log and service-specific-log.

The one-minute version

  • Of the fifteen documented streams below, three are on without being asked, two are partly on, and the remaining ten are off until somebody enables them.
  • Every stream misses something on purpose, and the misses are documented rather than discovered. Flow logs never carry payloads. Gateway logs carry metadata, not bodies.
  • Two misses are unusual enough to name. Private DNS query logs do not record an answer served from cache, and FastConnect has no log category at all.
  • Load balancer logs admit to skipping records under stress, and say so with their own error entries.
  • So the design question is never "do we have logs". It is which question do we need to answer, and which stream was enabled before the thing happened.

The mechanism: the undercount nobody expects

The reframing Read a telemetry design as a list of questions with an owner, not as a list of logs to enable. For each question worth asking during an incident, name the stream that answers it and confirm that stream is on. Everything else is storage you are paying for.
Four absences in this area are worth stating exactly, because each is easy to assume the other way. There is no stream recording the cloud operator's own staff access to your content. Identity domain events land in the ordinary audit stream rather than in a separate directory audit trail. There is no dedicated stream of authorisation denials: a refusal appears as an audit record with a failing response. And subnet rules and network security groups emit no per-rule verdict log, so flow logs carrying accepted and rejected traffic are what stands in.

The trade-off: every stream you enable is a bill and a decision

Enabling everything is not the safe default it appears to be. Ingestion is charged, high-volume streams are the most expensive and least often read, and a search across a store full of unread data is slower than one across a store full of chosen data.

The opposite failure is the one this page exists to prevent. A stream that was off when something happened cannot be turned on retrospectively, and no error message appears at the moment the gap is created. The middle path is to enable the streams that answer the questions your incident reviews actually ask, and to write that list down.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which visibility does the private circuit service provide?
There is no log category for it; visibility comes from metrics and from the audit trail of its lifecycle. The first and second attribute log streams to it that the reference does not list. The third describes a stream that does not exist anywhere on this platform.
A private DNS query count looks lower than expected. What is the likely reason?
Cache hits are not logged, so counts are a lower bound. The second borrows sampling from flow logs, a different stream. The third invents a filter. The fourth is true of public zones and does not explain a low count inside the network.
Where does an authorisation denial appear?
A refusal is an audit record carrying the failing response. The first names a stream this platform does not have. The second confuses an authorisation decision with a network one. The fourth is the absence error, and it would make every access investigation impossible.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A public service is being designed. The team lists the questions they expect to ask during an incident: which client addresses hit the service, which requests the firewall blocked and why, which pod produced an error, whether a customer's file was downloaded, and whether the private circuit to the data centre was degraded.

Name the stream for each question, mark which are off by default, and identify the one question whose answer will be incomplete however carefully it is configured.

Hint

Work down the table for each question in turn. Four of the five have a named stream. For the fifth, check the table row that says the category does not exist rather than assuming it must be somewhere.

Show the worked solution
  1. Client addresses. Load balancer access logs, off by default. Note the documented behaviour that records can be skipped under stress, so counts from this stream are approximate during exactly the busiest moments.
  2. Blocked requests and the reason. Web firewall logs, off by default, with short service-side retention and an option to deliver directly to a bucket for a longer keep.
  3. The pod that produced an error. Cluster logs. The control-plane part is on, but pod output needs an agent configured for it, so this is partly on and the part you want is the part that is not.
  4. Whether a file was downloaded. Object storage access logs, off by default and per bucket. If they were not on at the time, this question has no answer at all.
  5. The private circuit. This is the incomplete one. There is no log category for it, so the answer comes from metrics and from audit records of its lifecycle, and no per-connection detail exists to find.

Sanity check. Four of the five should be off or partly off by default, and exactly one should have no log stream at all. If every question had a stream you could enable, look again at the private circuit row.

Your teacher. Ask an agent to list the questions your last three incident reviews actually asked, then check each one against this table. The mismatch between the streams enabled and the questions asked is usually total, and it is cheap to fix once it is written down.

Primary source to go deeper

Read the service log reference. It is the index of every category the platform publishes, and the absence of a service from it is the fastest way to learn that a stream you assumed exists does not.
