Module 14 · Resilience and migration · Lesson 1401

A rehearsal that touches production is not a rehearsal, which is why the drill is a separate plan

working 10 min Capability area resilience-migration, matrix rows resilience-assessment and well-architected-framework.

The one-minute version

  • A drill stands up an isolated replica in the standby region and leaves the primary serving throughout. Starting and stopping it are the two operations.
  • That makes it the closest thing this platform offers to resilience testing: a rehearsal whose failure costs nothing.
  • It answers a question a precheck cannot: does the workload actually work once it is standing in the other region.
  • Deliberate fault injection is not a first-party service here. Where it is needed, the honest answer is third-party tooling running on your own cluster.
  • The Well-Architected Framework is published guidance across five pillars, and it is documentation rather than a console tool that reviews a workload for you.

The mental model: three levels of confidence

The mechanism: what a drill actually does

The reframing A recovery capability is not a configuration, it is a habit with a date on it. The question worth asking about a workload is not whether it has a plan but when the plan last ran, and the drill exists so that the answer can be recent without anyone taking a risk to make it so.
Be exact about what this is not. A drill rehearses a recovery; it does not inject faults into a running system to see what breaks. The verified inventory behind this course records that deliberate fault injection is not a first-party service on this platform, and that the practical answer is third-party tooling running on your own cluster. Saying "this platform has resilience testing" is fair if you mean drills, and misleading if the listener means fault injection.

The mechanism: the framework, and what it is not

The Well-Architected Framework here is a published set of pillars covering operational excellence, security, reliability, performance and cost, with the platform's services mapped onto them. It is guidance to read and apply.

What it is not is an automated review that inspects a workload and produces findings. There is no console tool of that kind, so a review against these pillars is a conversation somebody runs using the document, and the findings come from people rather than from a scan. Treat it as the structure for that conversation rather than as a service to enable.

The trade-off: a rehearsal costs an afternoon and buys the only real evidence

Running a drill takes an operator's time, produces no customer-visible improvement, and occasionally reveals that the plan does not work, which is uncomfortable. Every one of those is a reason teams do not do it.

What it buys is the difference between believing and knowing, and there is no cheaper way to obtain it. Given that the standby capacity is already paid for, the marginal cost of a drill is close to the cost of scheduling one, and a plan drilled twice a year is a different asset from a plan drilled never.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What happens to the primary region during a drill?
A drill uses an isolated replica and does not touch production. The second and fourth describe a switchover. The third describes a canary shift from module 12, which is a delivery technique rather than a recovery rehearsal.
A plan's prechecks pass. What is still unknown?
Prechecks verify readiness, not behaviour, so only a drill shows the workload standing up. The first and fourth are among the things prechecks look at. The second is a policy question and not what prechecks are for.
How should deliberate fault injection be described on this platform?
The inventory records no first-party fault injection service and names third-party tooling as the answer. The first invents the service. The second gives drills a capability they do not have. The third turns an absence into a prohibition, which is a much stronger and wrong claim.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An auditor asks a team to demonstrate that a critical workload can recover to a second region. The team has a protection group whose prechecks passed last week. They also want to know whether the application handles a database becoming slow rather than absent, and they ask what a recognised best-practice review would say about the design.

Answer all three requests, naming what satisfies each and stating plainly where this platform does not have a service.

Hint

The three requests sit at three different levels. One is answered by a drill, one is about a failure mode a drill does not produce, and one is about a document rather than a tool.

Show the worked solution
  1. The demonstration. Run a drill. It stands up an isolated copy in the standby region, the team confirms the workload comes up and serves, and the primary is untouched. That is evidence rather than a claim.
  2. Why the precheck is not enough. Passing prechecks says both sides are configured as the plan expects. It says nothing about whether the application starts correctly over there, which is what the auditor is really asking.
  3. The slow database. Not something a drill produces. This is fault injection, and there is no first-party service for it here. The honest answer is third-party tooling running on their own cluster, chosen and operated by them.
  4. The best-practice review. The Well-Architected pillars give the structure: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance and cost. Say clearly that this is documentation to review against rather than a console tool that produces findings, so somebody has to run the review.
  5. What to write down. The drill date, its result, and the two gaps: no first-party fault injection, and no automated review tool. An auditor is better served by an accurate list of gaps than by a claim that everything is covered.

Sanity check. One request should be fully satisfied by a service, and two should be answered with a named gap and a named alternative. If all three came back as capabilities of this platform, re-read what a drill does and does not do.

Your teacher. Ask an agent when each protection group last completed a drill. A group with a passing precheck and no drill in its history is the exact state this page is about, and the date is the only number that matters.
