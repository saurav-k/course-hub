A tenancy lives in one realm, and the realm decides which regions exist at all

foundation 9 min Capability areas resilience-migration and hybrid-connectivity, matrix rows region-az-model and on-prem-extension.

The one-minute version

  • Regions are grouped into realms. A tenancy belongs to exactly one realm and cannot reach a region outside it, whatever the identity model allows.
  • The commercial realm is the big one. Government and sovereign realms are separate clouds with their own, much shorter region lists.
  • Inside a realm a tenancy still has to subscribe to each region it uses, and trial or pay-as-you-go tenancies start limited to one subscribed region.
  • Region subscription is one of the controls a parent tenancy can lock onto a child, which is why it appears again in the governance module.
  • OCI also runs where there is no region: the same control plane and the same APIs ship as rack-scale systems and portable devices installed on your own site.

The mental model: a realm is a separate cloud

Oracle's Regions and Availability Domains page states the rule plainly: a tenancy exists in a single realm and cannot access regions outside it. The service availability page is where the separate sovereign and government lists are published, and the US Government Cloud documentation names the OC2 realm regions and the authorisations they carry.

The mechanism: how big each realm actually is

Being in a realm is necessary but not sufficient. A tenancy also has to subscribe to each region it wants to use, and the documentation records a starting limit that surprises people running their first proof of concept: trial and pay-as-you-go tenancies begin restricted to one subscribed region, while tenancies on Universal Credits can subscribe broadly.

Region subscription is not only a self-service switch. Under Organization Management, a parent tenancy can attach a governance rule naming the regions a child tenancy is allowed to subscribe to, and the child cannot change it. If a subscription appears locked with no local explanation, look up the tenancy tree rather than at the local policy.

Where OCI runs when there is no region

The sovereign cloud page is where Oracle positions Dedicated Region and Alloy alongside the EU Sovereign regions, and it is the reason the residency conversation on OCI has three answers rather than one: a commercial region, a sovereign realm, or a rack in your own building.

The reframing On OCI "where does this run" is three nested questions and not one. Which realm fixes the universe of regions. Which subscriptions fix the ones this tenancy can use. Which region is the only one most designs actually discuss, and it is the last and least binding of the three.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A commercial-realm tenancy needs to place a workload in a US Government region. What has to happen?
A tenancy cannot reach a region outside its own realm, so the answer is a second tenancy in the government realm. The first and fourth options describe real mechanisms that only work inside one realm, and the second describes a policy grant, which cannot create reachability that the realm boundary denies.
A new pay-as-you-go tenancy cannot subscribe to a second region. What is the most likely reason?
Trial and pay-as-you-go tenancies begin limited to one subscribed region, which is the documented default. The second option is possible but far less likely for an ordinary commercial region. The third is impossible because the home region is fixed at sign-up, and the fourth names something that has no bearing on subscription at all.
What does Compute Cloud@Customer put in your data centre?
It runs the OCI control plane and its services on rack-scale systems at your site, with the same APIs and console. The first option describes an edge cache, which is a different shape entirely. The third describes the offline data transfer appliance, and the fourth describes a hybrid network link - both real OCI things, neither of them this one.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A European insurer must keep policy data inside the European Union under a sovereignty commitment, run a two-region active-passive design, and give a regulator an isolated copy that never leaves one named building.

Decide which of the three OCI answers to "where does this run" each requirement lands on, and say which requirement is the one that forces a second tenancy.

Hint

Take the requirements one at a time and ask, for each, whether it constrains the realm, the subscription list, or the hardware. Only one of the three constraints cannot be satisfied inside a single tenancy.

Show the worked solution
  1. Place the sovereignty commitment. It constrains the realm. The EU Sovereign regions sit in their own realm, so meeting the commitment through them means a tenancy in that realm.
  2. Place the two-region requirement. It constrains the subscription list, and it has to be checked against the realm chosen in step 1. The EU Sovereign list names two regions, so an active-passive pair fits, with no third region to fail over to afterwards.
  3. Place the isolated regulator copy. It constrains the hardware. A named building that data never leaves is what Dedicated Region or Compute Cloud@Customer is for, because a public region is a location you cannot point at.
  4. Find the forced second tenancy. The realm is the only one of the three that a tenancy cannot cross. If the insurer already has a commercial-realm tenancy, the sovereignty requirement is what forces a second one - the other two requirements are satisfiable inside whichever tenancy is chosen.

Sanity check. Exactly one of the three requirements should have produced a second tenancy. If your answer produced two or three, check whether you treated a subscription limit or a hardware choice as a realm boundary, which is the usual slip.

Your teacher. If realms and subscriptions still feel like the same control, ask an agent to name, for one tenancy, its realm, its home region and its subscribed region list, and then to say which of those three it could change today. Only one of them is changeable, and that is the distinction the page is teaching.

Primary source to go deeper

Read the service availability page beside the Regions and Availability Domains documentation. The first tells you which regions exist in which realm and which services reach them; the second tells you what a realm is. Read in that order the two pages answer one question rather than two.
