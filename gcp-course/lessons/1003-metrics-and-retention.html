A metric does not expire, it loses resolution

frontier 9 min Capabilities metrics-store, managed-prometheus and telemetry-agent. Sourced from Monitoring quotas and the latency and retention reference.

The one-minute version

  • Every service's metrics are ingested automatically, and virtual machines emit hypervisor-visible processor, disk and network metrics with no agent at all.
  • The Ops Agent is what adds the layers the hypervisor cannot see: process and host metrics, and logs from applications on the machine. Nothing collects those until it is installed.
  • Retention runs on three different schedules. Named service families and custom, agent, external and workload metrics keep points for 24 months - but only 6 weeks at their original frequency, then 10-minute points.
  • Every other metric type, including log-based metrics, keeps 6 weeks in total. Managed Prometheus and OTLP have their own schedule again: 1 week native, then 1-minute points for 5 weeks, then extended storage at 10 minutes.
  • The two ingest delays differ by two orders of magnitude. Google Cloud service metrics become visible up to 240 seconds after sampling; user-defined metrics typically in 3 to 7 seconds.

1. What arrives without asking

2. Three schedules, and which one a metric is on

Log-based metrics deserve a sentence of their own. They sit in the second row, so a metric derived from logs keeps six weeks in total - shorter than the metric it was probably created to replace, and shorter than the 400-day audit retention of the logs it was derived from.

3. Two very different delays

The verified research is blunt about the consequence: where a reaction has to be faster than these delays allow, the supported path is a Pub/Sub subscription on a log sink from lesson 1000, not polling a metric store.

4. Prometheus, kept but not run by you

The reframing Ask two questions of every metric a design depends on. Which retention row is it on - because that decides how long its shape survives, and log-based metrics are on the short one. Which ingest path is it on - because that decides the floor on how fast anything built on it can react. Neither question is about the dashboard, and both are usually answered after an incident rather than before.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An engineer opens a service metric from three months ago and finds the spike they remember has flattened. Why?
Named service families keep points to 24 months but at original frequency for only six weeks, after which they are downsampled to ten-minute points. The first and third both assert deletion. The fourth gives an alerting policy a write it does not perform.
A team builds a log-based metric to track an error rate over the past year. What is wrong?
Log-based metrics fall in the every-other-type row, which is six weeks in total. The first invents a restriction. The second confuses a delay with a retention. The third applies the Managed Prometheus pricing model to a different metric type.
A requirement says a system must react within one second of a condition appearing. What does the research recommend?
Sub-second reaction is below every metric path's floor, and the documented answer is a subscription on a log sink. The second is the fastest metric path and is still several times too slow. The third tunes evaluation on top of a delay it cannot remove. The fourth names a retention property, not a latency one.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A team is designing monitoring for a payments service on virtual machines. They want an alert when the failure rate rises, a quarterly capacity review that compares this month against the same month last year, and a per-process view when a machine misbehaves. Their draft uses a log-based metric for the failure rate and nothing else.

Assess the draft against the three requirements, correct what fails, and state the floor on how quickly the alert can fire.

Hint

Each requirement lands on a different row of the retention figure or on a source from the first one. The last part is arithmetic on two numbers from this page.

Show the worked solution
  1. The alert. A log-based metric works for alerting, and its six-week retention does not matter for something evaluated continuously. This part of the draft is sound.
  2. The year-on-year review. This fails. Log-based metrics keep six weeks in total, so last year's value does not exist and never will. The fix is a custom metric written through the Monitoring API or OpenTelemetry, which sits in the 24-month row - accepting that beyond six weeks it is ten-minute points, which is ample for a capacity comparison.
  3. The per-process view. This is missing entirely. The hypervisor sees processor, disk and network for the machine and cannot see processes, so the Ops Agent has to be installed. Nothing collects that data until it is.
  4. State the floor on the alert. A user-defined metric is typically visible in three to seven seconds, and the alerting policy's own condition duration is added on top. So the alert cannot fire sooner than a few seconds plus whatever duration the condition requires - and had the team used a Google Cloud service metric instead, the floor would have been up to 240 seconds before evaluation even began.

Sanity check. Exactly one of the three requirements should have survived the draft unchanged, and your correction should move one metric to a different row rather than change its query. If the year-on-year comparison stayed on a log-based metric, count six weeks forward from today and ask what it compares against.

Your teacher. For any dashboard a team relies on, ask an agent which retention row each of its metrics is on. The panels that will be blank in seven weeks are worth knowing about before the quarter in which somebody needs them.
