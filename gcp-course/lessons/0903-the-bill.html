A budget raises an alert and stops nothing

working 7 min Capability cost-management. Sourced from Cloud Billing export.

The one-minute version

  • A billing account sits beside the resource hierarchy rather than inside it, and projects link to it. Lesson 0101 established that shape; this page is what you do with it.
  • A budget is a threshold that raises alerts. It does not cap spending, and nothing stops when it is crossed.
  • The console reports answer how much. The BigQuery billing export is what answers why, because it carries the detail at a grain the reports do not.
  • Two export flavours exist. The standard export lands daily; the detailed one updates more often, and the exact cadence is a vendor figure to read rather than to memorise.
  • The breakdown you get is the one your labels allow, which is why lesson 0901's metadata standard is a cost decision.

A project crosses its budget on the twentieth of the month. What happens to the resources?
A budget is a monitoring object, so crossing it notifies and changes nothing about what is running. The other three all describe enforcement, which is exactly the assumption this page exists to correct, and each would be a reasonable design on a platform that offered it.

Which part of the cost surface can change a decision before money is spent?
Only the estimate acts before anything is built. The second sets a threshold in advance and still only reports afterwards. The third and fourth both read spending that has already happened, however quickly they do it.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A finance director asks for a hard monthly cap of a fixed amount on a development environment, so that an accident cannot produce a large invoice. The platform team's first instinct is to set a budget at that amount.

Explain why the budget does not meet the requirement, build something that comes closer using mechanisms from this module and earlier ones, and state honestly what your design still does not guarantee.

Hint

The budget is the wrong axis. Cost is produced by resources existing, so the controls that bind are the ones on what may be created and how much of it - and two earlier lessons in this module supplied both.

Show the worked solution
  1. Explain the mismatch. A budget is a monitoring object. It raises alerts at thresholds and does not cap anything, so an accident that starts spending at midnight produces exactly the invoice the director is asking to prevent, plus three notifications.
  2. Move to the axis that binds. Spend follows from resources existing, so the effective controls are on creation. Organisation policy constraints on the development folder can forbid the expensive shapes outright - large machine types, accelerators, whichever the estate does not need there.
  3. Add the ceiling that is a real ceiling. Allocation quotas from lesson 0901. A limit on how many instances or how much of a resource may exist in that project is a genuine cap, because the next create is refused rather than reported.
  4. Keep the budget anyway. It is still the fastest signal that something unexpected is happening, and pairing its alert with an automation subscribed to the notification is what turns the message into a response.
  5. State what is not guaranteed. None of this is a monetary cap. Quotas bound quantities and policies bound shapes, and the mapping from those to a currency amount is your arithmetic, not the platform's. If the director needs a number that cannot be exceeded, the honest answer is that the platform does not offer one and the design approximates it.

Sanity check. Your design should contain at least one control that refuses an action rather than reporting it, and your final paragraph should decline to promise a hard monetary cap. If it promised one, name the object that enforces it - there is not one.

Your teacher. When a bill moves and nobody knows why, ask an agent to query the billing export grouped by your own labels rather than by service. Grouping by service tells you which product grew; grouping by label tells you who to talk to.
