Two different things inherit down the tree, and neither one is a ceiling

working 8 min Capability org-guardrail-policy. Sourced from Google's organization policy hierarchy evaluation page.

The one-minute version

  • Two systems inherit down the hierarchy and they answer different questions. Organization policy governs what configurations may exist. IAM governs who may act. Neither substitutes for the other.
  • The widespread belief that a policy set high cannot be loosened below it is false by default: a descendant's own policy supersedes its parent's, and inheritFromParent = false makes a child inherit nothing at all.
  • What does merge is narrower than the belief: list constraints merge parent and child values only when the child opts into inheritance, and deny wins inside that merge. A constraint's default is never merged - a child policy replaces it outright.
  • So the real guardrail is not the policy. It is withholding the permission to edit the policy below the organization node.
  • Policy targets resources by Resource Manager tags, which are a different object from labels however similar they look.

1. Two axes, not one

The Organization Policy Service sets constraints that inherit down the tree. A constraint is boolean, allowing or disallowing a behaviour outright, list, naming allowed or denied values, or custom, written as an expression against a service-defined schema. Two constraints do most of the teaching: constraints/compute.vmExternalIpAccess, which decides whether a virtual machine may hold a public address at all, and constraints/gcp.resourceLocations, which is how data residency is expressed as a rule rather than as a habit.

On the other axis, IAM decides who may act. Allow bindings attached anywhere on a resource's ancestor chain - organization, folder, project, resource - are taken together, so reach grows with height, exactly as the previous page described. Module 08 works the full evaluation order, including the deny policies and the eligibility boundaries that sit in front of it.

2. The inheritance rule almost everyone states backwards

This is the correction that matters most in this module. The hierarchy evaluation documentation states that a resource with its own organization policy supersedes the policy set by its parents by default, and it carries the worked example above. A child can also opt out of inheritance entirely: with inheritFromParent = false it inherits nothing from its parent at all.

Something does merge, and knowing exactly what keeps the rule usable. List constraints merge the parent's and the child's values when the child sets inheritFromParent = true, and inside that merge a denied value wins over an allowed one. A constraint's default behaviour is never merged - any child policy replaces it outright. So the familiar sentence, a child cannot re-enable what an ancestor disabled, is true for a list constraint whose child opted into inheritance, and false as a general rule.

The reframing The blast-radius brake on this platform is IAM, not the policy. Withhold roles/orgpolicy.policyAdmin below the organization node and nobody lower can loosen anything, because they cannot edit the policy at all. The ceiling is the permission to change the guardrail - never the guardrail by itself.

That has a direct operational consequence. Setting a residency or public-address constraint at the organization node is necessary and it is not sufficient: descendants that hold the policy admin role can replace it. A real estate sets the constraint high and audits for local overrides, because both halves of the guardrail can be true independently.

4. Tags are not labels, and only one of them can be policed

Both are key-value metadata and the names invite the mistake. Resource Manager tags are governance-grade identifiers that IAM conditions and organization policy rules can reference, so a tag can decide whether a rule applies to a resource. Labels are metadata for cost breakdown, filtering and automation, and no policy engine reads them.

Engineers arriving from another platform usually bring one word, tags, for what Google Cloud splits into two objects. Attaching a policy to what you thought was a tag and was actually a label produces a rule that matches nothing, silently and permanently. When a conditional grant or a constraint mysteriously never fires, this is the first thing to check.
What this certification tests Guardrails are examined on the security side of both blueprints. The Associate Cloud Engineer guide puts Configuring access and security at about 20 per cent of the exam. The Professional Cloud Architect guide puts Designing for security and compliance at about 17.5 per cent, where residency questions are usually really organization policy questions.

Answer from memory before revealing.

A folder enforces a boolean constraint. A project beneath it sets the same constraint to not enforced. What happens?
Google documents exactly this case and the constraint is not enforced on that project. The first option is the belief this page exists to correct. The second describes the merge behaviour of a list constraint whose child opted into inheritance, applied to the wrong constraint kind. The fourth invents a rule nobody documents.
A platform team wants a residency constraint that project owners genuinely cannot loosen. What actually delivers that?
The ceiling is the permission to edit the policy, so the guardrail is an IAM decision. The first and third options both set the policy in more places and change nothing about who may replace it. The fourth misreads the merge rule: deny wins inside a list merge, and only when the child opted into inheritance.
A conditional role grant is written against a key-value pair on a resource and never fires. What should you check first?
IAM conditions read Resource Manager tags and never read labels, so a condition written against a label matches nothing. The other three name plausible-sounding mechanisms: height in the tree changes reach rather than matching, conditions are not evaluated by region by default, and ordering does not matter because policy is evaluated per request.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An auditor asks a platform team to prove that no virtual machine anywhere in the organization can be given a public address. The team replies that constraints/compute.vmExternalIpAccess is enforced at the organization node.

Write down why that reply is not yet a proof, and list the two things the team must add to make it one.

Hint

Ask who is able to change the policy, and then ask what evidence exists that nobody has.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the gap. A descendant's own policy supersedes its parent's by default, so any folder or project holding the policy admin role can set the same constraint to not enforced and the organization setting stops applying there.
  2. Close it on the identity axis. Withhold roles/orgpolicy.policyAdmin everywhere below the organization node. This is the actual ceiling, and it is an IAM change rather than a policy change.
  3. Close it on the evidence axis. Audit the current effective policy of every folder and project for local overrides. The constraint being set high says nothing about whether it is set differently lower down, and only an inspection of descendants answers that.
  4. State the proof. The claim that survives an auditor is: the constraint is enforced at the root, no principal below the root can edit organization policy, and no descendant policy currently overrides it. All three clauses are needed and the team supplied one.

Sanity check. Your answer should name one identity control and one inspection. If it named a second or stricter constraint instead, the correction on this page has not landed - adding policy does not defend policy, because the thing that defeats it is the permission to edit it.

Your teacher. If the supersede rule still feels wrong, ask an agent to find Google's own worked example of a folder enforcing a constraint and a project beneath it not enforcing it, and to quote the outcome sentence verbatim. Most published summaries of this behaviour, including confident ones, state it backwards.
