Persistent Disk sells performance by the gigabyte and Hyperdisk stops doing that

working 9 min Capability block-storage. Sourced from Google's Hyperdisk documentation.

The one-minute version

  • Three products sit under one capability. Persistent Disk and Hyperdisk are network-attached and durable. Local SSD is physically attached and is not.
  • On Persistent Disk, performance scales with size, so buying speed means buying capacity you may not need.
  • Hyperdisk is disaggregated: capacity and performance are provisioned independently, in Balanced, Balanced High Availability, Extreme, ML and Throughput types. New designs should prefer it.
  • A regional Persistent Disk replicates synchronously into a second zone, which is the block-level version of the placement decision module 01 introduced.
  • Local SSD is the fastest and has no durability at all, so it belongs to caches, scratch space and state that is replicated somewhere else.

1. Three products, one row

2. The coupling Hyperdisk removes

Hyperdisk comes in Balanced, Balanced High Availability, Extreme, ML and Throughput types, and adds storage pools and exapools where capacity is shared across many disks rather than committed to each one. The research behind this course records the guidance plainly: prefer Hyperdisk for new designs. Persistent Disk remains the older shape, in balanced, SSD, HDD and extreme flavours, and it is still what a great many running systems are on.

3. The zone question, at the block layer

Snapshots are the other half of the durability story and they answer a different question. Replication protects against losing a zone. A snapshot protects against losing the contents - a bad write, a deletion, a corrupted upgrade - which replication faithfully copies to the second zone at the speed of light.

The reframing Local SSD is the one that has to be understood as a guarantee rather than as a speed. It is physically attached NVMe with the fastest input and output of the three, and no durability. Google's own framing of what belongs on it is the useful list: swap, caches, and state that is replicated somewhere else. A database's only copy is not on that list, and no amount of care about shutdown procedure moves it there.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A workload needs high input and output rates and very little capacity. What does Persistent Disk force, and what does Hyperdisk change?
Performance scaling with size is the coupling, and disaggregation is what removes it. The second names an instance-level limit rather than the volume-level coupling. The third invents a billing difference. The fourth is wrong twice, since Persistent Disk has a regional form of its own.
A team puts a database's only data directory on Local SSD for speed. What is wrong with that?
Local SSD is ephemeral scratch storage, so an only copy on it is an only copy that is not durable. The first option is wrong about the performance ranking, which is exactly why the mistake is attractive. The second and fourth invent constraints and would each be an inconvenience rather than a data-loss risk.
A regional Persistent Disk protects against which failure, and which one does it not touch?
Synchronous replication into a second zone survives a zone loss and faithfully copies whatever was written, including a mistake - which is what snapshots are for. The first option reverses the two. The third mistakes synchronous replication for a lag that would happen to act as a backup. The fourth denies the feature entirely.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A transactional database runs on a Compute Engine instance. It needs sustained high input and output, must survive the loss of its zone, and must be recoverable from a bad schema migration applied last night.

Choose the block storage for it and say which of the three requirements each part of your answer satisfies. Then name the requirement that no disk choice can satisfy.

Hint

Three requirements, and they are answered by three different mechanisms. One of them is not a property of a volume at all.

Show the worked solution
  1. Satisfy the performance requirement. Sustained high input and output with a modest data size is the disaggregation case, so Hyperdisk, whose performance is provisioned independently of capacity. On Persistent Disk the same performance would mean buying capacity to reach it.
  2. Satisfy the zone requirement. The volume must have a synchronous copy in a second zone - the regional form. This is a placement property of the volume and it is chosen when the volume is created.
  3. Satisfy the recovery requirement. Nothing about replication helps here: a bad migration is a valid write and is replicated correctly. Recovery from last night needs a snapshot taken before the migration.
  4. Name what no disk choice covers. Point-in-time recovery is not a volume property. It comes from snapshots on a schedule, and the schedule has to be older than the mistake - so the requirement is really about how far back the snapshots go, which is a policy decision made long before the incident.

Sanity check. Two of the three requirements should be answered by properties of the volume and one by something outside it. If your answer solved the migration with replication, remember that synchronous replication is faithful - it copies the mistake exactly and immediately.

Your teacher. If the choice between the two network disks is unclear for a workload you have, ask an agent for the capacity a Persistent Disk would need to reach your required performance, then compare it with the capacity you actually need. The gap between those two numbers is the argument.
