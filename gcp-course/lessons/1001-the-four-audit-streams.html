Four audit streams, and the one you want history of expires first

frontier 9 min Capabilities control-plane-audit-log, data-plane-access-log, policy-denied-audit-log and access-transparency-logs. Sourced from the audit logs overview.

The one-minute version

  • Admin Activity and System Event logs are always on, cannot be disabled, and are free. They land in _Required for 400 days.
  • Data Access logs are off by default for all services except some BigQuery logs. They are enabled per service in the IAM audit configuration, and once enabled they are billed as normal ingestion.
  • Policy Denied logs are always generated and cannot be disabled, and they are billed and land in _Default - not in _Required. An exclusion filter can stop the storage but not the generation.
  • Access Transparency records actions by Google's own personnel on your content, and it is on when the organisation holds a Standard, Enhanced or Premium support level.
  • Audit entries are immutable, and they arrive in seconds. What they do not capture is a longer list than people expect.

2. The stream that records a refusal, and forgets it soonest

Two properties of this stream repay attention. It cannot be turned off, so it is on the bill whether or not the estate reads it; and it lands in _Default rather than _Required, because the required bucket's filter covers only the activity, system event and access transparency log identifiers.

3. The stream that is off, and what that means

What the audit streams do not capture is a longer list than people expect: data reads on most services, unless Data Access is enabled for that service; anything happening inside your virtual machines; application payloads; and DNS responses served from a cache. The entries that are written are immutable, and they arrive in seconds - the limits here are about coverage, not about trust or delay.
The reframing Ask three questions of any audit requirement. Is the evidence in a free stream - if so, 400 days already exist and there is nothing to build. Is it in Data Access - if so, it is not being recorded right now and enabling it fixes only the future. Is it a refusal - if so, it exists, it is already costing money, and it disappears in thirty days unless somebody routes it.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which statement about Policy Denied audit logs is correct?
Always generated, billed as normal log storage, and in _Default rather than _Required. The first is the assumption that makes estates lose them. The second confuses this stream with Data Access. The third is nearly right - generation cannot be disabled, but an exclusion filter does stop the storage.
An incident review asks who read a sensitive table last month. Data Access logging was never enabled. What is the answer?
The gap is retrospective and nothing recovers it. The first invents a backfill. The third confuses configuration-changing calls with data reads, which is the split that defines these two streams. The fourth records access by Google personnel, not by your own principals.
Which of these is genuinely outside every audit stream?
Audit logs record calls to Google Cloud APIs, so what happens inside your own machines is not covered - the Ops Agent from lesson 1003 is what collects that. The first is Admin Activity regardless of the tool used. The second is Policy Denied. The fourth is Access Transparency.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A regulator requires three things for a two-year period: a record of every configuration change, a record of every read of one particular dataset, and a record of every access attempt that was refused by a security control. The organisation has an organisation-level view of its logs and a modest logging budget.

Work out for each requirement whether the evidence exists today, design what is missing, and identify the requirement that cannot be satisfied retrospectively at all.

Hint

One requirement is nearly free but the period is longer than the free retention. One is already costing money and is about to expire. One does not exist yet in any form.

Show the worked solution
  1. Configuration changes. Admin Activity, always on and free, kept 400 days in _Required. That is more than a year and less than two, so the requirement is met for the recent past and needs a sink to a longer-retention destination for the rest. The stream itself costs nothing; the copy you keep does.
  2. Reads of the dataset. Data Access logging, which is off for almost every service. It must be enabled for that service in the IAM audit configuration, and once enabled it is billed as normal ingestion - so scope it to the one service rather than turning it on everywhere.
  3. Refused attempts. Policy Denied. These are already being generated and already being billed, and they land in _Default at 30 days. Route them with a sink into a log bucket in a project and raise that bucket's retention, remembering that above a project the default bucket cannot be extended.
  4. Identify what cannot be recovered. The reads. Admin Activity and Policy Denied both exist today, so the only question for them is where the copies go. Data Access records nothing about the period before it was enabled, so two years of read history simply cannot be produced.
  5. Control the cost. Scope the Data Access configuration to the single service, and filter the Policy Denied sink to the resources in scope rather than routing every refusal in the estate.

Sanity check. Two of the three requirements should have been about moving evidence that already exists, and exactly one should be unrecoverable. If your Policy Denied answer raised retention on an organisation-level bucket, re-read the previous lesson - that bucket is fixed at thirty days.

Your teacher. Ask an agent one question at design time rather than at incident time: for each service holding sensitive data, is Data Access logging enabled today. The answer is a list, it takes minutes, and it is the difference between an incident review that has evidence and one that has a theory.
