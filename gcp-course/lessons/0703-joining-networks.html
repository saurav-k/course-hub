Peering does not chain, which is why the estate needs a different shape

working 8 min Capabilities network-peering, shared-vpc, transit-hub and network-manager. Sourced from VPC Network Peering, Shared VPC and Network Connectivity Center.

The one-minute version

  • VPC Network Peering joins two networks privately across projects and organisations. It is non-transitive, and the peers may not have overlapping primary ranges.
  • Shared VPC is the other answer, and it avoids the problem instead of solving it: a host project owns the network and service projects attach workloads to it. The inventory calls it the landing-zone default.
  • Network Connectivity Center is the hub-and-spoke transit plane. Spokes can be VPCs, VPN tunnels, Interconnect attachments and router appliances, and it supersedes peering at scale.
  • The arithmetic is the argument: a full mesh of peerings grows as the square of the network count, and a hub grows linearly.
  • There is no central multi-network management plane. Shared VPC centralises ownership and hierarchical firewall policies push rules down, and neither is a management console over many networks.

1. Non-transitive, stated as the thing it stops you doing

The second constraint is addressing: peers may not have overlapping primary ranges, which reaches back to the ratchet in lesson 0700. A range chosen carelessly in one network can make a future peering impossible, and the range cannot be shrunk to make room.

2. The arithmetic that decides the shape

3. Two ways out, and they are not alternatives

Shared VPC is an organisation-level construct and it needs its own IAM roles, so adopting it is a governance decision as much as a networking one. Module 09 returns to it as part of the landing zone.

There is no central multi-network management plane here, and the verified inventory records what stands in its place: Shared VPC centralises ownership, and hierarchical firewall policies push rules down from the organisation and folder levels. Neither gives one console that manages many networks, so an estate with many VPCs manages them one at a time.
The reframing Count before you connect. Two networks is a peering and the arithmetic does not matter. Many teams, one address plan is Shared VPC, and it means there was never more than one network. Many networks that must stay separate is a hub, and the moment to adopt it is before the mesh exists, because unpicking a mesh is far harder than never building one.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

VPC A peers with B, and B peers with C. What can A reach?
Non-transitivity is the defining property and the reason a mesh is needed for full reachability. The first two describe transitive behaviour, and the second dresses it up with a real constraint that does not change the answer. The fourth invents a precedence rule.
Ten separate VPCs must all reach one another. What does a full mesh of peerings cost?
Ten times nine divided by two is forty-five - the pair count, because peering is non-transitive. The second confuses a mesh with a hub. The third would be right if a central VPC could relay, which non-transitivity forbids. The fourth invents a redundancy rule.
What does the inventory record in place of a central multi-network management plane?
Ownership and rules are centralised by two different mechanisms, and neither is a management console. The first names a transit plane, which connects rather than manages. The third over-reads a partial absence. The fourth names an address-reservation API from lesson 0700.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An organisation has six VPCs today, one per business unit, joined by a full mesh of peerings. Two acquisitions will add four more networks whose address ranges were chosen elsewhere and cannot be renumbered. A data centre link is planned as well.

Compute the mesh today and after the acquisitions, identify the risk the acquired ranges carry, and recommend a shape, saying what the data centre link becomes under it.

Hint

The formula is on this page. One of the constraints on peering has nothing to do with count, and it is the one an acquisition is most likely to violate.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count today. Six networks, so 6 × 5 ÷ 2 = 15 peerings to create, document and operate.
  2. Count after. Ten networks, so 10 × 9 ÷ 2 = 45. The estate grew by two thirds and the connections tripled, which is the square-law in one sentence.
  3. Identify the addressing risk. Peers may not have overlapping primary ranges. The acquired ranges were chosen by other people and cannot be renumbered, so any overlap with an existing network makes that peering impossible - and a primary range never shrinks to make room.
  4. Recommend the shape. Network Connectivity Center, with each VPC as a spoke. Ten spokes replace forty-five peerings, and the count then grows in step with the estate rather than ahead of it.
  5. Place the data centre link. It becomes another spoke on the same hub rather than a separate arrangement bolted to one VPC, which is the property that stops the hybrid link from being reachable by only one business unit.

Sanity check. Your two counts should be 15 and 45, and the recommendation should reduce forty-five objects to ten. If the addressing risk did not appear in your answer, note that it is the one problem a hub does not automatically remove - overlap still has to be resolved wherever two spokes must reach each other.

Your teacher. Before adding a peering, ask an agent how many exist already and apply the formula to the number you are heading towards, not the number you have. The decision to adopt a hub is nearly always taken one estate too late.
