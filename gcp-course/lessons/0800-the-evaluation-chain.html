Deny beats everything, and any allow anywhere on the chain wins

working 9 min Capabilities iam-principals, iam-roles and iam-policy-language. Sourced from IAM policy types and the IAM overview.

The one-minute version

  • A principal is a Google account, a group, a domain, a workforce identity or a service account. A role is a collection of permissions, and an allow policy binds the two.
  • Roles come in three kinds: basic (owner, editor, viewer) which are coarse, predefined which are curated, and custom which you assemble. Prefer predefined; write custom only when no predefined role fits least privilege.
  • Evaluation has four stages. Principal Access Boundary policies gate eligibility first and fail closed. Deny policies come next and end the matter. Allow policies are a union across the whole ancestor chain. Conditions narrow when a binding applies.
  • There is no explicit deny inside an allow policy. Subtracting a permission comes from a deny policy or from a boundary policy, never from the grant itself.
  • Google documents the stages as a teaching model; in reality IAM evaluates every policy type at once and compiles the result.

1. The funnel

The policy-types page carries its own caveat, and it is worth repeating: IAM evaluates all policy types simultaneously and compiles the results. The numbered order is how Google teaches it rather than a sequence you can observe.

2. Union of allows, and what that costs

This is the sentence that catches people: there is no explicit deny inside an allow policy. A narrower binding on a project cannot take away a broader binding on the organisation. Removing a permission somebody already has means a deny policy or a boundary policy, both of which are separate objects with separate administration.

4. Conditions, and the layers that veto independently

Conditions themselves are expressions on a binding, narrowing when it applies by resource tag, time or other attributes. Note the object: they read Resource Manager tags, which lesson 0102 distinguished from labels.

The reframing Four sentences, in order, and they answer almost every access question on this platform. Eligibility gates first and fails closed. Deny beats everything. Any allow on the chain wins, because allows are a union. Conditions narrow a binding rather than subtracting a permission. If a design needs to take something away, none of the last three will do it from inside a grant.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A principal has a broad allow binding at the organisation node. A narrower binding is added on one project. What is the effect on that project?
Allow policies are the union of every binding on the ancestor chain and contain no explicit deny, so nothing subtracts. The first imports a nearest-wins rule from a different model. The third and fourth invent conflict behaviour that does not exist.
What happens if IAM cannot evaluate the relevant Principal Access Boundary policies at all?
Fail-closed is the documented behaviour and it is different from the case where no relevant boundary policies exist, which continues. The first conflates those two cases. The second denies the gate. The third invents a retry.
Which role kind should be reached for first, and what does the last one cost?
Predefined roles are curated per service, and a custom role stops tracking the service as permissions are added. The second and fourth put basic roles first, which is the opposite of the guidance. The third inverts the order and states a half-truth as a reason.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An audit finds that a contractor group can delete storage buckets in a production project. The group has editor at the organisation node, granted years ago for a migration. The team's proposal is to add a viewer binding on the production project and to write a custom role for the contractors.

Explain why the proposal fails, give two mechanisms that would actually remove the permission, and say which of them can be applied without touching the original grant.

Hint

The failure is a property of allow policies rather than of the bindings chosen. Two of the four stages on this page can subtract, and only one of them is a separate object with separate administration.

Show the worked solution
  1. Explain the failure. Allow policies are a union across the ancestor chain and carry no explicit deny. Adding a viewer binding on the project adds permissions; it removes nothing. The organisation-level editor grant still applies, so the contractors keep the delete permission.
  2. Note what the custom role does not fix. A custom role changes what a new binding grants. It has no effect on a binding that already exists at a higher level, so it is the right tool aimed at the wrong object.
  3. Give the first real mechanism. Remove or narrow the organisation-level binding itself. This is the honest fix, and it is the one that touches the original grant - which is why teams avoid it when they do not know what else depends on it.
  4. Give the second. A deny policy naming the delete permission and the contractor principal set. Deny is evaluated before allows and ends evaluation, so it subtracts without the original grant being edited. A Principal Access Boundary policy is the other subtractive layer, bounding which resources the principals are eligible for at all.
  5. Answer the last part. The deny policy, and the boundary policy. Both are separate objects administered separately, which is exactly why they exist - the grant can stay while the permission goes.

Sanity check. Your answer should have rejected both halves of the proposal for the same underlying reason, and both of your mechanisms should come from the stages above allow in the funnel. If either fix was a binding, look again at what a binding can do.

Your teacher. When somebody says "we restricted it at the project level", ask an agent for every binding for that principal on the whole ancestor chain, not just on the project. The union is what decides, and the binding people remember is rarely the one that matters.
