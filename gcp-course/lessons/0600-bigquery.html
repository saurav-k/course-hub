BigQuery separates storage from compute inside the warehouse itself

working 8 min Capabilities data-warehouse and serverless-query-engine. Sourced from the BigQuery introduction.

The one-minute version

  • BigQuery is serverless, and its storage and compute are separate. That single property is what makes the rest of this module possible.
  • It is both the data warehouse and the serverless query engine: the second capability has no product of its own here, because BigQuery already is it.
  • It queries its own managed storage and external lake tables through the same SQL surface, and on the on-demand model it bills per byte scanned over either.
  • The alternative billing model is capacity, bought as slots under Editions. The two models make opposite kinds of bill from the same queries.
  • Two features change the shape of a design: BI Engine for interactive speed, and authorized views for giving access to a result without access to the source.

1. The separation, and what it lets one service be

The verified inventory records the query-engine row as delivered by BigQuery rather than as missing, and the distinction matters when reading any comparison: nothing is absent here, and there is simply no second product to buy. Streaming inserts and built-in machine learning sit on the same surface, so a table can be written to continuously and modelled without leaving SQL.

2. Two ways to pay for the same queries

Under the on-demand model the unit of cost is a byte a query reads, and that applies to external lake tables as well as to managed storage. Under capacity the unit is a slot held for a period. Both are configured under BigQuery Editions, and moving between them is a commercial decision rather than a migration.

3. Two features that change a design rather than a setting

BI Engine is the other feature worth naming at design time, because it changes what an interactive dashboard feels like rather than what a report costs. Both are documented on the BigQuery introduction, and both are reached for at the point where a warehouse acquires readers who are not engineers.

The reframing Do not read BigQuery as "the warehouse product". Read it as a query engine with a storage service attached that it does not have to use. Everything that follows in this module - lake tables, streaming pipelines that land in it, catalogues that govern it, clean rooms that share it - is a consequence of the engine being able to point somewhere else.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does the serverless query engine capability have no separate product on Google Cloud?
The row is delivered by BigQuery itself, so it is filled rather than empty. The first is the over-reading this course warns about and is plainly false. The second names a pipeline service, not a query engine. The fourth invents a merge that the inventory does not record.
A team's monthly scan volume grows steadily. What changes about their BigQuery bill?
On demand prices the bytes a query reads and capacity prices held slots, so only one of the two follows scan volume. The second denies the on-demand model entirely. The third reverses the pair. The fourth collapses two models that are deliberately different.
What does an authorized view let you do that granting the table does not?
The view holds the access, so the reader is never granted the underlying table. The first describes what BI Engine is for. The second describes external lake tables. The third describes streaming inserts. All three are real BigQuery capabilities answering different questions, which is what makes them good distractors.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A finance team runs a handful of enormous month-end queries and almost nothing in between. A product team runs a dashboard that refreshes continuously all month for a hundred people. Both use the same warehouse. The finance team also wants to publish one aggregated table to a partner who must not see the underlying transactions.

Reason about which billing model suits each pattern, say what the shared warehouse implies for that choice, and design the partner access.

Hint

Look at the two lines in the chart and ask which one each traffic pattern sits on. The third part is not a billing question at all.

Show the worked solution
  1. Characterise the finance pattern. Few queries, each scanning a great deal, concentrated in a short window. Cost under on demand is driven by those bytes; cost under capacity is paid all month whether or not the queries run. Spiky and infrequent leans on demand.
  2. Characterise the product pattern. Continuous refreshes all month. Under on demand every refresh is billed for what it reads, so a steady high volume accumulates; under capacity the same volume costs the held slots and no more. Steady and heavy leans capacity.
  3. Resolve the conflict. The two patterns want opposite models, and the model is a commercial configuration rather than a property of a query. The lever is therefore organisational - separate the two workloads so each can carry its own model - not a rewrite of anybody's SQL.
  4. Design the partner access. This is a permission question. Publish an authorized view over the aggregate, grant the partner the view and never the transaction table, and the view definition becomes the boundary. Nothing about the billing model changes what the partner can see.

Sanity check. Your two billing answers should differ, and the reason should be the shape of the traffic rather than the size of the data. If your partner answer involved copying the aggregate into a separate dataset, ask what keeps the copy current - the view has no such problem.

Your teacher. If you are unsure which billing model a warehouse should be on, ask an agent for the bytes scanned per day over the last few months and look at the shape rather than the total. A flat line and a spiky line of the same area point at different models, and that is the whole decision.
