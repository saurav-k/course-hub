Six pillars to judge a design, and the score nobody computes for you

frontier 8 min Capabilities well-architected-framework and resilience-assessment. Sourced from the Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework.

The one-minute version

  • The framework has six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance optimisation, cost optimisation and sustainability. They are the questions to ask of any design on this platform.
  • Fault injection exists - the service mesh from module 03 ships managed delay and abort faults on a percentage of requests, so "there is no first-party fault injection here" would be false.
  • Reliability guidance is not purely written down either. Active Assist from lesson 0904 publishes tool-scored reliability and change-risk recommendations.
  • What is genuinely absent is a standalone chaos platform and a policy-scored resilience rating against stated recovery objectives. Nothing computes a resilience score for you.
  • That absence is the sharpest example of the reading skill this whole course has been teaching: an absence is a shape, not a wall, and getting the shape right is the difference between a design decision and a decision to leave.

1. Six pillars, and where this course answered each

2. What exists to test resilience, precisely

The mesh feature was introduced in lesson 0301 and the recommendation family in lesson 0904, where its cost recommenders were the subject. Both do resilience work as a side effect of being what they are, which is why neither appears when somebody searches for a chaos product.

3. What the missing score would have given you

The reframing, and the last one Every absence in this course has had a shape, and reading it correctly has mattered more than knowing the products. No product, capability delivered elsewhere - the graph database, the query engine, the front door. No product, assemble it - the ledger, appliance insertion, conditional access. Genuinely nothing, and here is what people use instead - the storage gateway, the client VPN, the managed device broker. Ask which of those three you are looking at, every time. It is the most portable thing this course has to give.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Is it true that Google Cloud ships no first-party fault injection?
The research is explicit that this claim would be false: managed delay and abort faults on a percentage of requests are a mesh feature. The first two assert the absence the inventory took care to deny. The third invents a partial scope.
What is genuinely recorded as absent in this area?
The two absences are the orchestration platform and the scoring, both stated precisely. The first is denied by Active Assist's reliability recommendations. The third is denied by the mesh. The fourth is denied by the recovery orchestration in lesson 1400.
What is the most portable thing to take from how this course treated absences?
Three shapes, and asking which one you are looking at is the reading skill. The first is a conclusion rather than an observation. The second is false - some absences are genuine. The fourth would discard the finding entirely, which is the opposite of what a verified inventory is for.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A board asks for an annual assurance that the platform estate meets its stated recovery objectives, and for evidence that failure has been tested rather than assumed. An architect replies that Google Cloud offers neither resilience scoring nor fault injection, and proposes buying a third-party chaos platform and a third-party posture tool.

Correct the two claims, build what the board asked for from what exists, and state honestly the one part that remains manual work.

Hint

One of the architect's two claims is simply false and the other is true with a qualifier. The assurance itself is assembled from three earlier modules.

Show the worked solution
  1. Correct the fault-injection claim. It is false. The service mesh ships managed fault injection - delay and abort faults on a percentage of requests - so failure can be tested against real traffic today, without buying anything.
  2. Correct the scoring claim, partly. There is no policy-scored resilience rating, so that half is right. But reliability guidance is not purely written down: Active Assist publishes tool-scored reliability and change-risk recommendations, so the estate already produces a machine-generated view the architect did not count.
  3. Build the testing evidence. Fault injection in the mesh, run deliberately against defined scenarios, with the results recorded. Add the recovery-plan rehearsals from lesson 1400, which are the equivalent exercise for the parts that are not request-serving.
  4. Build the assurance. List every business process, and for each one list every place it writes and the replication mechanism of each, as lesson 1401 asked. The largest window in each path is that process's real recovery point. Compare each against the board's stated objective.
  5. State what remains manual. That comparison. Nothing on the platform scores a design against a recovery objective, so the table is written by a person and goes stale as the estate changes. The honest commitment to the board is a review cadence, not a dashboard.
  6. Say what the third-party purchases would buy. Orchestration around experiments the platform can already run, and a posture product beside one the estate already has. Both may still be worth buying - but the argument for them is not that the capability is missing.

Sanity check. One of the architect's claims should have been rejected outright and the other narrowed, and the final deliverable should be a document with a review date rather than a product. If the answer bought both tools, note what the mesh was already able to do this week.

Your teacher. When somebody says a cloud cannot do something, ask an agent for the exact wording of the finding it rests on. Three shapes of absence produce three different decisions, and the sentence that collapses them - "this cloud cannot" - is the one that costs a platform migration nobody needed.
