Module 02 · Scaling & Data · Lesson 05

Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

2020 Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown, Chess, Child, Gray, Radford, Wu, Amodei (OpenAI / Johns Hopkins). · arXiv:2001.08361

The one-minute version

  • Train a lot of Transformers of different sizes and measure their test loss. The loss does not wander randomly - it falls along a smooth, straight line on a log-log plot. That straight line is a power law.
  • Three things drive the loss down predictably: model size N (parameters), dataset size D (tokens), and training compute C. Each obeys its own power law across seven-plus orders of magnitude.
  • Bigger models are more sample-efficient: they reach a given loss after seeing fewer tokens. So given a fixed compute budget, the paper says train a very large model and stop before convergence, pouring most extra compute into model size, not data.
  • The handy compute rule is C ≈ 6ND. These laws let you predict a big model's loss from small experiments - the reason labs now plan runs on a spreadsheet before spending millions.
  • Caveat: two years later, Chinchilla (Lesson 08) revises the "make it huge, feed it little" recommendation - the data side was undercooked here.

1. Why this paper exists

Before 2020, picking a model size was mostly folklore. You had a compute budget, you guessed a width and depth, you trained, and you hoped. There was no map. If someone asked "what loss will a 10x bigger model reach?" the honest answer was a shrug.

This paper drew the map. The authors trained Transformers spanning from tiny to large, varied the data and the compute, and plotted loss against each. The surprise was how orderly the result is: over an enormous range, test loss is a simple, smooth function of scale. No cliffs, no magic sizes, just a predictable slope. Suddenly you could extrapolate - measure cheaply at small scale, then read off where a giant model will land.

2. What a power law looks like

A power law means one quantity equals another raised to a fixed exponent: y = a · xk. Its signature trick: take the logarithm of both sides and it becomes a straight line. So if you plot loss versus model size on log-log axes and the points fall on a line, you have found a power law, and the slope is the exponent.

The paper reports three such laws, one for each resource, when the other two are not the bottleneck:

L(N) ≈ (Nc / N)αN   L(D) ≈ (Dc / D)αD   L(C) ≈ (Cc / C)αC L is the test loss (cross-entropy per token - lower is better). N is the number of (non-embedding) parameters, D the number of training tokens, C the compute. Nc, Dc, Cc are fitted constants that set where the line sits. The exponents α are small positive numbers (roughly αN ≈ 0.076, αD ≈ 0.095, αC ≈ 0.050): loss shrinks slowly but relentlessly as scale grows. Small exponent means you need multiplicative increases in scale to keep gaining.
Read the exponent A tiny exponent like 0.076 is not disappointing - it is the whole story. It says every time you multiply the model size by 10, the loss drops by a fixed fraction. Gains never stop, but they get expensive: progress is linear in the log of scale, which is why the field keeps needing 10x more of everything.

3. The three knobs, and the one that runs out

Loss depends on N, D, and C together. Push only one and eventually another becomes the bottleneck. Grow the model but freeze the data and you start to overfit - the model memorises the small dataset instead of learning general patterns. The paper captures this with a joint law, but the intuition is a simple flow.

4. Bigger models are more sample-efficient

Here is the paper's most counter-intuitive finding. You might expect a huge model to be data-hungry and wasteful. The opposite is true: a larger model reaches any target loss after seeing fewer tokens than a smaller model would. Its extra capacity lets it extract more from each example. So per unit of data, big is efficient.

Combine that with a fixed compute budget and you get the paper's famous recommendation. If compute is capped, do not spend it training a modest model to convergence. Instead train a very large model and stop early, before the loss flattens. Most of any extra compute should go into model size, a smaller share into data and steps. Roughly, the paper found the optimal model size grows fast with compute while the optimal data grows slowly.

Nopt ∝ C≈0.73   Dopt ∝ C≈0.27 Nopt is the best model size and Dopt the best number of tokens for a given compute C. The exponents (about 0.73 and 0.27) say: when you get 10x more compute, grow the model a lot (about 5x) and the data only a little (about 2x). This "mostly grow the model" advice is exactly what Chinchilla later corrects.

5. The compute rule: C ≈ 6ND

To reason about budgets you need to convert parameters and tokens into raw compute. The paper uses a clean approximation: training a dense Transformer costs about six floating-point operations per parameter per token.

C ≈ 6 · N · D C is total training compute in FLOPs (floating-point operations). N is parameters, D is tokens. The 6 breaks down as ~2 FLOPs for the forward-pass multiply-add per parameter per token, plus ~4 for the backward pass (gradients cost roughly twice the forward pass). Multiply parameters by tokens by 6 and you have the cost of the whole training run.
Why this matters C ≈ 6ND is the bridge between "how big" and "how expensive". It turns the abstract laws into a planning tool: fix your compute budget C, and the trade-off between a bigger model (N) and more data (D) becomes a single equation you can solve. Lesson 08 (Chinchilla) does exactly that and reaches a different answer than this paper.

6. Implement it: fit a power law and extrapolate

The whole paper is, at heart, "fit a straight line in log space, then extend it". Let us do that. We generate synthetic loss-versus-size data that follows a power law with noise, fit the exponent with numpy, and extrapolate to a model 100x larger than anything we "trained". Read it against the log-log diagram in Section 2.

scaling_fit.py · runnable with numpy (matplotlib optional)
import numpy as np

# ---- 1. synthesise "measured" loss for a range of model sizes ----
# ground-truth power law: L(N) = (Nc / N) ** alpha  (plus a noise floor)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Nc, alpha, floor = 8.8e13, 0.076, 1.7      # constants in the spirit of the paper
N = np.logspace(6, 9, 12)                   # 1e6 .. 1e9 params: our "cheap" runs
L_true = (Nc / N) ** alpha + floor
L_meas = L_true * (1 + 0.02 * rng.standard_normal(N.size))   # add 2% noise

# ---- 2. fit the power law as a STRAIGHT LINE in log-log space ----
# subtract the irreducible floor, then log both sides:
#   log(L - floor) = alpha * log(Nc) - alpha * log(N)
x = np.log(N)
y = np.log(L_meas - floor)
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)      # least-squares straight line
alpha_hat = -slope                          # exponent is the negative slope
Nc_hat = np.exp(intercept / alpha_hat)
print(f"fitted alpha = {alpha_hat:.4f}  (true {alpha})")

def predict(n):                             # the extrapolation function
    return (Nc_hat / n) ** alpha_hat + floor

# ---- 3. EXTRAPOLATE to a model 100x bigger than any we trained ----
N_big = N.max() * 100                        # far beyond the fitted range
print(f"predicted loss at N={N_big:.1e}: {predict(N_big):.4f}")
print(f"actual   loss at N={N_big:.1e}: {(Nc/N_big)**alpha + floor:.4f}")

# ---- 4. (optional) draw the log-log line the paper is built on ----
try:
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
    grid = np.logspace(6, 11, 100)
    plt.loglog(N, L_meas - floor, 'o', label='measured (small runs)')
    plt.loglog(grid, predict(grid) - floor, '-', label='fitted + extrapolated')
    plt.xlabel('parameters N'); plt.ylabel('loss above floor')
    plt.legend(); plt.savefig('scaling.png', dpi=120)
except ImportError:
    pass                                     # numpy-only run still prints the fit

The point is not the exact numbers - it is the workflow. A handful of small, affordable runs pin down the slope and intercept. From those two numbers you predict a model you never trained. That single capability, forecasting loss before spending the money, is why every frontier lab now runs a "scaling suite" before a flagship training run.

7. How the three laws line up

LawHolds the loss down viaApprox exponentBottleneck when
L(N)More parametersαN ≈ 0.076Data D is plentiful
L(D)More training tokensαD ≈ 0.095Model N is large enough
L(C)More total computeαC ≈ 0.050Spent optimally on N and D

All three exponents are small and positive: loss falls, slowly and forever, as any resource grows - provided the others do not run out first. The art is keeping them in balance, which is the whole subject of Lesson 08.

The catch Chinchilla found This paper concluded "make the model very large, feed it comparatively little data, stop early". In 2022, DeepMind's Chinchilla re-ran the analysis more carefully (fixing a learning-rate-schedule subtlety) and found N and D should grow together, roughly 20 tokens per parameter. GPT-3, built on this lesson's advice, was too big and under-trained. Keep this asterisk in mind - you will resolve it in Lesson 08.

What shape does test loss trace when plotted against scale on log-log axes?
A power law becomes a straight line in log-log space; its slope is the exponent, and that is what makes extrapolation possible.
According to this paper, how should a fixed compute budget be spent?
Kaplan et al. recommend big models trained short of convergence, allocating most extra compute to parameters. Chinchilla later revises this.
What does the rule C ≈ 6ND let you compute?
Each token costs about 6N FLOPs (2 forward, 4 backward), so the whole run is about 6ND. It converts "how big" into "how expensive".
Why are larger models described as more sample-efficient?
Extra capacity extracts more from each example, so a big model hits a given loss on fewer tokens than a small one would.

How this connects

  • Previously, GPT-2 hinted that scale alone unlocks new abilities. This paper turns that hint into a quantitative law.
  • Next, GPT-3 is this paper's advice taken to the extreme: a 175B-parameter model built because the laws said bigger keeps winning.
  • The Pile tackles the resource this paper treats as a simple number D, arguing the quality and diversity of tokens matters, not just the count.
  • Chinchilla directly revises the headline recommendation here: scale N and D together, roughly 20 tokens per parameter, not "mostly grow the model".
Your teacher. The single idea to internalise is "loss is a straight line in log-log space, so you can predict it". If the exponents feel abstract, ask the agent to plug real numbers into C ≈ 6ND for GPT-3 (175B params, 300B tokens) and check the FLOP count. Then ask it to contrast this paper's allocation advice with Chinchilla's before you reach Lesson 08.
