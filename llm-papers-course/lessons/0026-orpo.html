ORPO

2024 Hong, Lee, Thorne (KAIST AI). "ORPO: Monolithic Preference Optimization without Reference Model." · arXiv:2403.07691

The one-minute version

  • DPO (Lesson 25) removed the reward model and RL, but still needs two stages - SFT first, then preference tuning - and a frozen reference model in memory.
  • ORPO folds everything into one stage with no reference model at all: it does SFT and preference alignment at the same time.
  • The loss adds a small odds-ratio penalty to the ordinary language-modeling loss. The penalty raises the odds of the preferred answer over the dispreferred one.
  • Odds, not probability: odds(y) = P(y) / (1 − P(y)). Using the odds ratio gives a milder, more stable push than a raw probability ratio, which would over-suppress the rejected answer and risk degeneration.
  • Loss: LSFT + λ · LOR. One model, one pass, no reference, no reward model - the simplest alignment recipe yet.

1. Why this paper exists

Follow the trend of this module. InstructGPT needed a reward model plus a full RL loop. DPO deleted both, but still ran in two phases - first supervised fine-tuning to teach the format, then a separate preference phase - and it kept a frozen reference model around to compute log-ratios. That reference doubles the model memory during training and adds a stage to the pipeline.

ORPO asks whether the SFT stage and the preference stage can be the same stage, with no reference at all. Its answer starts from an observation about plain fine-tuning: cross-entropy training pushes up the probability of the target answer, but it does nothing to push down plausible wrong answers. A tiny extra term can add exactly that missing contrast, and it turns out you can bolt it onto SFT for free.

2. The gap in plain fine-tuning

Standard supervised fine-tuning maximises the likelihood of the good answer. That raises its probability, but the gradient is blind to the bad answers - it never explicitly lowers the odds of a dispreferred response that the model also finds plausible. ORPO's claim is that a small penalty which contrasts chosen against rejected, added right into SFT, closes that gap without a second stage.

3. Odds, and the odds ratio

First, the vocabulary. The odds of an answer is its probability divided by the probability of not producing it. If the model would generate y 75% of the time, its odds are 0.75 / 0.25 = 3 - three to one in favour. The odds ratio between chosen and rejected is just how many times higher the chosen answer's odds are.

oddsθ(y | x) = Pθ(y | x) / ( 1 − Pθ(y | x) ) Pθ(y | x): the model's probability of producing answer y for prompt x, computed as a length-normalized (per-token geometric-mean) probability so long and short answers compare fairly. 1 − Pθ: the probability of not producing it. odds: their ratio - how strongly the model favours generating y versus not. Odds of 3 means three-to-one in favour.

Why odds instead of the raw probability ratio DPO-style methods use? Because the probability ratio produces a very sharp margin between chosen and rejected. When alignment is folded into SFT, that sharp push over-suppresses the rejected answer's token logits and can cause degeneration - the model collapsing into repetitive or broken text. The odds ratio applies a gentler, more stable pressure, which is what makes single-stage training work.

Odds ratio vs probability ratio A probability ratio can shoot to extreme values and crush the rejected answer's logits, destabilising the shared SFT training. The odds ratio grows more slowly, so it discriminates chosen from rejected without the excessive margin that leads to degeneration. Milder penalty, stabler training.

4. The ORPO loss, exactly

The full objective is the ordinary fine-tuning loss plus a weighted odds-ratio penalty, averaged over preference triples.

LORPO = E(x, yw, yl) [ LSFT + λ · LOR ] LSFT: the standard next-token negative-log-likelihood (cross-entropy) loss on the chosen answer yw - the usual "learn to produce the good answer." λ: a small weight (the paper uses roughly 0.1 to 0.25) balancing the two terms. LOR: the odds-ratio penalty defined below. E(x, yw, yl): average over prompts with a chosen answer yw and a rejected one yl.

The SFT term is the cross-entropy you already know. The new piece is the odds-ratio penalty:

LOR = − log σ( log[ oddsθ(yw | x) / oddsθ(yl | x) ] ) oddsθ(yw) / oddsθ(yl): how many times higher the chosen answer's odds are than the rejected answer's - we want this large. log[·]: take its logarithm so the ratio lives on an additive scale. σ: the sigmoid, turning the log-odds-ratio into a number between 0 and 1. − log σ(·): the loss is small when the chosen odds dominate the rejected odds, and large when they do not - so minimizing it raises the odds of the preferred answer over the dispreferred one. Note: no πref appears anywhere - the penalty is computed from the single trained model.
Compare with DPO DPO's loss is −log σ(β(log πθ(yw)/πref(yw) − log πθ(yl)/πref(yl))) - a log-sigmoid of a ratio to a reference model. ORPO's is −log σ(log odds(yw)/odds(yl)) - a log-sigmoid of a ratio of odds within the same model. Same log-sigmoid shape, but ORPO needs no reference and rides alongside SFT.

5. Implement it: the combined ORPO loss

Both terms come from the same forward pass over the chosen and rejected answers. You need the per-token log-probs of each answer; the rest is arithmetic. Read it against the two formulas above.

orpo_loss.py · SFT loss plus odds-ratio penalty, no reference model
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def orpo_loss(logp_chosen, logp_rejected, lam=0.1):
    """
    logp_chosen  : (B,) length-normalized log P_theta(y_w | x)  (mean per-token log-prob)
    logp_rejected: (B,) length-normalized log P_theta(y_l | x)
    lam          : weight on the odds-ratio term (paper: ~0.1 to 0.25)

    Combined loss = L_SFT + lam * L_OR, all from ONE model. No reference.
    """
    # --- L_SFT: standard causal-LM loss = negative log-likelihood of the chosen answer
    # (here logp_chosen is already the mean per-token log-prob of y_w)
    L_sft = -logp_chosen.mean()

    # --- L_OR: odds-ratio penalty
    # odds(y) = P(y) / (1 - P(y)); log-odds = log P(y) - log(1 - P(y))
    # with P = exp(logp) (length-normalized), use log1p for a stable (1 - P) term.
    logodds_chosen   = logp_chosen   - torch.log1p(-torch.exp(logp_chosen))
    logodds_rejected = logp_rejected - torch.log1p(-torch.exp(logp_rejected))

    # log ratio of odds, then -log sigmoid: reward chosen odds > rejected odds
    log_odds_ratio = logodds_chosen - logodds_rejected
    L_or = -F.logsigmoid(log_odds_ratio).mean()

    return L_sft + lam * L_or, L_sft, L_or

# --- smoke test (length-normalized log-probs, so each in (-inf, 0)) ---
B = 4
logp_w = torch.tensor([-0.20, -0.50, -0.15, -0.30])   # chosen: fairly likely
logp_l = torch.tensor([-0.80, -0.60, -1.20, -0.90])   # rejected: less likely
total, l_sft, l_or = orpo_loss(logp_w, logp_l, lam=0.1)
print(float(total), float(l_sft), float(l_or))
# L_SFT teaches the chosen answer; L_OR widens the odds gap to the rejected one

The whole method is those two terms. L_sft is exactly the loss you would use to fine-tune on the good answers alone; L_or is the single extra line that turns fine-tuning into preference alignment. No reward model, no reference model, no RL, no second stage - and because both answers share one forward pass, it costs little more than plain SFT.

6. Why it mattered

ORPO showed that alignment need not be a bolt-on phase at all. By adding one odds-ratio term to the standard fine-tuning loss, it aligns a model in a single reference-free pass, cutting both memory and pipeline complexity while matching or beating multi-stage methods on preference benchmarks. It is the natural conclusion of a module that started with a three-part RL pipeline and ended with a one-line addition to cross-entropy.

What does ORPO remove that DPO still requires?
DPO keeps a frozen reference and runs preference tuning after SFT. ORPO needs no reference and folds preference alignment into a single SFT pass.
How is the odds of an answer defined in ORPO?
odds(y) = P(y) / (1 - P(y)) - how strongly the model favours producing the answer versus not, using length-normalized probability.
Why use the odds ratio instead of a raw probability ratio?
A probability ratio gives too sharp a margin and over-suppresses the rejected logits, risking degeneration. The odds ratio is milder and more stable.
What are the two terms in the ORPO loss?
L_ORPO = L_SFT + lambda * L_OR: the ordinary next-token loss on the chosen answer, plus lambda times the odds-ratio penalty.

How this connects

  • Previously, DPO removed the reward model and RL but kept a reference model and a separate preference stage; ORPO drops both.
  • The odds-ratio penalty plays the same contrastive role as DPO's log-ratio and InstructGPT's reward gap - push chosen above rejected.
  • ORPO trains on the same preference pairs that Constitutional AI can generate with AI feedback.
  • Next, GRPO takes the other branch: keep reinforcement learning for reasoning tasks, but strip out the value network to cut cost.
