Direct Preference Optimization

2023 Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning, Finn (Stanford University). "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model." · arXiv:2305.18290

The one-minute version

  • RLHF (Lesson 23) is powerful but fiddly: train a reward model, then run PPO with sampling, a value network, and a KL penalty. Lots of moving parts, easy to destabilise.
  • DPO proves the RLHF objective has a closed-form optimal policy. Rearranging it shows the reward is just a scaled log-ratio of the trained policy to a reference policy.
  • Substitute that into the preference model and the intractable normaliser cancels. What remains is a plain classification loss on preference pairs.
  • So you can optimise preferences directly: no reward model, no RL, no sampling during training. Just one loss over (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples.
  • The loss: −log σ( β( log πθ(yw)/πref(yw) − log πθ(yl)/πref(yl) ) ). Stable, cheap, and now the default alignment recipe.

1. Why this paper exists

InstructGPT works, but look at everything it needs: a reward model trained on comparisons, then a PPO loop that samples fresh answers, scores them, tracks a separate value network, and clips updates while a KL penalty holds the policy near the SFT model. Each piece has hyperparameters, and RL is famously touchy. Practitioners spent enormous effort just keeping PPO stable.

DPO's insight is that all of that RL scaffolding is solving a problem with a known analytic answer. The RLHF objective - maximise reward while staying close to a reference - has an exact optimal solution you can write down. Once you do, the reward model and the RL loop dissolve into a single supervised loss. Same goal, a fraction of the machinery.

2. The key idea: your policy is secretly a reward model

Start from the RLHF objective. It says: pick the policy that earns the most reward, but do not stray too far from the reference (SFT) policy - the same reward-versus-KL tug-of-war from Lesson 23.

maxπ   Ex~D, y~π(y|x) [ r(x, y) ] − β · DKL[ π(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x) ] π: the policy we are optimizing. r(x, y): the reward for answer y to prompt x. β: how hard the KL leash pulls. DKL[π ‖ πref]: how far the policy has drifted from the reference model. This is exactly InstructGPT's objective - maximize reward, stay near SFT.

This particular objective has a known closed-form solution. The optimal policy re-weights the reference model by the exponentiated reward:

πr(y|x) = ( 1 / Z(x) ) · πref(y|x) · exp( (1/β) r(x, y) ) πr: the best possible policy for this reward. πref(y|x) · exp((1/β) r): take the reference model and up-weight answers with high reward, more sharply when β is small. Z(x): the partition function - a normaliser that sums that product over all possible answers so the probabilities add to 1. It is astronomically expensive to compute, and it is the whole reason people fell back on RL instead of using this formula directly.

Now the pivot. Take logs of that equation and solve for the reward instead of the policy:

r(x, y) = β · log( πr(y|x) / πref(y|x) ) + β · log Z(x) r(x, y): the reward, now written in terms of policies. β log(πr/πref): the reward of any answer is just how much more probable the optimal policy makes it versus the reference, scaled by β. β log Z(x): a term that depends only on the prompt x, not on the answer. This is the "secret reward model": the policy itself encodes the reward through its log-ratio to the reference.

3. Why the impossible term cancels

Human preferences are usually modelled with Bradley-Terry: the probability that answer y1 beats y2 is the sigmoid of their reward difference. That word - difference - is the whole trick. Substitute the reward expression above, and the β log Z(x) term appears in both rewards. Since both answers share the same prompt x, the term is identical and subtracts to zero. The expensive normaliser we could never compute simply vanishes.

p(yw > yl | x) = σ( r(x, yw) − r(x, yl) ) = σ( β log(π(yw|x)/πref(yw|x)) − β log(π(yl|x)/πref(yl|x)) ) σ(r(x,yw) − r(x,yl)): the Bradley-Terry model - preference probability depends only on the reward difference. after substitution: each reward becomes a β-scaled log-ratio, and the log Z(x) terms (equal for yw and yl) cancel. What is left is written entirely in terms of the policy π and reference πref - no reward model anywhere.

4. The DPO loss, exactly

Fit that preference probability to the data by maximum likelihood and you get the DPO loss (the paper's Equation 7). It is the exact loss named in this lesson's summary.

LDPO(πθ; πref) = − E(x, yw, yl) ~ D [ log σ( β log( πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x) ) − β log( πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x) ) ) ] πθ: the policy being trained. πref: the frozen reference (usually the SFT model). yw: the chosen (preferred) answer; yl: the rejected one. log(πθ(yw)/πref(yw)): the implicit reward of the chosen answer - how much more the trained policy likes it than the reference did. β: scales those implicit rewards and, as before, controls how far the policy may drift from πref. log σ(·): push the chosen answer's implicit reward above the rejected one's. E...~D: average over all preference triples.
Read it in one line Make the chosen answer more likely than the reference made it, and the rejected answer less likely, with β setting how aggressively. That is the entire method. It is the InstructGPT reward loss from Lesson 23 with the reward replaced by a log-ratio of policies - so the "reward model" and the policy became one object.

The gradient tells you what it does

The gradient (Equation 8) is illuminating: it raises the probability of the chosen answer and lowers the rejected one, weighted by how wrong the implicit reward currently is. When the model already orders a pair correctly, the update is small; when it confidently gets the pair backwards, the update is large.

∇θ LDPO = − β · E [ σ( r̂(x,yl) − r̂(x,yw) ) · ( ∇θ log π(yw|x) − ∇θ log π(yl|x) ) ] r̂(x, y) = β log(πθ(y|x)/πref(y|x)): the implicit reward the policy assigns. σ(r̂(yl) − r̂(yw)): the weight - large exactly when the model wrongly rates the rejected answer above the chosen one. ∇ log π(yw) − ∇ log π(yl): push probability toward the chosen answer, away from the rejected. This weighting is what stops the model degenerating; without it, naive fine-tuning misbehaves.

5. Implement it: DPO loss in PyTorch

The formula is a few lines. You need four numbers per example - the log-probabilities the trained policy and the frozen reference each assign to the chosen and rejected answers. From those, DPO is a single log-sigmoid.

dpo_loss.py · the full DPO objective
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(
    policy_logp_chosen,   # (B,) log pi_theta(y_w | x)   - trained policy, chosen
    policy_logp_rejected, # (B,) log pi_theta(y_l | x)   - trained policy, rejected
    ref_logp_chosen,      # (B,) log pi_ref(y_w | x)     - frozen reference, chosen
    ref_logp_rejected,    # (B,) log pi_ref(y_l | x)     - frozen reference, rejected
    beta=0.1,
):
    """
    Direct Preference Optimization loss (Rafailov et al., 2023, Eq. 7).
    Each log-prob is the SUM of per-token log-probs of that whole answer.
    """
    # implicit reward = beta * log( pi_theta(y) / pi_ref(y) )
    #                 = beta * (policy_logp - ref_logp)   for each answer
    chosen_reward   = beta * (policy_logp_chosen   - ref_logp_chosen)
    rejected_reward = beta * (policy_logp_rejected - ref_logp_rejected)

    # we want the chosen answer's implicit reward ABOVE the rejected one's.
    # loss = -log sigma(chosen_reward - rejected_reward)
    logits = chosen_reward - rejected_reward
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()

    # handy diagnostic: fraction of pairs already ordered correctly
    acc = (logits > 0).float().mean()
    return loss, acc

# --- smoke test with made-up log-probs ---
B = 4
pol_c = torch.tensor([-5.0, -8.0, -3.0, -6.0])   # policy: chosen
pol_r = torch.tensor([-6.0, -7.0, -9.0, -6.5])   # policy: rejected
ref_c = torch.tensor([-5.5, -8.5, -3.5, -6.0])   # reference: chosen
ref_r = torch.tensor([-6.0, -7.0, -8.0, -6.0])   # reference: rejected
loss, acc = dpo_loss(pol_c, pol_r, ref_c, ref_r)
print(float(loss), float(acc))   # loss drops as chosen outscores rejected

Every term maps to the math. policy_logp - ref_logp is the log-ratio; times beta it is the implicit reward; the difference between chosen and rejected goes through logsigmoid. No answers are sampled during training - you compute log-probs on the fixed pairs you already have, which is why DPO is so much cheaper and steadier than PPO.

6. Why it mattered

PropertyRLHF / PPO (Lesson 23)DPO
Separate reward modelRequiredNone - policy is the reward
Reinforcement learning loopRequired (PPO)None - plain classification
Sampling during trainingYes, every stepNo, log-probs on fixed pairs
Extra networks in memoryPolicy, reward, valuePolicy plus frozen reference
Stability & tuning burdenHighLow, few hyperparameters

DPO matched or beat PPO-based RLHF on summarisation and dialogue while being dramatically simpler to run. It turned alignment from a specialist RL task into something any team with preference data and a fine-tuning script can do. It is now the default post-training method across open models, and the springboard for the next two lessons, which push even further - ORPO removes the reference model, GRPO keeps RL but drops the value network.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does DPO remove from the RLHF pipeline?
DPO keeps the preference data and a reference model, but drops the trained reward model and the whole PPO loop, replacing them with one classification loss.
Why does the intractable partition function Z(x) cancel?
Z(x) is the same for both answers to a prompt, so in the reward difference inside Bradley-Terry it subtracts to exactly zero.
What is the "implicit reward" of an answer in DPO?
The reward is beta times log(pi_theta(y)/pi_ref(y)). The policy encodes the reward through its log-ratio to the reference - the paper's title point.
How does the DPO gradient weight each training pair?
The sigmoid weight is largest when the implicit reward orders the pair backwards, so confidently wrong pairs drive the biggest updates.

How this connects

  • Previously, InstructGPT (RLHF) is the pipeline DPO compresses - its reward loss is the same Bradley-Terry form, now applied to the policy directly.
  • Constitutional AI (Lesson 24) shows preference data can be AI-labeled; DPO happily trains on those pairs too.
  • Next, ORPO goes further and removes the reference model, folding preference learning into SFT in one stage.
  • GRPO keeps reinforcement learning but drops the value network, a different way to cut RLHF's cost.
Your teacher. The one thing to nail is the cancellation: why the scary Z(x) disappears. Ask the agent to write out r(x, yw) − r(x, yl) term by term and point to where the two log Z(x) terms meet and vanish. Then ask it to set β very large versus very small in the code and describe how the leash to the reference changes. Do not move on until "the policy is its own reward model" clicks.
