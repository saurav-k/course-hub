BERT: Deep Bidirectional Transformers

2018 Devlin, Chang, Lee, Toutanova (Google AI Language). · arXiv:1810.04805

The one-minute version

  • The Transformer from Lesson 01 had two halves. BERT keeps only the encoder and asks it to understand text rather than generate it.
  • Its superpower is being deeply bidirectional: every token sees both its left and right context at every layer, unlike a left-to-right language model.
  • It learns this by a clever trick called the Masked Language Model: hide about 15% of the tokens and train the model to fill them back in from both sides.
  • A second task, Next Sentence Prediction, teaches it whether two sentences follow each other. Pretrain once on huge text, then fine-tune with a tiny task head.
  • Result: one recipe set new state of the art on eleven understanding benchmarks (GLUE, SQuAD) and made "pretrain then fine-tune" the default for NLP.

1. Why this paper exists

By 2018 people knew pretraining a language model on lots of text and then fine-tuning it was powerful. But the leading recipe, a left-to-right language model, had a built-in handicap: to predict a word it may only look at the words before it. That is fine for generation, where you write left to right anyway. It is a waste for understanding, where the words after a token are just as informative. In "the animal did not cross the street because it was too tired," the word after "it" tells you what "it" refers to.

Earlier attempts to be bidirectional cheated by training two separate models, one reading forward and one backward, then gluing their features together. That is shallow: neither direction ever conditions on the other inside the network. BERT's contribution is a training objective that lets a single deep stack condition on both sides at once, honestly.

2. Encoder-only: what BERT keeps and drops

Recall the original Transformer was an encoder-decoder. BERT is just the encoder stack: a pile of the very same blocks you built in Lesson 01 (multi-head self-attention, feed-forward, residuals, LayerNorm), with one crucial change - no causal mask. Every token attends to every other token, past and future, freely.

BERT came in two sizes. BERT-base has 12 layers, hidden size 768, 12 attention heads, about 110M parameters - deliberately matched to GPT-1 for a fair comparison. BERT-large has 24 layers, hidden size 1024, 16 heads, about 340M parameters.

3. The input: WordPiece, [CLS], and [SEP]

Before any attention happens, text is turned into tokens by WordPiece, a subword tokenizer with a vocabulary of about 30,000 pieces. Rare words split into known chunks (for example "playing" becomes "play" + "##ing"), so the model never meets a truly unknown word. BERT then wraps the input with two special tokens:

  • [CLS] - a token glued to the front of every input. Its final vector is used as a summary of the whole sequence for classification.
  • [SEP] - a separator placed between two sentences (and at the very end), so the model can tell sentence A from sentence B.

Each token's input vector is the sum of three learned embeddings: the token itself, its position, and which segment (A or B) it belongs to.

4. Pretraining task 1: the Masked Language Model

Here is the heart of the paper. If every token could see every other token, a left-to-right prediction target would be trivial - the answer is right there in the input. So BERT changes the target. It randomly picks 15% of the tokens and hides them, then trains the model to predict the originals from the surrounding context. Because the hidden word could be anywhere, the model must build a representation that fuses both sides.

One wrinkle: the special [MASK] token appears only during pretraining, never during fine-tuning, which would create a mismatch. BERT softens this. Of the 15% chosen tokens: 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% are replaced with a random word, and 10% are left unchanged. The model never knows which case it is looking at, so it must keep a good guess for every token.

5. Pretraining task 2: Next Sentence Prediction

Many tasks (question answering, natural language inference) are about the relationship between two sentences, which word-level prediction alone does not teach. So BERT adds a second, sentence-level objective. Build training pairs where half the time sentence B genuinely follows sentence A in the corpus (label IsNext) and half the time B is a random sentence from elsewhere (label NotNext). The [CLS] vector feeds a tiny classifier that predicts which case it is.

6. The two-phase recipe: pretrain, then fine-tune

The whole point is reuse. You pretrain once, expensively, on a huge unlabeled corpus (BooksCorpus, 800M words, plus English Wikipedia, 2,500M words). Then for each downstream task you add one small output layer and fine-tune the entire model briefly on the labeled task data. The same pretrained weights become a sentiment classifier, a question answerer, or an entailment model, just by swapping the head.

7. The math: the masked-LM loss

Only the masked positions contribute to the language-model loss. For each masked position the model outputs a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary, and we penalise it by the negative log probability it gave to the true token. That is ordinary cross-entropy, summed over the masked set.

LMLM = − ∑i ∈ M log P( xi | x\M ) M: the set of masked positions (about 15% of tokens). xi: the true token that used to be at position i. x\M: the whole sequence with the masked positions hidden - the context the model is allowed to see. P( xi | ... ): the probability the model assigns to the correct token there. We take the log, negate it, and add it up: guessing the right word with high confidence makes the loss small.

The full pretraining loss is simply this masked-LM term plus the next-sentence classification loss, added together and minimised at the same time.

Why only the masked positions Because attention is bidirectional, an unmasked token can trivially see itself, so predicting it would teach nothing. By scoring the loss only at the hidden positions, BERT forces every prediction to come from surrounding context. This is the single design choice that makes honest deep bidirectionality possible.

8. Implement it: a bidirectional encoder block + MLM head

Below is the core of BERT in small, runnable PyTorch: one bidirectional encoder block (note there is no causal mask - compare to Lesson 01), stacked a few times, topped with a masked-LM head, plus a tiny loop showing how the 15% masking and cross-entropy loss fit together.

bert_core.py · runnable with torch
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class EncoderBlock(nn.Module):
    """A BERT encoder block: bidirectional self-attention, no causal mask."""
    def __init__(self, d_model=256, n_heads=4, mlp_mult=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(d_model, n_heads, batch_first=True)
        self.ln1  = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.mlp  = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(d_model, mlp_mult * d_model),
            nn.GELU(),                                 # BERT uses GELU, not ReLU
            nn.Linear(mlp_mult * d_model, d_model),
        )
        self.ln2  = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)

    def forward(self, x, pad_mask=None):
        # key_padding_mask hides padding only. There is NO causal mask:
        # every token may attend to every other token, left and right.
        a, _ = self.attn(x, x, x, key_padding_mask=pad_mask, need_weights=False)
        x = self.ln1(x + a)                            # residual + norm
        x = self.ln2(x + self.mlp(x))                  # residual + norm
        return x

class TinyBERT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab=30000, d_model=256, n_layers=4, max_len=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.tok = nn.Embedding(vocab, d_model)
        self.pos = nn.Embedding(max_len, d_model)
        self.seg = nn.Embedding(2, d_model)            # segment A / B
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList(EncoderBlock(d_model) for _ in range(n_layers))
        self.mlm_head = nn.Linear(d_model, vocab)      # predict a token per position

    def forward(self, ids, seg=None, pad_mask=None):
        B, N = ids.shape
        pos = torch.arange(N, device=ids.device).unsqueeze(0)
        x = self.tok(ids) + self.pos(pos)
        if seg is not None:
            x = x + self.seg(seg)
        for blk in self.blocks:
            x = blk(x, pad_mask)
        return self.mlm_head(x)                        # (B, N, vocab) logits

# ---- one masked-LM training step -------------------------------------------
MASK_ID = 103                                          # id of the [MASK] token
model = TinyBERT()
ids   = torch.randint(1000, 30000, (2, 16))            # fake batch: 2 seqs, len 16
labels = ids.clone()

# choose 15% of positions to mask, remember their true tokens as labels
prob = torch.full(ids.shape, 0.15)
masked = torch.bernoulli(prob).bool()
labels[~masked] = -100                                 # -100 = ignore in the loss
ids[masked] = MASK_ID                                  # (simplified: all -> [MASK])

logits = model(ids)                                    # (2, 16, vocab)
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, 30000), labels.view(-1), ignore_index=-100)
print(round(loss.item(), 3))                           # only masked spots count

Read it against the diagrams. The EncoderBlock is Lesson 01's block with the causal mask removed. The training step picks 15% of positions, replaces them with [MASK], and scores cross-entropy only on those positions (the ignore_index=-100 line is how "only the masked positions" is enforced in code).

9. BERT versus the GPT branch

PropertyBERT (this paper)GPT branch
Transformer half keptEncoderDecoder
Attention directionBidirectionalLeft-to-right (causal)
Pretraining objectiveMasked LM plus NSPNext-token prediction
Natural strengthUnderstanding and taggingFree-form generation
Downstream useFine-tune a task headPrompt or fine-tune
Can write a paragraph?No, not designed toYes, that is its job

Neither branch is "better" - they answer different questions. BERT dominated the 2018-2020 era of classification and extraction leaderboards (a GLUE score of 80.5 and SQuAD v1.1 F1 of 93.2, both records at the time). The GPT branch, which you meet next, chased generation and scale, and that is the path that led to modern chat models.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What makes BERT bidirectional where a plain language model is not?
The Masked LM hides tokens and predicts them from surrounding context on both sides, inside one deep stack - honest bidirectionality, not a glued pair of models.
Why score the masked-LM loss only at the hidden positions?
With bidirectional attention, a visible token trivially sees its own identity. Only hidden positions force a prediction from context.
What is the [CLS] token's final vector mainly used for?
The [CLS] vector aggregates the whole sequence and feeds the classification head (for example, Next Sentence Prediction or sentiment).
How does BERT get reused across many different tasks?
One expensive pretraining yields a general checkpoint; each task adds a small output layer and a short fine-tune.

How this connects

  • Built on Attention Is All You Need - BERT is that paper's encoder stack with the causal mask removed.
  • Contrast with GPT-1, the very next lesson, which keeps the decoder half and trains left-to-right generation instead.
  • The subword tokenizer idea here (WordPiece) becomes byte-level BPE in GPT-2.
  • BERT's bidirectional attention still pays the O(N²) cost, the target of FlashAttention and friends in Module 05.
Your teacher. The trickiest idea here is why you cannot just train a bidirectional model with a next-token target. Ask the agent to walk through what would go wrong (the token can peek at itself), then to hand-trace the 80/10/10 masking on a short sentence. Do not move on until the Masked LM feels obviously necessary.
