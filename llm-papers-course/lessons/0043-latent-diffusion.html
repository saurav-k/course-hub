Latent Diffusion

frontier 2021 16 min Rombach, Blattmann, Lorenz, Esser, Ommer · LMU Munich and Runway · arXiv:2112.10752

The one-minute version

  • Diffusion on raw pixels is unaffordable. Compress the image first with an autoencoder, run the whole diffusion process in that small latent space, and decode once at the end.
  • At the usual settings that is 48 times fewer numbers to denoise at every one of the many steps, which is the difference between a datacentre artifact and one people run at home.
  • This is the convergence point of the whole module. One checkpoint contains a VAE doing the compression, an adversarial loss that trained it, a CLIP text encoder supplying the prompt, a diffusion model doing the generation, and after DiT a Vision Transformer as the denoiser.
  • That is also why the "old" papers are not optional history. If you can name those five parts inside one artifact, this module has done its job.

1. Why this paper exists

The previous lesson ended on a cost problem. Diffusion needs many forward passes per image, and in pixel space every one of those passes operates on every pixel of a full-resolution picture. Multiply a large tensor by many steps and the bill is why high-quality image generation was, until this paper, something only large labs did.

The observation the paper builds on is that most of the bits in an image are not where the meaning is. Fine texture and imperceptible detail cost a great deal of compute and carry little of what makes the picture that picture. So split the job in two: let a separate model handle the perceptually unimportant compression once, and let diffusion work only in the space that is left.

2. Two stages, trained separately

3. The arithmetic that makes it affordable

Do the count rather than accepting "smaller". The usual configuration downsamples by 8 in each spatial direction and uses 4 latent channels where the image had 3.

  1. The image. 512 × 512 pixels with 3 colour channels is 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 numbers.
  2. The latent. Downsampling by 8 gives 512 ÷ 8 = 64 per side, and 4 channels, so 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 numbers.
  3. The ratio. 786,432 ÷ 16,384 = 48. Every reverse step now touches 48 times fewer values, and a sampling run makes many such steps, so the saving multiplies by the step count.

4. The convergence: five papers in one checkpoint

Now open the box. The abstract does not carry this detail, so it is worth quoting the method section directly: the autoencoder is trained with "a combination of a perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial objective". For the latent itself the paper offers two regularisations. One "imposes a slight KL-penalty towards a standard normal on the learned latent, similar to a VAE"; the other "uses a vector quantization layer within the decoder".

Read those two sentences against the last three lessons and the parts name themselves.

The sentence to keep The adversarial term is exactly what stops the VAE's blur reaching the reader. Lesson 40 showed that a reconstruction loss wants an average; lesson 41 showed that a discriminator punishes an average. Latent diffusion puts them in the same optimiser, and that is why a decoder trained this way returns sharp texture rather than a mean.

5. The generation path, in order

latent_pipeline.py · runnable with torch, components are stand-ins
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

H = W = 512
DOWN, ZCH = 8, 4                                   # 8x spatial, 4 latent channels

encoder = nn.Conv2d(3, ZCH, kernel_size=DOWN, stride=DOWN)      # image -> latent
decoder = nn.ConvTranspose2d(ZCH, 3, kernel_size=DOWN, stride=DOWN)
denoiser = nn.Conv2d(ZCH + 1, ZCH, kernel_size=3, padding=1)    # stands for a U-Net
text_encoder = nn.Embedding(1000, 64)                           # stands for CLIP

def predict_noise(z, t, cond):
    """A real denoiser cross-attends to cond at every block. Here the shapes
    are what matters: the prompt never enters the latent, it steers the model."""
    t_map = torch.full_like(z[:, :1], float(t) / 50.0)
    return denoiser(torch.cat([z, t_map], dim=1)) * (1.0 + 0.0 * cond.mean())

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(prompt_ids, steps=50, guidance=7.5):
    cond = text_encoder(prompt_ids)                 # encoded once, reused every step
    uncond = torch.zeros_like(cond)                 # the blank prompt, for guidance
    z = torch.randn(1, ZCH, H // DOWN, W // DOWN)   # noise, in LATENT space
    for t in reversed(range(steps)):
        eps_c = predict_noise(z, t, cond)
        eps_u = predict_noise(z, t, uncond)
        eps = eps_u + guidance * (eps_c - eps_u)    # classifier-free guidance
        z = z - eps / steps                         # a stand-in for the real update
    return decoder(z)                               # decode ONCE, at the end

img = torch.rand(1, 3, H, W)
print("image elements ", img.numel())               # 786432
print("latent elements", encoder(img).numel())      # 16384
print("ratio          ", img.numel() // encoder(img).numel())   # 48
print("output shape   ", generate(torch.tensor([[1, 2, 3]])).shape)

The update rule in that loop is deliberately a stand-in; the sampler from the previous lesson belongs there. What is faithful is the structure: encode or start in latent space, loop with guidance, decode once. Every shipped image pipeline has that shape.

6. The reading order is not the dependency order

This module was read as ViT, CLIP, VAE, GAN, diffusion, and now this. That is not the order the ideas arrived in, and it is worth saying plainly where the two differ.

The VAE is from 2013 and the GAN from 2014, six and seven years before ViT. The original diffusion paper is from 2015, and DDPM predates ViT by four months. Nothing in the generative line depends on anything in the vision-transformer line. The module reads the other way round for one reason: the payoff lesson is this one, and you cannot explain the conditioning inside a latent diffusion model without CLIP, or CLIP without ViT. Teaching the generative line first would mean either explaining this checkpoint twice or leaving its text encoder unexplained.

7. Does this still matter in 2026?

Yes, and more than any single paper in this module. The two-stage shape - a frozen learned codec, generation inside its latent, one decode at the end - is what every shipped image and video model looks like. What has moved is the inside of stage two: the U-Net has been replaced by a transformer, and the diffusion path itself by rectified flow in the newest systems.

It also gives you a debugging heuristic worth carrying. When generated images come out with washed colours, mushy fine detail or odd artefacts in flat regions, the fault is usually in the codec rather than the sampler, because the sampler never saw a pixel. That is exactly why the community swaps decoder checkpoints, and it only makes sense once you know the two stages are separate models.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does latent diffusion move into the latent space?
Every reverse step happens in the latent, and pixels are produced by one decode at the end. The text encoder stays outside, and this is not a storage optimisation.
Which loss keeps the decoder's output from going blurry?
The autoencoder is trained with a perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial objective, and the adversarial term is the anti-blur one. The KL penalty tidies the latent, and guidance and schedules belong to stage two.
Which five papers are inside one such checkpoint?
The autoencoder is a VAE trained partly adversarially, conditioning is a CLIP text encoder, generation is diffusion, and the backbone became a ViT after DiT. The other lists mix in language-only papers that are not in the pipeline.
Generated images look washed out. Where do you look first?
The sampler never sees a pixel - it works entirely in the latent. Colour and fine texture are produced by the decoder, which is why swapping that checkpoint is the usual fix.
