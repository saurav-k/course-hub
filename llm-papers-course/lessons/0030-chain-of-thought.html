Chain-of-Thought Prompting

2022 Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, Zhou (Google Research). · arXiv:2201.11903

The one-minute version

  • Large models were surprisingly bad at multi-step arithmetic and reasoning when asked for just the final answer.
  • Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting fixes this by showing a few examples where the answer comes after a written-out series of intermediate reasoning steps.
  • The model imitates that style and reasons step by step on the new question, giving large gains on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic tasks.
  • It is an emergent ability: it barely helps small models, then appears sharply at roughly 100B parameters. On GSM8K math with a 540B model and just 8 exemplars, CoT set a new state of the art.

1. Why this paper exists

By 2022 large language models could write fluent prose but stumbled on problems a schoolchild can do: "Roger has 5 balls, buys 2 cans of 3, how many now?" Ask for just the number and even a huge model often blurts a wrong one. The issue is not knowledge; it is that a single forward pass has to compress all the intermediate arithmetic into one leap to the answer. Hard multi-step problems do not fit in one leap.

The fix requires no training, no gradient updates, no new data pipeline. You simply change the prompt. That is what makes CoT such a landmark: it revealed that the reasoning ability was already latent in the model, and the right prompt unlocks it.

2. The mechanism: exemplars that show their work

CoT is a form of few-shot prompting. You prepend a handful of worked examples (exemplars) to the question. In standard few-shot each exemplar is just question then answer. In chain-of-thought each exemplar is question, then a reasoning chain, then answer. The model, being an imitator of the pattern in its context, continues the new question in the same show-your-work style.

Why does writing steps help at all? Two reasons. First, each generated token is fed back in as input, so a reasoning chain lets the model store partial results in its own output and read them again - a scratchpad. Second, spreading a hard computation across many tokens gives the model more forward passes of compute to reach the answer, instead of cramming everything into one.

3. It is an emergent ability

The paper's most striking finding is about scale. CoT does not gently help models of every size. On small models it can even hurt, producing fluent but wrong chains. Then, somewhere around 100 billion parameters, the benefit appears sharply and grows. Reasoning through prompting is an emergent ability: absent below a scale threshold, present above it.

Concretely, the authors used PaLM 540B with only eight chain-of-thought exemplars and set a new state of the art on GSM8K, a benchmark of grade-school math word problems, surpassing even a finetuned GPT-3 that had a separate verifier. The gains held across arithmetic (GSM8K, SVAMP), commonsense (StrategyQA), and symbolic tasks (last-letter concatenation, coin flips).

4. Two important follow-ups

CoT sparked a small family of prompting ideas. Two are essential to know.

Zero-shot CoT. Kojima et al. (arXiv:2205.11916) showed you often do not even need worked exemplars. Just appending the phrase "Let's think step by step" to the question triggers a reasoning chain on its own. One sentence, no examples, big gains.

Self-consistency. Wang et al. (arXiv:2203.11171) noted that a single chain can go wrong by a fluke. So sample many chains with some randomness, then take a majority vote over their final answers. Different reasoning paths that converge on the same answer reinforce each other; a lone mistaken path gets outvoted.

5. The math, read in plain English

Standard prompting asks the model for the answer directly. CoT inserts a latent reasoning chain z between the question and the answer, and marginalizes over it. Self-consistency approximates that marginal by sampling and voting.

â = argmaxa   P(a ÷ question)   vs   â = argmaxa   Σz P(a, z ÷ question) Left (standard): pick the answer a that is most probable given the question alone, with no room to reason. Right (CoT): sum over possible reasoning chains z, so the answer is supported by a whole path of steps. Σz: in practice you cannot enumerate every chain, so you sample a few and let them vote.
â = mode { a(1), a(2), …, a(N) },   each a(i) from an independently sampled chain z(i) a(i): the final answer extracted from the i-th sampled reasoning chain. mode {…}: the most frequent answer across all N chains - the majority vote. Sampling with temperature above zero makes the chains diverse, so agreement among them is meaningful evidence.

6. Implement it: standard vs CoT prompt, plus self-consistency vote

The code builds both prompt styles and shows the self-consistency loop. It uses a stub call_model so it runs offline; swap in a real API call and it becomes a working reasoner.

chain_of_thought.py · runnable (model call stubbed)
import re
from collections import Counter

# --- two ways to build the same question into a prompt ---
def standard_prompt(question):
    # few-shot, answer only: no room to reason
    ex = ("Q: There are 3 cars and 2 more arrive. How many cars?\n"
          "A: 5\n\n")
    return ex + f"Q: {question}\nA:"

def cot_prompt(question):
    # few-shot, WITH a worked reasoning chain before each answer
    ex = ("Q: There are 3 cars and 2 more arrive. How many cars?\n"
          "A: Start with 3 cars. 2 more arrive. 3 + 2 = 5. "
          "The answer is 5.\n\n")
    return ex + f"Q: {question}\nA: Let's think step by step."

# --- pull the final number out of a generated answer ---
def extract_answer(text):
    nums = re.findall(r"-?\d+", text)
    return nums[-1] if nums else None          # last number = final answer

# --- self-consistency: sample many chains, majority vote ---
def self_consistency(question, call_model, n=5):
    answers = []
    for _ in range(n):
        # temperature > 0 inside call_model makes each chain different
        out = call_model(cot_prompt(question))
        ans = extract_answer(out)
        if ans is not None:
            answers.append(ans)
    if not answers:
        return None, []
    winner, _ = Counter(answers).most_common(1)[0]   # the majority answer
    return winner, answers

# --- stub model: pretend samples, mostly right with one slip ---
def call_model(prompt):
    import random
    chains = [
        "A robe takes 2 bolts blue and half that white. "
        "Half of 2 is 1. 2 + 1 = 3. The answer is 3.",
        "Blue is 2 bolts. White is half of 2 = 1. Total 2 + 1 = 3. Answer: 3.",
        "2 blue and 1 white. 2 + 1 = 3. The answer is 3.",
        "2 blue, half white so 1. Sum is 2 + 1 = 4. The answer is 4.",  # slip
    ]
    return random.choice(chains)

q = "A robe takes 2 bolts of blue and half that much white. How many total?"
print("STANDARD prompt:\n", standard_prompt(q), "\n")
print("COT prompt:\n", cot_prompt(q), "\n")
final, votes = self_consistency(q, call_model, n=7)
print("sampled answers:", votes)
print("majority answer:", final)     # 3 wins the vote despite the odd slip

Notice how little there is to it. The only structural difference between standard_prompt and cot_prompt is that the exemplar shows its working and the new question ends with "Let's think step by step." Everything else - the scratchpad effect, the emergent gains - comes from the model, not from clever code.

Prompt, not training CoT changes nothing inside the network. It is the clearest demonstration in the course that a big model's behavior is steerable at inference time. The same weights that fail with a bare question succeed when the prompt invites reasoning. That realization is what launches the rest of Module 08.

7. Where it wins

Task typeExample benchmarkStandard promptChain-of-thought
ArithmeticGSM8KWeakState of the art (540B)
CommonsenseStrategyQAModestClear improvement
SymbolicLast-letter concatPoor on long inputsGeneralizes further
Single-fact recallTrivia lookupFine alreadyLittle to gain

The pattern: chain-of-thought helps most where the answer requires multiple dependent steps. For a one-hop lookup there is nothing to reason through, so it adds little. For a problem that needs working, it is transformative - and it costs only a few extra lines in the prompt.

What does a chain-of-thought exemplar add compared to a standard one?
CoT exemplars demonstrate the format of reasoning-then-answer, and the model copies that show-your-work style on the new question.
Why is chain-of-thought described as an emergent ability?
Below about 100B parameters CoT barely helps or hurts; above it, the same prompt unlocks a sharp jump in reasoning accuracy.
How does self-consistency improve on a single chain of thought?
Multiple diverse chains are sampled; correct reasoning converges on the same answer and wins the vote, outvoting a lone mistaken path.
What is the zero-shot chain-of-thought trigger from Kojima et al.?
Zero-shot CoT needs no exemplars: just appending "Let's think step by step" elicits a reasoning chain on its own.

How this connects

  • CoT is the seed of the whole reasoning module. Tree of Thoughts generalizes a single chain into a searchable tree of chains.
  • ReAct interleaves CoT-style reasoning with tool actions, so the model can look things up mid-thought.
  • Spending more inference compute on reasoning is exactly what Test-Time Scaling formalizes and pushes to its limit.
  • Emergence at scale ties back to GPT-3's few-shot learning and the scaling laws of Module 02.
Your teacher. Try it live. Ask the agent a two-step word problem twice: once demanding "just the number," once with "let's think step by step." Watch the difference, then ask it to sample five chains and vote. Seeing self-consistency rescue a wrong lone chain is the fastest way to trust the idea.
