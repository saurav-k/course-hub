Variational Autoencoders (VAE)

frontier 2013 15 min Kingma, Welling · University of Amsterdam · arXiv:1312.6114

The one-minute version

  • You want a generative model with continuous hidden variables, but the posterior over those variables is intractable, so you cannot train it. The VAE's answer is to approximate the posterior with a second network and optimise a lower bound instead of the true likelihood.
  • The move that made it work is the reparameterisation trick: do not sample from a distribution whose parameters you need gradients through. Sample fixed noise and push it through a deterministic function of those parameters instead.
  • Its samples are blurry for a structural reason, not a tuning one. A Gaussian decoder likelihood is a per-pixel squared error, and the best answer under squared error is an average.
  • In 2026 nobody trains a VAE to generate images, and almost everybody runs one. The "latent" in latent diffusion is a VAE latent, so it survives as a codec rather than as a generator.

1. Why this paper exists

Suppose you believe your data was produced in two steps: draw a hidden vector z from some simple distribution, then draw the image from a distribution that depends on z. That story is attractive because z is short and the image is long, so z is where the meaning lives.

Training it means asking, for an image you have, which z produced it. That is the posterior, and for any decoder worth using it has no closed form. Before this paper you attacked that with Markov chains or expensive approximate inference, neither of which scales to a large dataset and neither of which fits inside a gradient-descent loop.

The VAE makes the intractable step into a second neural network. An encoder looks at the image and proposes a distribution over z. Training then optimises how good that proposal is at the same time as it optimises the decoder.

2. The mechanism

Why a cloud rather than a point. An ordinary autoencoder maps each image to one latent point, and the space between the points can decode to nothing meaningful. Forcing the encoder to emit a distribution, and penalising it for straying from a standard normal, packs the latents into a region you can later sample from blindly. That is the difference between a compressor and a generative model.

3. The reparameterisation trick

Here is the obstacle. You need the gradient of the loss with respect to the encoder's parameters, but the path from the encoder to the loss goes through a random draw, and you cannot differentiate through "sample from this distribution".

The paper states the consequence plainly: "a reparameterization of the variational lower bound yields a lower bound estimator that can be straightforwardly optimized using standard stochastic gradient methods". After that sentence a latent-variable generative model is just a network you train with your usual optimiser.

The idea that outlived the model Reparameterisation is not about images. It is the general answer to backpropagating through a sampling step, and it turns up wherever a stochastic node sits in the middle of a differentiable system. The VAE is where you meet it; it is not where it stays.

4. The maths, read aloud

The objective is a lower bound on the log-likelihood of the data, usually called the ELBO. It has exactly two terms, and both have a plain reading.

L(x) = Eq(z|x)[ log p(x|z) ] − KL( q(z|x) ‖ p(z) ) q(z|x): the encoder's proposed distribution over latents for this image. p(x|z): the decoder's distribution over images given a latent. Eq(z|x)[ log p(x|z) ]: the reconstruction term - draw a latent from the encoder's proposal and ask how probable the real image is under the decoder. Higher is better. KL( q(z|x) ‖ p(z) ): the Kullback-Leibler divergence, a measure of how far the proposal has drifted from the prior p(z), which is a standard normal. It is subtracted, so drifting is penalised. L(x): the bound. It is never above the true log-likelihood, so pushing it up pushes the real quantity up too.
z = μ(x) + σ(x) · ε,   ε ~ N(0, 1) μ(x) and σ(x): the mean and standard deviation the encoder outputs for this image, both ordinary differentiable functions of the encoder's weights. ε: a draw from a standard normal, which depends on nothing you are training. z: the latent, now a differentiable function of μ and σ with the randomness supplied from outside. Read it as: keep the noise, move it out of the way.

5. Implement it

vae.py · runnable with torch
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_dim=784, hidden=400, latent=20):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(in_dim, hidden), nn.ReLU())
        self.mu = nn.Linear(hidden, latent)          # centre of the proposal
        self.logvar = nn.Linear(hidden, latent)      # log variance, so it can be negative
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(latent, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(hidden, in_dim))

    def encode(self, x):
        h = self.enc(x)
        return self.mu(h), self.logvar(h)

    def reparameterise(self, mu, logvar):
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)                # log-variance to std
        eps = torch.randn_like(std)                  # randomness from outside
        return mu + std * eps                        # differentiable in mu and std

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.encode(x)
        z = self.reparameterise(mu, logvar)
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar

def elbo_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar):
    """Negative ELBO, so lower is better. Two terms, summed per image."""
    recon = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction="sum")
    # closed form of KL(N(mu, sigma) || N(0, 1)) for a diagonal Gaussian
    kl = -0.5 * torch.sum(1 + logvar - mu.pow(2) - logvar.exp())
    return recon + kl

model = VAE()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
x = torch.rand(64, 784)                              # stand-in for a batch of images
for step in range(3):
    logits, mu, logvar = model(x)
    loss = elbo_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar) / len(x)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    print(step, round(float(loss), 2))

# generation needs no input image: sample the prior and decode
with torch.no_grad():
    sample = torch.sigmoid(model.dec(torch.randn(8, 20)))
print(sample.shape)                                  # torch.Size([8, 784])

The last three lines are the point of the whole model. Generation draws z from a standard normal and decodes it, with no image involved, and that only produces something sensible because the KL term spent training pushing the encoder's clouds towards exactly that distribution.

6. What it did not solve: the blur has a cause

VAE samples are famously soft, and the reason is worth teaching because it is a property of the loss rather than of the network. A Gaussian decoder makes the reconstruction term a per-pixel squared error. When several different sharp images are all plausible for one latent, the prediction that minimises expected squared error is not any of them - it is their average, and the average of sharp images is a blurry image.

The second failure is posterior collapse. Pair a VAE with a decoder powerful enough to model the data on its own and the model learns to ignore the latent entirely: the KL term is happiest at zero, and a strong decoder does not need z. VQ-VAE was invented to dodge exactly this and says so, describing vector quantisation as a way to "circumvent issues of 'posterior collapse' - where the latents are ignored when they are paired with a powerful autoregressive decoder".

7. Does this still matter in 2026?

Yes, but not as a generative model. It matters as a codec. This is the judgement to carry out of the lesson.

Nobody in 2026 trains a VAE to generate pictures. But everyone running a latent diffusion model runs a VAE on every single generation, because the latent that diffusion operates in is a VAE latent, encoded on the way in and decoded on the way out. That has a very practical consequence: when generated images come out with washed colours or mushy fine detail, the encoder and decoder are the first place a competent engineer looks, not the sampler.

The discrete branch matters more broadly still. Vector quantisation of a continuous signal - the VQ-VAE move - is the mechanism behind turning audio and images into token sequences a language model can read, which is what "everything becomes tokens" means in an early-fusion multimodal model.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What problem does the reparameterisation trick solve?
Sampling directly from the encoder's distribution breaks the gradient path. Drawing fixed noise first and combining it arithmetically restores it. The other three describe things the trick does not do.
Why are VAE samples blurry?
A Gaussian decoder makes reconstruction a per-pixel squared error, whose optimum is the mean of the possibilities. Latent width and network sizes change the degree, not the cause.
What is posterior collapse?
A decoder strong enough to model the data alone lets the KL term drive the latent towards the prior and carry no information. VQ-VAE's discrete latents were introduced to avoid exactly this.
Where does a VAE actually run in a 2026 image pipeline?
Latent diffusion encodes to a latent, denoises there, and decodes back. The VAE is the codec at both ends; the generation in the middle is diffusion's job.

How this connects

  • Generative Adversarial Networks is the other answer to the blur problem: instead of fixing the loss, learn it.
  • Diffusion Models are trained on a weighted variational bound, so the mathematics of this lesson is the mathematics of that one.
  • Latent Diffusion is where the VAE reappears as the compression stage, and where its blurriness is fixed by a term borrowed from the next lesson.
  • The discrete branch is why a picture or a sound can become a token sequence at all, which is the assumption behind every early-fusion multimodal model.
