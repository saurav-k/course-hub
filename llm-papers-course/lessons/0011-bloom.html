BLOOM: A 176B-Parameter Open-Access Multilingual Model

2022 BigScience Workshop (Scao, Fan, Akiki, and hundreds of collaborators). · arXiv:2211.05100

The one-minute version

  • BLOOM is a 176-billion-parameter open multilingual model built by BigScience, a one-year collaboration of hundreds of researchers - science done in the open, by committee, on purpose.
  • It covers 46 natural languages and 13 programming languages, trained on the ROOTS corpus (about 1.6TB) assembled with explicit data governance.
  • It trained on the public French Jean Zay supercomputer, and uses ALiBi position embeddings and an extra embedding LayerNorm for stability.
  • Its point is open science and language inclusion: most large models were English-first and closed; BLOOM is many-language and fully documented.

1. Why this paper exists

The large models before BLOOM shared two blind spots. They were overwhelmingly English (or a handful of high-resource languages), and their data pipelines were opaque - nobody outside the lab knew exactly what went in. For most of the world's languages, and for anyone who cares where training text comes from, that was a problem. BLOOM was built to fix both, and to prove that a frontier model could be produced by an open, international collaboration rather than a single company.

2. BigScience: open science as method

BLOOM is unusual in that the process is part of the contribution. BigScience ran for roughly a year with working groups on data, modelling, evaluation, ethics, and governance. Decisions were documented publicly. The compute was granted on Jean Zay, a public French national supercomputer - not a private data center. The result is a model whose entire lineage, from data selection to training config, is inspectable.

3. The ROOTS corpus and data governance

BLOOM's training data is the ROOTS corpus: roughly 1.6TB of text spanning 46 natural languages and 13 programming languages. What sets it apart is governance - sources were chosen deliberately, documented, and vetted for licensing and consent, rather than scraped indiscriminately. The language mix was curated so that lower-resource languages actually get meaningful representation instead of being drowned out by English.

4. Two architecture choices: ALiBi and embedding LayerNorm

BLOOM is a decoder-only Transformer, but with two notable tweaks aimed at stability and length generalisation.

ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases, Lesson 14) replaces positional embeddings entirely. Instead of adding a position vector to each token, ALiBi adds a small penalty to attention scores that grows with the distance between tokens: the farther away a token is, the less it is attended to. This helps the model handle sequences longer than it saw in training.

score(i, j) = qi · kj − m · (i − j) qi · kj: the usual attention match between query i and key j. (i − j): how many positions apart the two tokens are. m: a fixed per-head slope - a small constant that sets how harshly distance is penalised. Subtracting m · (i − j) means closer tokens get a bonus, distant tokens a penalty, and there are no learned position vectors at all.

Embedding LayerNorm - BLOOM adds a normalisation layer right after the token embeddings, before the first Transformer block. This small addition improved training stability at 176B scale, damping the kind of instabilities OPT's logbook (Lesson 10) documented.

5. Multilingual tokenization and fertility

A tokenizer breaks text into subword pieces. For a multilingual model this is delicate: a tokenizer trained mostly on English will chop other languages into many tiny pieces, wasting sequence length and hurting quality. The metric for this is fertility: the average number of tokens per word. Lower is better - it means the tokenizer represents the language efficiently.

BLOOM's tokenizer is a byte-level BPE with byte fallback. Byte-level BPE means the base alphabet is the 256 raw bytes, so any character in any language or script can always be represented as a sequence of bytes. There are no unknown tokens ever - a character it never saw is simply spelled out byte by byte. That guarantee is what lets one tokenizer serve 46 languages and many scripts.

6. Implement it: tokenizer fertility comparison

Fertility is easy to measure and instructive to see. This script compares tokens-per-word across languages for two tokenizers, and demonstrates the byte-fallback guarantee: no character is ever unrepresentable.

fertility.py · runnable with transformers + tokenizers
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

# BLOOM's multilingual byte-level BPE vs an English-centric one
multi = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bigscience/bloom-560m")
eng   = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")   # English-centric baseline

samples = {
    "English":  "internationalization of machine learning models",
    "French":   "l'internationalisation des modeles d'apprentissage",
    "Hindi":    "मशीन लर्निंग मॉडल",   # 'machine learning model'
    "Arabic":   "نماذج تعلم الآلة",          # 'machine learning models'
}

def fertility(tok, text):
    words  = text.split()
    tokens = tok.encode(text)
    return len(tokens) / max(len(words), 1)   # tokens per word: lower is better

print(f"{'language':10} {'BLOOM':>8} {'GPT-2':>8}")
for lang, text in samples.items():
    fm = fertility(multi, text)
    fe = fertility(eng,   text)
    print(f"{lang:10} {fm:8.2f} {fe:8.2f}")

# Byte-fallback guarantee: any character round-trips, even unseen scripts.
exotic = "\U0001F600 ᚠᚢ こんにちは"    # emoji, runes, Japanese
ids = multi.encode(exotic)
assert multi.decode(ids).strip() == exotic.strip()   # nothing is ever 'unknown'
print("byte fallback round-trip OK:", exotic)

The pattern you will see: for English, both tokenizers are efficient. For Hindi and Arabic, the English-centric GPT-2 tokenizer has much higher fertility - it shreds the text into far more tokens - while BLOOM's multilingual tokenizer stays lean. The final assertion shows byte fallback: emoji, runes, and Japanese all round-trip perfectly, because at worst a character is encoded as its raw bytes. No token is ever unknown.

Why fertility is fairness High fertility is a hidden tax on a language. If your language needs three times as many tokens per sentence, you pay more for inference, fit less in the context window, and often get worse quality. Building a tokenizer across all target languages is a concrete act of inclusion, not just an engineering nicety.

What most distinguishes BLOOM from earlier large models?
BLOOM's identity is open science plus language inclusion: 46+13 languages, a governed ROOTS corpus, built by a global collaboration.
What does ALiBi do instead of adding position vectors?
ALiBi biases attention: it subtracts a slope times the token distance from each score, favouring nearby tokens without any position vectors.
In tokenization, what does "fertility" measure?
Fertility is tokens per word; lower means the tokenizer represents a language more efficiently, which matters a lot across many languages.
What guarantee does byte-fallback BPE provide?
Because the base alphabet is the 256 bytes, any character can be spelled out byte by byte, so there is never an unknown token.
