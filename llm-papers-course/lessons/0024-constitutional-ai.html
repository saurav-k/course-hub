Constitutional AI

2022 Bai, Kadavath, Kundu, Askell, Kernion, Jones, ... Kaplan (Anthropic). "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback." · arXiv:2212.08073

The one-minute version

  • RLHF (Lesson 23) needs humans to label thousands of harmful outputs - slow, costly, and hard on the labelers who read toxic content all day.
  • Constitutional AI replaces most of that human labeling with a short written constitution: a list of plain-English principles the model uses to police itself.
  • Phase 1 (supervised): the model answers a harmful prompt, then critiques its own answer against a sampled principle and revises it. Fine-tune on the revised answers.
  • Phase 2 (RLAIF): an AI - not a human - compares answer pairs for harmlessness using the constitution, producing preference data. Train a reward model on it, then run RLHF-style RL.
  • Result: a model that is harmless and non-evasive - it explains why it declines instead of stonewalling - trained with far fewer human harmlessness labels.

1. Why this paper exists

InstructGPT showed that human feedback aligns a model. But the harmlessness half of that feedback is expensive in a way that does not scale. To teach a model not to help with dangerous requests, humans must read thousands of harmful model outputs and label them. That is slow, costly, and genuinely unpleasant work. It also caps quality: a model can only be as consistent as the crowd of humans labeling it, and humans disagree.

Constitutional AI asks a sharper question: what if the model supervised itself? Humans still set the values, but they do it once, in writing, as a small list of principles - a constitution. From then on the model uses those principles to critique and improve its own behaviour, and to generate its own preference labels. The only human oversight is the rulebook.

2. The whole method in one picture

The method has two phases, run in order, starting from a model that is already helpful (a helpful-only RLHF assistant). Phase 1 uses supervised learning; Phase 2 uses reinforcement learning from AI feedback.

3. Phase 1: critique then revise

Start with a prompt designed to elicit harm ("How do I pick a lock?"). The helpful-only model answers, and because it was trained only to be helpful, the answer is often genuinely harmful. Now the trick: the model is asked to critique its own answer against a randomly sampled principle from the constitution ("Identify ways the response is harmful or unethical"), then revise the answer in light of that critique. Repeat the critique-revise loop a few times, drawing a fresh principle each step. Finally, fine-tune the original model on the final revised answers.

Why revise instead of just filter You could throw away harmful answers, but then the model never learns the correction. Revising keeps the useful shape of a reply while stripping the harm, so the model learns to be harmless and non-evasive - it engages with a hard question by explaining its objection rather than refusing flatly.

4. Phase 2: reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF)

Phase 1 gives a decent harmless model, but supervised imitation only goes so far. Phase 2 borrows the RLHF machinery from Lesson 23 - reward model plus RL - but swaps the human labeler for an AI. The SL-CAI model generates a pair of answers to each prompt. A feedback model is shown both answers as a multiple-choice question ("Which response is more harmless, per this principle?") and its answer, weighted by the probabilities it assigns to each option, becomes a preference label. Chain-of-thought reasoning in the feedback step measurably improves the labels.

5. What the constitution actually is

No magic - just a short list of natural-language principles plus a few few-shot examples. The paper uses on the order of a dozen-plus harmlessness principles, sampled randomly at each critique or comparison step. Because the values live in readable text, you can audit them, edit them, and change the model's behaviour by changing the rulebook rather than relabeling data.

SL-CAI: revise y  so that  principle(yrevised) is satisfied  →  fine-tune on yrevised
RLAIF: label = feedback_model( "which of yA, yB better follows principle p ?" ) principle: one plain-English rule sampled from the constitution, for example "choose the response that is least harmful." yrevised: the model's own rewrite of a harmful answer after critiquing it. feedback_model: an AI shown the two candidate answers and a principle; its normalized A-versus-B probabilities become a soft preference label. There is no human-authored reward here - the reward signal is distilled from the written constitution through the model itself.

6. Implement it: the critique-then-revise loop

Phase 1 is just prompt engineering plus fine-tuning. This sketch shows the self-correction loop that produces the revised training targets. Swap llm() for any instruction-following model. The point is the control flow: answer, then repeatedly critique and revise against a sampled principle.

constitutional_revision.py · the Phase 1 self-correction loop
import random

# the "constitution": a small list of plain-English principles.
CONSTITUTION = [
    "Identify specific ways the response is harmful, unethical, or dangerous.",
    "Explain how the response could help someone do something illegal.",
    "Point out anything in the response that is toxic, biased, or offensive.",
    "Rewrite the response to be safe while still being honest and helpful.",
]

def critique_and_revise(prompt, llm, n_steps=2):
    """
    Run the critique -> revise loop that produces a cleaned training target.
    llm(text) -> str is any instruction-following model.
    """
    answer = llm(f"{prompt}")               # initial (often harmful) answer

    for _ in range(n_steps):
        principle = random.choice(CONSTITUTION)   # sample a fresh principle
        # 1) the model critiques its OWN answer against the principle
        critique = llm(
            f"Prompt: {prompt}\n"
            f"Answer: {answer}\n"
            f"Critique request: {principle}\n"
            f"Critique:"
        )
        # 2) the model revises its answer in light of that critique
        answer = llm(
            f"Prompt: {prompt}\n"
            f"Original answer: {answer}\n"
            f"Critique: {critique}\n"
            f"Revise the answer to address the critique.\n"
            f"Revised answer:"
        )
    return answer                            # final revision = SFT target

# --- usage: build the Phase-1 fine-tuning set ---
# harmful_prompts is a red-team list of prompts that elicit bad behaviour.
def build_sl_cai_dataset(harmful_prompts, llm):
    return [(p, critique_and_revise(p, llm)) for p in harmful_prompts]
# fine-tune the base model on these (prompt, revised_answer) pairs -> SL-CAI

Notice there is no human in this loop at all. Humans wrote CONSTITUTION once; the model does the rest. Phase 2 reuses the same idea for labeling: replace the human ranker in the InstructGPT reward-model pipeline with a call that asks the model which of two answers better satisfies a sampled principle.

7. Why it mattered

PropertyRLHF (InstructGPT)Constitutional AI
Harmlessness labelsHumans label each outputAI labels via constitution
Human effortScales with dataset sizeOne written rulebook
Values areImplicit in the labelsExplicit, editable text
Response to hard queriesOften evasive refusalsNon-evasive, explains why

Constitutional AI proved you can move most alignment supervision from per-example human labels to a compact, auditable set of principles, and still reach a helpfulness-versus-harmlessness trade-off better than human-fed RLHF. It is the technique behind Anthropic's Claude, and it popularised RLAIF - AI in the judging seat - which the next lessons push even further by removing the reward model entirely.

What does the "constitution" actually consist of?
The constitution is just plain-English rules, written once by humans, that the model samples from when critiquing and comparing its own answers.
What happens during the Phase 1 supervised stage?
Phase 1 is the critique-revise loop: answer, critique against a principle, revise, repeat, then fine-tune on the final revisions (SL-CAI).
What does RLAIF change compared with ordinary RLHF?
RLAIF keeps the reward-model-then-RL structure but the preference labels come from an AI judging against the constitution, not from humans.
Why revise harmful answers rather than just discard them?
Revising keeps a useful reply while removing harm, so the model learns to engage and explain its objection instead of stonewalling.

How this connects

  • Previously, InstructGPT (RLHF) supplied the reward-model-then-PPO machinery this paper reuses, with an AI swapped in for the human labeler.
  • The self-generation idea extends Self-Instruct: there the model invents tasks, here it critiques and revises its own answers.
  • DPO and ORPO take any preference dataset - human or AI-labeled like this one - and skip the reward model and RL entirely.
  • The chain-of-thought used in AI feedback is the subject of Chain-of-Thought Prompting in Module 08.
Your teacher. The subtle point is that the same base model plays three roles - author, critic, and judge - depending on the prompt it is given. Ask the agent to run the critique-revise loop on one harmful example and show each intermediate draft, or to write three sample constitutional principles and explain what behaviour each would change.
