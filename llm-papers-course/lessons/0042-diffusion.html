Diffusion Models

frontier 2020 16 min Ho, Jain, Abbeel · UC Berkeley · after Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, Ganguli (2015) · arXiv:2006.11239

The one-minute version

  • Add a little Gaussian noise to a real image, over and over, until nothing is left. That forward process is fixed, needs no learning, and has a closed form for any step.
  • Train one network to undo a single step. To generate, start from pure noise and run the learned reverse step many times. The hard task is decomposed rather than compressed, which is exactly what the VAE could not do.
  • The objective is "a weighted variational bound", so this is VAE mathematics. In code it reduces to something almost embarrassingly simple: predict the noise that was added, and take the mean squared error against it.
  • In 2026 this is the default generative model for every continuous modality - image, video, audio, 3D. The whole cost story is sampling: one output needs many forward passes, and every engineering advance since has been about reducing that number.

1. Why this paper exists

A VAE asks one network to produce a whole image from a short latent in a single shot. That is a hard mapping, and the loss that trains it settles for an average. A GAN removes the averaging but replaces it with a game that has no scalar to watch.

Diffusion changes the question. Instead of one enormous step from noise to image, take a thousand tiny steps, each of which asks only "make this slightly less noisy". Every individual task is easy, and every one is trained by a plain regression loss with a convergence curve you can look at.

The reframing Compression is not the only way to make generation tractable. Decomposition works too, and it buys you a stable loss, because each sub-task is a small supervised problem rather than one giant unsupervised leap.

2. The two processes

The forward process being fixed is what makes training cheap. To make one training example you take a real image, pick a random step number, jump straight to that noise level in one closed-form operation, and ask the network what noise you added. There is no simulation of the chain and no rollout.

3. The maths, read aloud

Two equations. The first says you can skip to any step directly. The second is the entire training loss.

xt = √(āt) · x0 + √(1 − āt) · ε,   ε ~ N(0, 1) x0: the real image. t: which step of the chain you want, chosen at random during training. āt (alpha-bar): a number between 1 and 0 fixed in advance by the noise schedule, close to 1 early in the chain and close to 0 at the end. √(āt) · x0: what survives of the original image. √(1 − āt) · ε: the noise mixed in, with ε drawn from a standard normal. Read it as: a weighted blend of the picture and pure noise, where the weights are decided by the schedule and nothing is learned.
L = Et, x0, ε ‖ ε − εθ(xt, t) ‖² ε: the noise you actually added, which you know because you drew it. εθ(xt, t): the network's guess at that noise, given the noisy image and the step number. ‖ · ‖²: squared error between the two. Et, x0, ε: averaged over random steps, random images and random noise draws. Read the whole line as: guess the noise, and be scored on how close the guess is.

That simple form is not the paper's starting point, and the connection is the reason this lesson sits after the VAE. DDPM's stated contribution is training on "a weighted variational bound designed according to a novel connection between diffusion probabilistic models and denoising score matching with Langevin dynamics". A weighted variational bound is the ELBO of Lesson 40, reweighted. The mean-squared-error line above is what that bound reduces to in practice.

4. Implement it

diffusion.py · runnable with torch, model is a stand-in
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

T = 1000
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)          # the noise schedule, fixed in advance
alphas = 1.0 - betas
abar = torch.cumprod(alphas, dim=0)            # alpha-bar: how much image survives

def add_noise(x0, t, eps):
    """Jump straight to step t in one operation - no chain simulation."""
    a = abar[t].view(-1, 1)
    return a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps

# a real model is a U-Net or a transformer - the shape is what matters here
DIM = 64
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(DIM + 1, 256), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(256, DIM))
def predict_noise(x, t):
    return model(torch.cat([x, (t.float() / T).view(-1, 1)], dim=1))

opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
x0 = torch.randn(32, DIM)                       # stand-in for a batch of real data

for step in range(200):                         # ---- training ----
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (len(x0),))         # a different step per example
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)                  # the noise we will ask it to name
    xt = add_noise(x0, t, eps)
    loss = ((eps - predict_noise(xt, t)) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

@torch.no_grad()                                # ---- sampling ----
def sample(n=4):
    x = torch.randn(n, DIM)                     # start from pure noise
    for i in reversed(range(T)):                # and walk the chain backwards
        t = torch.full((n,), i)
        eps_hat = predict_noise(x, t)
        mean = (x - betas[i] / (1 - abar[i]).sqrt() * eps_hat) / alphas[i].sqrt()
        x = mean + betas[i].sqrt() * torch.randn_like(x) if i > 0 else mean
    return x

print(sample().shape)                           # torch.Size([4, 64])

Read the two halves against each other. Training touches one random step per example and never runs the chain. Sampling runs the chain in full, once per generated output. That asymmetry - cheap training steps, expensive sampling - is the whole cost model of diffusion.

5. Conditioning, and what guidance_scale is

A generator nobody can steer is a curiosity. Conditioning gives the network the prompt as an extra input, but early results were not obedient enough, so a classifier was used to push samples towards a class. Classifier-free guidance removed the separate classifier: train one network to work both with and without the condition, then at sampling time push away from the unconditional prediction and towards the conditional one.

ε̂ = εθ(xt, ∅) + w · ( εθ(xt, c) − εθ(xt, ∅) ) εθ(xt, c): the noise prediction when the model is shown the condition c, such as the prompt. εθ(xt, ∅): the prediction when the condition is blanked out. Their difference: the direction the prompt is pulling in. w: the guidance weight, exposed in an image API as guidance_scale. ε̂: the prediction actually used for this step. Read it as: start from what the model would do with no prompt, then step further along the direction the prompt asks for than the model would go on its own.
Guidance is a blunt instrument and its cost is visible. Turning w up trades diversity for prompt adherence, and pushed far enough it produces oversaturated, samey images. Everything built after it - extra conditioning networks, adapters, regional prompting - exists because a single text string is a poor controller.

6. What it did not solve

Sampling cost is the whole downside. One output needs many forward passes, where a GAN needed one. Almost all applied diffusion engineering since has been the history of reducing that number: deterministic samplers, better numerical solvers, consistency models, and distillation.

Cost in pixel space is the second limit, and the next lesson is entirely about it. Editing and likelihoods are the third: a diffusion model does not hand you a clean likelihood for a given image, and editing a real photograph means inverting the sampler, which is approximate.

7. Does this still matter in 2026?

Yes. It is the default generative model for every continuous modality - image, video, audio, 3D - and there is no live competitor for photorealistic image and video synthesis. Two things a staff engineer should carry beyond that.

The architecture converged on transformers. DiT replaced the U-Net backbone with a Vision Transformer and reported that "DiTs with higher Gflops - through increased transformer depth/width or increased number of input tokens - consistently have lower FID", reaching an FID of 2.27 on ImageNet at 256 by 256. The practical consequence is that your intuitions about parameter count and compute now transfer from language models to image models unchanged. Later work moved the training path as well: SD3 uses rectified flow, which connects "data and noise in a straight line", on a two-stream architecture with "separate weights for the two modalities" and "a bidirectional flow of information between image and text tokens".

Diffusion has started moving into text. Shipped diffusion language models exist, and their pitch is generating tokens in parallel rather than one at a time, with reported throughput in the region of one to two thousand tokens per second on a single accelerator.

Treat those throughput figures as vendor claims rather than measurements. No independent benchmark of diffusion-language-model throughput was found while this lesson was written, so the durable point is directional: the autoregressive decoder is no longer the only option for text, and the reason to care is latency rather than quality.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which part of a diffusion model is learned?
The forward process is fixed in advance and has a closed form, which is what makes training examples free. Only the reverse step is a network.
What does the training loss actually ask the network for?
In implementation the weighted variational bound reduces to predicting the added noise under mean squared error. Predicting the clean image is a related but different parameterisation, and no explicit likelihood is optimised.
What does raising guidance_scale do?
Guidance amplifies the difference between the conditioned and unconditioned predictions, buying prompt adherence with diversity. Classifier-free guidance exists precisely so no separate classifier is needed.
Why is diffusion expensive at generation time?
Sampling walks the chain backwards, one forward pass per step. The forward process is never simulated, and guidance costing two passes per step is a detail on top of the real driver.

How this connects

  • The bound this is trained on is the one derived in Variational Autoencoders, reweighted.
  • Generative Adversarial Networks is the method it displaced, and the coverage argument there is the reason.
  • Latent Diffusion answers the pixel-space cost, and puts the CLIP text encoder in charge of conditioning.
  • Once the backbone is a transformer, the scaling arguments of Scaling Laws and the kernels of FlashAttention apply to image models too.
Your teacher. Ask the agent to plot abar against the step number, so you can see the schedule move an image from mostly-signal to mostly-noise. Then ask it to sample with the loop truncated to 50 steps instead of 1000 and compare - that gap, and how cheaply it can be closed, is the entire applied diffusion literature.
