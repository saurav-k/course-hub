The Pile

2020 Gao, Biderman, Black, Golding, Hoppe, Foster, Phang, and co-authors (EleutherAI). · arXiv:2101.00027

The one-minute version

  • The Pile is an 825 GiB English text dataset for training language models, assembled from 22 diverse, high-quality sub-datasets - PubMed, arXiv, GitHub, Stack Exchange, books, Wikipedia, and more.
  • Its thesis: data diversity matters, not just data quantity. A wide mix of domains teaches broader knowledge than scraping Common Crawl alone.
  • The sources are not mixed in raw proportion. High-quality sets are up-weighted (trained for more epochs) so a small but excellent corpus like Wikipedia contributes more than its raw size suggests.
  • Careful curation and de-duplication are treated as first-class levers - as important as model architecture. This paper helped make "what you train on" a serious research topic.
  • It became the default open pre-training corpus for a generation of open models (GPT-Neo, GPT-J, and others).

1. Why this paper exists

By 2020 the recipe for a strong language model was clear: a Transformer, scaling laws, and a mountain of text. But where does the text come from? The usual answer was Common Crawl - a raw scrape of the open web. It is enormous, but it is also messy, repetitive, and skewed toward whatever the internet happens to contain: SEO spam, boilerplate, low-effort forums.

The Pile's authors argued that what you feed a model shapes it as much as how big the model is. A model that never sees medical papers will be weak at medicine; one that never sees code will be weak at code. So instead of one giant homogeneous scrape, they deliberately assembled many curated domains and mixed them on purpose.

2. What is inside: 22 sources across many domains

The 22 components span academic, technical, conversational, and literary text. A few of the large ones:

  • Pile-CC - a quality-filtered slice of Common Crawl (kept, but cleaned).
  • PubMed Central - biomedical and life-sciences papers.
  • arXiv - physics, maths, and CS preprints (heavy in equations).
  • GitHub - open-source code across many languages.
  • Stack Exchange - question-and-answer threads.
  • Books3 / Bibliotik and Project Gutenberg - long-form books.
  • Wikipedia, FreeLaw, USPTO, PubMed Abstracts, HackerNews, and more.

3. Mixing weights: not everything counts equally

Here is the subtle part. You do not just concatenate the sources and train once through. Some corpora are small but extremely high quality (Wikipedia); others are huge but noisier (Pile-CC). To get the best model you up-weight the good stuff by training on it for more epochs - more passes - than its raw byte size would give.

So each source gets an epoch multiplier. Wikipedia might be seen ~3 times per training epoch while a bulk web source is seen once. The effective contribution of a source is its raw size times its weight, and those effective sizes are what determine the final data mix.

4. Curation as a pipeline: dedup and clean

Raw sources are full of duplicates - the same article mirrored on many sites, boilerplate repeated everywhere. Duplicates waste compute and can make a model memorise instead of generalise. So a real curation pipeline de-duplicates and filters before mixing. The paper treats this cleaning as a first-class step, not an afterthought.

5. Implement it: mixing weights, epochs, and a dedup sketch

The data recipe is arithmetic you can run yourself. Below we compute effective epochs-per-source from raw sizes and quality weights, turn them into sampling probabilities, then show a minimal de-duplication using content hashes. This is the shape of a real corpus-mixing script.

pile_mix.py · runnable Python (standard library only)
import hashlib
from collections import OrderedDict

# raw size (GiB) and a quality-driven epoch multiplier per source.
# Small, trusted sources get a multiplier > 1 (seen more than once).
sources = OrderedDict([
    #  name            raw_GiB   epoch_weight
    ("Pile-CC",        (227.0,   1.0)),
    ("PubMed-Central", ( 90.0,   2.0)),
    ("arXiv",          ( 56.0,   2.0)),
    ("GitHub",         ( 95.0,   1.0)),
    ("StackExchange",  ( 32.0,   2.0)),
    ("Wikipedia",      (  6.0,   3.0)),   # tiny but excellent -> up-weighted
    ("Gutenberg",      ( 10.0,   2.5)),
])

# 1. effective tokens = raw size * epoch weight (bytes ~ tokens for a sketch)
effective = {name: gib * w for name, (gib, w) in sources.items()}
total = sum(effective.values())

# 2. sampling probability = a source's share of the effective pool
print("source           epochs  sampling weight")
for name, (gib, w) in sources.items():
    prob = effective[name] / total
    print(f"{name:15s}  {w:4.1f}    {prob:6.3%}")

# 3. epochs-per-source over a full training run of E "pool passes"
E = 1.0                                   # one pass over the weighted pool
for name, (gib, w) in sources.items():
    # a source with weight w is traversed w times per pool pass
    print(f"{name:15s} seen {w * E:.1f} times per training run")

# ---- 4. de-duplication sketch: drop near-identical documents ----
def dedup(docs):
    """Keep the first copy of each document by content hash."""
    seen, kept = set(), []
    for d in docs:
        h = hashlib.sha256(d.strip().lower().encode()).hexdigest()
        if h not in seen:                  # unseen content -> keep
            seen.add(h)
            kept.append(d)
    return kept

raw = ["The otter swims.", "the otter swims.",   # duplicate after normalising
       "arXiv paper on scaling.", "The otter swims."]
print(f"before dedup: {len(raw)} docs -> after: {len(dedup(raw))} docs")

Run it and you see the two ideas in numbers: Wikipedia's 6 GiB, multiplied by 3 epochs, contributes far more than 6 GiB of Pile-CC would; and the dedup step collapses three copies of one sentence into one. Both are levers on final model quality that have nothing to do with the network architecture.

The reframing Before The Pile, "the dataset" was often an afterthought - grab Common Crawl and move on. After it, the data recipe - which sources, at what weights, cleaned how - became a research artefact you publish, measure, and iterate on. Later frontier models live or die on this exact craft.

6. Does diversity actually help?

The paper's evaluation is the payoff. Models trained on The Pile improve over models trained on raw Common Crawl or CC-100 across the board - including on domains The Pile adds that a plain web scrape lacks. Diversity is not decoration; it broadens what the model knows.

How this connects

  • Previously, GPT-3 trained on a largely web-scraped corpus. The Pile is the open community's answer: publish a better, more diverse recipe.
  • Scaling Laws treats data as a single number D. The Pile argues the composition behind that number is a lever in its own right.
  • Next, Chinchilla shows models need far more tokens than GPT-3 used - which makes a large, high-quality corpus like this even more valuable.
  • LLaMA and other open models lean heavily on curated public data in exactly the spirit The Pile pioneered.
Your teacher. The habit to build: when you read about any model, ask "what was it trained on, and in what proportions?" If the mixing-weight arithmetic is fuzzy, ask the agent to re-run pile_mix.py with different epoch weights and watch the sampling probabilities shift. Data curation is quietly one of the highest-leverage skills in the field.
