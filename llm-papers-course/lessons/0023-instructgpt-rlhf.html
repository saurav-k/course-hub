InstructGPT (RLHF)

2022 Ouyang, Wu, Jiang, Almeida, Wainwright, Mishkin, Zhang, Agarwal, Slama, Ray, Schulman, Hilton, Kelton, Miller, Simens, Askell, Welinder, Christiano, Leike, Lowe (OpenAI). · arXiv:2203.02155

The one-minute version

  • A raw language model only knows how to continue text. It is not trying to be helpful, honest, or safe - it just predicts the next token. Ask it a question and it might reply with more questions.
  • InstructGPT aligns the model to follow instructions using human feedback in three stages: SFT (copy human demonstrations), a reward model (learn human taste from rankings), and PPO (reinforcement learning to maximise that taste).
  • The reward model is trained on pairwise comparisons: humans rank several answers, and the model learns to score preferred answers higher.
  • PPO then pushes the policy toward high reward while a KL penalty keeps it close to the SFT model, so it does not drift into gibberish that games the reward.
  • The punchline: labelers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT over the 175B GPT-3 - 100x fewer parameters, better behaviour. Alignment beat raw scale.

1. Why this paper exists

GPT-3 (Lesson 6) was trained on one objective: predict the next token in web text. That makes a brilliant autocomplete, but not an assistant. The training objective and what a user actually wants are misaligned. Ask "Explain the moon landing to a six-year-old" and a raw model might continue with "Explain gravity to a six-year-old. Explain the sun to a six-year-old." - because that pattern is common in its training data. It is doing its job perfectly. Its job is just the wrong one.

The paper's fix is to add a second training phase that optimises for human preferences instead of raw likelihood. This is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, or RLHF. It is the recipe that turned GPT-3 into something you could actually talk to, and the direct ancestor of ChatGPT.

2. The three-stage pipeline

RLHF is not one step but three, run in order. Each stage produces an artefact the next stage needs. Read this diagram top to bottom - it is the whole paper in one picture.

3. Stage 1: Supervised fine-tuning

The simplest stage. OpenAI collected prompts (many from real API traffic) and paid trained labelers to write high-quality answers - the demonstrations. Then GPT-3 is fine-tuned with ordinary supervised learning to imitate those answers: the same next-token loss as pretraining, but now on curated (prompt, ideal answer) pairs. The result is the SFT model. It already follows instructions reasonably well, and it is the foundation for everything after.

4. Stage 2: The reward model learns human taste

Writing a perfect answer is hard. Judging which of two answers is better is easy. Stage 2 exploits that asymmetry. For a prompt, the SFT model samples several candidate answers, and a labeler ranks them from best to worst. From K ranked answers you get every pairwise comparison - C(K, 2) of them. In the paper K ranged from 4 to 9, giving 6 to 36 comparisons per prompt from a single labeling session.

A reward model (a copy of the model with the token head replaced by a single scalar output) is trained so that, for every compared pair, the winner scores higher than the loser.

The reward-model loss, exactly

The loss is a Bradley-Terry pairwise ranking loss. It says: the difference in reward between winner and loser, squashed through a sigmoid, should be as close to 1 as possible.

loss(θ) = − ( 1 / C(K,2) ) · E(x, yw, yl) ~ D [ log σ( rθ(x, yw) − rθ(x, yl) ) ] rθ(x, y): the scalar reward the model gives answer y for prompt x. yw: the winning (human-preferred) answer; yl: the losing one. r(x, yw) − r(x, yl): how much the model prefers the winner - we want this positive and large. σ: the sigmoid, turning that gap into a probability the winner beats the loser. log σ(·): reward the model when that probability is near 1. 1 / C(K,2): average over all pairs from one prompt so a prompt with more answers does not dominate. E...~D: average over the whole comparison dataset.
Why pairwise, not a score Ask a human "rate this answer 0 to 10" and you get noisy, drifting numbers. Ask "which of these two is better" and you get a clean, consistent signal. Bradley-Terry converts many such binary judgements into a single continuous reward function. It is the same math behind Elo chess ratings.

5. Stage 3: PPO optimises the policy against the reward

Now the reinforcement learning. The policy (initialised from the SFT model) generates an answer, the frozen reward model scores it, and PPO - Proximal Policy Optimization, Schulman et al. 2017 - nudges the policy to make high-reward answers more likely. Left alone, this goes wrong: the policy discovers weird text that the reward model over-scores, and quality collapses. This is reward hacking.

The fix is a KL penalty. Every token, the objective subtracts a penalty proportional to how far the RL policy's probabilities have drifted from the SFT model's. The policy is free to improve, but on a leash.

The PPO objective, exactly

The paper maximises this objective (their Equation 2). Two pieces matter: the reward-minus-KL term, and an optional pretraining-mix term that stops the model forgetting general skills.

objective(φ) = E(x,y) ~ πφRL [ rθ(x, y) − β · log( πφRL(y|x) / πSFT(y|x) ) ] + γ · Ex ~ Dpretrain [ log πφRL(x) ] πφRL: the policy being trained (parameters φ). rθ(x, y): the frozen reward model's score for the answer it just generated - the thing we want to raise. β · log(πRL/πSFT): the per-token KL penalty; it grows when the RL policy assigns very different probabilities than the SFT model, and β sets how tight the leash is. γ · EDpretrain[log πRL]: the pretraining mix - keep scoring well on ordinary pretraining text so RL does not degrade general ability (this variant is "PPO-ptx"; set γ = 0 for plain PPO).

6. Implement it: the two objectives in code

The two formulas above are short in PyTorch. First the reward-model pairwise loss, then a simplified sketch of the PPO reward-minus-KL objective. These are teaching versions - a real PPO loop adds clipping, advantages, and a value head - but the core signals are exactly these.

reward_model_loss.py · the pairwise ranking loss
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def reward_model_loss(reward_win, reward_lose):
    """
    reward_win : (B,) scalar reward the RM gave the human-preferred answer
    reward_lose: (B,) scalar reward the RM gave the rejected answer
    Bradley-Terry: maximise log-sigmoid of (winner - loser).
    """
    # want reward_win - reward_lose to be large and positive
    return -F.logsigmoid(reward_win - reward_lose).mean()

# --- smoke test ---
# imagine a batch of 4 comparisons. The RM currently scores them like this:
r_win  = torch.tensor([2.0, 0.5, -1.0, 3.0])   # preferred answers
r_lose = torch.tensor([1.0, 1.5,  0.0, 1.0])   # rejected answers
loss = reward_model_loss(r_win, r_lose)
print(float(loss))     # positive; drops toward 0 as winners outscore losers
# note: comparison 2 (0.5 vs 1.5) is currently WRONG - the loss punishes it most
ppo_objective_sketch.py · reward minus KL, per token
import torch

def ppo_reward_with_kl(logp_rl, logp_sft, reward, beta=0.02):
    """
    logp_rl : (B, T) log-prob the RL policy gave each generated token
    logp_sft: (B, T) log-prob the frozen SFT model gave the SAME tokens
    reward  : (B,)   scalar RM reward for the whole answer
    beta    : strength of the KL leash to the SFT model
    Returns a per-sequence score PPO tries to MAXIMISE.
    """
    # per-token KL estimate: how far the RL policy drifted from SFT.
    # log(pi_RL / pi_SFT) = logp_rl - logp_sft
    per_token_kl = logp_rl - logp_sft            # (B, T)
    kl_penalty   = beta * per_token_kl.sum(dim=-1)   # sum over the answer

    # the RM reward lands on the full sequence; subtract the KL leash
    score = reward - kl_penalty                   # (B,)
    return score.mean()

# --- smoke test ---
B, T = 2, 5
logp_rl  = torch.log(torch.rand(B, T))           # pretend log-probs
logp_sft = torch.log(torch.rand(B, T))
reward   = torch.tensor([1.2, -0.3])             # RM scores
print(float(ppo_reward_with_kl(logp_rl, logp_sft, reward)))
# high reward pushes score up; drifting far from SFT (big KL) pushes it down

Read the two together and the whole method is visible: the reward model turns human rankings into reward, and the PPO objective spends that reward while paying a KL tax for every step away from the trusted SFT model. Balance is everything.

7. Why it mattered

The headline number reframed the field: a 1.3B InstructGPT was preferred to the 175B GPT-3, 100x larger. At the 175B scale, InstructGPT outputs were preferred to GPT-3 outputs about 85% of the time. Alignment, not just scale, was suddenly a first-class lever. Every stage here - SFT, reward model, PPO with KL - is what the next few lessons streamline or replace.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What data does the reward model actually train on?
Humans rank several answers per prompt; each ranking yields C(K,2) pairwise winner-loser pairs, and the RM learns to score winners higher.
What is the KL penalty in the PPO objective for?
Without the KL leash the policy drifts into strange text that fools the reward model. The penalty anchors it to the trusted SFT model.
Why is judging answers easier for humans than writing them?
The paper exploits this asymmetry: comparisons are quick and consistent, so they scale the human signal far better than demonstrations.
What was the paper's headline result about size?
A model 100x smaller won on human preference once aligned. Alignment, not just parameter count, drives usefulness.

Your teacher. The confusing part is that three models are in play at once: the reward model, the RL policy, and the frozen SFT reference. Ask the agent to trace one prompt through all three and show where each number goes, or to demonstrate reward hacking by setting β = 0 in the PPO sketch. Do not move on until "reward pulls, KL holds back" feels obvious.
