Module 09 · Caching · Lesson 0904

Every invalidation strategy buys a staleness window, and each one loses a different race

working 11 min The second half of the hard problem, and the one with a race in it. · Nishtala et al., Scaling Memcache at Facebook (NSDI 2013)

The one-minute version

  • Only three things ever remove a wrong entry: a lifetime running out, a write removing it, or a write replacing it. Everything else is one of those three with delivery attached.
  • A lifetime is reliable and slow: it needs no message, so nothing can lose it, and its window is up to the whole lifetime.
  • A removal is fast and unreliable: its window is a few milliseconds when the message arrives and unbounded when it does not.
  • The race neither of them wins is the stale set - a reader that missed, queried, and is then descheduled can store the old value after the write removed it. Correct ordering does not close it.
  • What it costs: closing the race needs coordination on the miss path - a lease, a compare-and-set - and the alternative to closing it is a lifetime short enough to bound the damage you accepted.

1. The mental model: reliability and immediacy are different purchases

Three mechanisms, and what each one needs to work

A lifetime removes an entry with no message from anybody. That is its entire advantage: there is no delivery to fail, no consumer to be down, no ordering to get wrong, and no code that can forget. It works when the process that wrote the entry has since been replaced, when the invalidation daemon is broken, and when nobody remembers the cache exists. Its cost is that it is indifferent to your writes - the entry stays wrong for the rest of its lifetime whether the row changed a second ago or a week ago.

A removal on write is the opposite purchase. It responds to the actual change, so the window shrinks to the delivery delay - and it is a message, so it can be lost, delayed, reordered, or sent to a cache that has since restarted. AWS's guidance recommends running both, and states why in one line: a lifetime added to every write "doesn't guarantee that a value isn't stale. However, it keeps data from getting too stale". That is the correct framing. The lifetime is not the cheap version of invalidation; it is the bound on how badly invalidation can fail.

What the two are worth, arithmetically

The two combine into one expression, and writing it down is what makes the argument for keeping both instead of choosing.

mean staleness = (1 − p) × d + p × (T ÷ 2) Read as: on average an entry is wrong for the delivery delay d of a removal that arrives, which happens a 1 − p share of the time, plus the lifetime's own contribution T ÷ 2 on the p share of writes whose removal never lands. T is the entry's lifetime and p is the probability the removal is lost.
  1. Assume how the writes land. Take a write arriving at a uniformly random moment during an entry's life, so with no removal at all the entry is wrong for half a lifetime on average. That uniformity is an assumption, not a measurement, and every number below rests on it.
  2. Read the lifetime on its own. Every removal is lost, so p = 1 and the expression collapses to T ÷ 2. At a five-minute lifetime that is 150 seconds of mean staleness.
  3. Add a removal. Take d = 5 ms and p = 0.001, both illustrative. Then (0.999 × 0.005) + (0.001 × 150) = 0.005 + 0.15 = 0.155 s.
  4. Take the ratio. 150 ÷ 0.155 is about 970 times less staleness for one delete call on the write path.
  5. Now remove the lifetime. With no T the second term has nothing to multiply, and the one write in a thousand whose removal was lost leaves an entry that is wrong permanently, with no mechanism anywhere that will ever notice.

2. The mechanism: the race that both of them lose

The stale set, in four messages

Lesson 0902 established the ordering rule - store first, cache second - and warned that it narrows a race without closing it. This is that race. It does not need the writer to do anything wrong; it needs a reader to be slow between two of its own steps, which every reader occasionally is. The memcache paper names the outcome: "A stale set occurs when a web server sets a value in memcache that does not reflect the latest value that should be cached", and attributes it to concurrent updates being reordered.

Closing it: the miss has to be claimed

Closing the race means giving the cache a way to know that the value being stored was computed before something invalidated it. Facebook's memcache does it with a lease: on a miss the cache hands the client "a 64-bit token bound to the specific key the client originally requested", the client presents that token when it sets, and the cache refuses the set if the token has been invalidated by a delete in the meantime. The paper compares it to load-link and store-conditional, which is the right mental model - the set is conditional on nothing having happened to the key since the miss.

Where the invalidation should come from

One more failure sits between the write and the removal, and it is the reason large systems do not send invalidations from application code. A service that commits its transaction and then crashes before issuing the delete has produced a durable change and a lost invalidation, and no retry survives the process that was going to do the retrying. Facebook's answer is to derive the invalidation from the same durable record as the write: daemons on each database inspect the committed SQL, extract the deletes that were amended into it, and broadcast them, which also lets them batch - the paper reports an 18× improvement in deletes per packet from batching alone.

The pattern generalises to any service with a database and is worth naming, because it is the same shape as a transactional outbox: the invalidation is written inside the transaction that made the change, so it is impossible to have one without the other, and a separate process delivers it with retries that outlive any single request. Module 12 develops the delivery half of that mechanism; the part that belongs here is the rule that an invalidation issued outside the transaction can be lost independently of the write it describes.

3. The trade-off: you are choosing which guarantee to give up

Line the three mechanisms up against what each one needs and the choice stops being a matter of taste. A lifetime needs nothing and gives you a bound. A removal needs delivery and gives you immediacy. A lease or a compare-and-set needs coordination on every miss and gives you correctness under concurrency. No single mechanism gives all three, and the reason is not implementation quality: an entry stored by a process that cannot see the future is going to be wrong occasionally, and the only questions are for how long and who finds out.

What a design owes its reader, then, is the number. Memcache states one for its own cluster-warming mode - deletes carry a two-second hold-off, and the paper says plainly that "the operational benefits of cold cluster warmup far outweigh the cost of rare cache consistency issues". That is a staleness window written down, argued for, and accepted. The alternative is not a system with no window; it is a system whose window is whatever the last person to set a lifetime happened to type.

The strongest guarantee a cache-aside design can offer without leases is read-after-write for the writer's own request, and it is worth building deliberately because users notice its absence immediately - they saved a form and the page still shows the old value. Memcache does exactly this: a web server that modifies data "also sends invalidations to its own cluster to provide read-after-write semantics for a single user request". In a smaller system it means the request that performed the write skips the cache on its subsequent read, or reads through to the store, for the rest of that request. It does nothing for any other reader, and saying so out loud is part of the design rather than an admission.

The read path is the culprit: a reader that missed, queried and then stalled writes back a value the writer has already superseded, and no ordering the writer can adopt prevents it.
