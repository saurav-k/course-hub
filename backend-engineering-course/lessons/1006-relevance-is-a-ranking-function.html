Module 10 · Async work and search · Lesson 1006

Matching gives you a set and ranking gives you an order, and only one of them is what a reader sees

working 10 min The module closes on the part of search you can actually read. · Lucene - BM25Similarity

The one-minute version

  • Lesson 1005's index answers a boolean question. Which of ten thousand matches goes first is a different question, and it has no objectively right answer - only a model.
  • BM25 is that model, and it is legible: a term's contribution rises with how rare the term is, rises with how often it occurs, and falls with how long the document is.
  • The rise with frequency saturates. Lucene's k1 "controls non-linear term frequency normalization (saturation)" and defaults to 1.2, which puts a hard ceiling on what repetition can buy.
  • b "controls to what degree document length normalizes tf values" and defaults to 0.75 - three quarters of the way towards full length normalisation.
  • PostgreSQL's ts_rank is a different model, not a weaker one: it ranks on the frequency of matching lexemes within the document you hand it, and ranking "requires consulting the tsvector of each matching document", which is where its cost lives.

1. The mental model: two questions, asked in order

The set is objective and the order is not

Ask whether a document contains the lexeme refund and there is a fact of the matter. The index knows it, the answer does not change with taste, and two implementations that disagree have a bug. Ask which of four hundred documents containing refund should be first and there is no fact at all - there is a claim about what a person wanted, inferred from the words they typed and from statistics about the corpus.

That is why the two are separate stages rather than one clever query. The match narrows a million documents to four hundred using structure alone, which is cheap and exact. The rank then scores those four hundred, which is expensive and approximate, and the expense is bounded by the match having already thrown away everything else. Getting the stages the wrong way round - scoring first, filtering after - is how a search implementation becomes slow in a way that no index will rescue.

2. The mechanism: three intuitions, one expression

Repetition helps, and then it stops helping

Start with the intuition that carries the most weight in practice. A document that mentions refund five times is more likely to be about refunds than one that mentions it once. A document that mentions it fifty times is not ten times better than the one that mentions it five - it is probably the same kind of document, or it is a page trying to be found. So the contribution of term frequency must rise and then flatten, and the flattening is not a patch bolted on, it is the shape of the curve.

Two more intuitions complete the picture. A term that appears in almost every document tells you almost nothing about which one to read, so a term's weight should rise as it gets rarer across the corpus - that is the inverse document frequency. And a long document contains any given word more easily than a short one, by sheer length rather than by being about it, so the frequency should be judged against the document's length relative to the average. Put the three together and the standard expression follows.

score(D, Q) = ∑q in Q IDF(q) × ( f(q,D) × (k1 + 1) ) ÷ ( f(q,D) + k1 × (1 − b + b × dl ÷ avgdl) ) Read as: D is one candidate document and Q is the query, and the score is a sum over each query term q. f(q,D) is how many times q occurs in D. IDF(q) is how rare q is across the whole corpus, so a word in every document contributes little. dl is the length of D in words and avgdl is the average length across the corpus, so dl ÷ avgdl is above one for a long document. k1 sets how fast the frequency term saturates and b sets how much the length ratio is allowed to matter.

The two constants, and what a default means

Both are stated in the same place, in the same words, by Lucene and by Elasticsearch. Lucene's BM25Similarity documents k1 as "Controls non-linear term frequency normalization (saturation)" and b as "Controls to what degree document length normalizes tf values", with defaults of 1.2 and 0.75, and Elasticsearch repeats both descriptions and both defaults for its own default similarity. Reading b against the formula makes it concrete: at b = 0 the length ratio disappears entirely and only raw frequency counts, at b = 1 the frequency is fully divided by relative length, and 0.75 sits three quarters of the way along.

Neither number is a law. They are the values that worked well across the TREC collections where BM25 was developed - Lucene names the origin, Robertson and colleagues' Okapi work at TREC-3 - and they are a starting point for a corpus that resembles those. A corpus of product titles, where every document is eight words long, is not that corpus, and length normalisation is doing something quite different there than it does over news articles.

PostgreSQL ranks too, and it ranks differently

If your search lives in the database, the ranking functions are ts_rank and ts_rank_cd. The first "ranks vectors based on the frequency of their matching lexemes"; the second computes a cover density ranking, which is similar except that the proximity of matching lexemes to each other is taken into account - useful when a two-word query should prefer documents where the words appear together.

Two differences from BM25 matter when you choose. First, the arguments are a document vector and a query, so there is no corpus-wide term statistic in the call at all: a rare word and a common one are not separated the way an IDF factor separates them, and if you need that distinction you build it yourself. Second, length normalisation is an explicit option rather than a fitted constant - a bit mask where 0 ignores document length, 1 divides by one plus the logarithm of the length, and 2 divides by the length, among others. What you do get for free is weights: four labels with a default of {0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 1.0}, which is how a title match is made to count for more than a body match.

3. The trade-off: ranking costs, and it cannot be measured by looking

The cost is stated bluntly in the documentation and applies to every implementation, not only PostgreSQL's: "Ranking can be expensive since it requires consulting the tsvector of each matching document, which can be I/O bound and therefore slow. Unfortunately, it is almost impossible to avoid since practical queries often result in large numbers of matches." Matching is set arithmetic over compact lists; ranking has to open every match. That is the argument for narrowing hard before scoring, and for treating a ranked result page as an expensive object that module 09 would happily cache.

The harder cost is that you cannot tell whether your ranking is good by reading it. Every change to k1, to b, to a field weight, to the stemmer, moves results for every query at once, and the engineer making the change is not a representative user searching for a representative thing. Relevance is measured with judged queries - a set of searches with known good answers, scored before and after - and a team that changes ranking without one is tuning by anecdote. That measurement is a small piece of work and it is the difference between a search that improves and one that oscillates.

BM25 knows nothing about meaning. Every term in it is a count, so a document that says "reimbursement" is invisible to a search for "refund" no matter how well it answers the question, and the model cannot tell you it missed - it returns the documents it can see, ranked confidently. Synonym expansion, controlled vocabularies and vector retrieval all exist to attack that gap, and all of them add a second failure surface. Know which gap you have before you buy a mechanism: a ranking that puts the wrong matched document first is a scoring problem, and one that never matched the right document at all is a vocabulary problem, and they have no fix in common.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does raising k1 from 1.2 to 3.0 do to the ranking?
k1 controls how quickly the term-frequency contribution saturates, so a larger value pushes the ceiling up and flattens the curve later - repetition buys more before it stops paying. Rarity is the IDF factor and is untouched by k1. Length is b's department. And nothing in the scoring function changes which documents matched, because the set was fixed before any score was computed.
Why is ranking expensive in a way that matching is not?
Matching is arithmetic over compact postings lists, while ranking consults the vector of each matching document, which the documentation names as potentially I/O bound and almost impossible to avoid. Sorting a few hundred scores is trivial by comparison. Scores are computed per query rather than stored, since they depend on the query. And rarity is a statistic the index already holds, needing no extra pass.

How this connects

  • Ahead, Module 11 is where the judged-query set belongs beside your other measurements, because a relevance change with no measurement is an outage you cannot see.
  • Behind you, search is an inverted index produced the set this page orders, and its analyser decides which terms exist to be counted at all.
  • Behind you, collections need pagination is what a ranked result set of four hundred documents still owes its caller, and a cursor over it needs the order to be stable.
Your teacher. Write ten searches a real user of your product would type, and the document that should come first for each. Run them and count how many are right. That number is your baseline, and until you have it, every ranking change you make is unfalsifiable. Then ask the agent which of the ten failures are scoring problems and which are vocabulary problems.

Primary source to go deeper

Lucene's BM25Similarity is the shortest authoritative statement of what k1 and b control and what they default to, and it names the Okapi TREC-3 work the function comes from. Elasticsearch's similarity settings confirm both defaults for the engine most readers will meet it in, and PostgreSQL's ranking controls give the alternative model, its weights, its normalisation flags and its cost warning. For the derivation itself, Robertson and Zaragoza's The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond (Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval, 2009) is the standard treatment.
