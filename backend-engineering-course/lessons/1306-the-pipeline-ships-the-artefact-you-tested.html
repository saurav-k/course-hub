Build once, and everything after it moves the same bytes

frontier 9 min The closing page of the module, and the one place every earlier decision is asserted together. · The twelve-factor app - build, release, run

The one-minute version

  • A pipeline's job is carrying one artefact's identity from a commit to a running process, so that what serves traffic is what passed the gate.
  • Three stages, strictly separated: build turns a commit into a bundle, release combines it with this environment's config, run executes a release.
  • A release is immutable and gets a unique identifier. Rebuilding on the way to production breaks the chain and ships bytes nothing tested.
  • Continuous delivery means the software is releasable at any time. Continuous deployment means every change actually goes, and needs the first to exist.
  • What it costs: the pipeline is now the riskiest system you own, and the research says speed and stability move together rather than trading off.

1. The mental model: an identity, carried

Build, release, run, and why the wall matters

The fifth factor divides the path into three stages and insists they stay divided. The build stage "converts a code repo into an executable bundle", fetching dependencies and compiling. The release stage "takes the build produced by the build stage and combines it with the deploy's current config", producing something ready to execute. The run stage launches processes against a selected release. The rule attached to them is absolute: "it is impossible to make changes to the code at runtime, since there is no way to propagate those changes back to the build stage".

Two properties of a release do the work. Each one has a unique identifier, and "releases are an append-only ledger and a release cannot be mutated once it is created. Any change must create a new release." That gives a deployment a name that can be pointed at afterwards, which is what makes a rollback a selection rather than a rebuild, and lesson 1304's revision history is the same idea one layer down.

2. The mechanism: what a run actually does

Two disciplines that are often confused

The words are not interchangeable. Continuous delivery is "a software development discipline where you build software in such a way that the software can be released to production at any time", with four tests: the software is deployable throughout its lifecycle, the team prioritises keeping it deployable over new features, anybody can get fast automated feedback on production readiness, and you can perform push-button deployments of any version to any environment on demand. Continuous deployment is the stricter thing: "every change goes through the pipeline and automatically gets put into production". The relation between them is one-directional, and stated as such: to do continuous deployment you must be doing continuous delivery.

Notice what the four tests are about. Three of them are properties of the team and the codebase rather than of the tooling, which is why a pipeline installed on a codebase that is not kept deployable produces a fast way to ship an unreleasable thing.

Rollout strategies differ only in who is exposed

The rolling update of lesson 1304 replaces old pods with new ones inside a stated envelope, and every user is served by the new version as soon as their request lands on one. A canary sends a small share of traffic to the new version first, so a defect is discovered by a bounded population rather than by everyone. A blue-green deployment runs the two versions side by side and switches the balancer, which makes the reversal instant at the cost of running two fleets. All three make the same trade in different currencies: exposure against the cost of standing capacity, and none of them detects anything by itself. Each one needs the health signal the pipeline is watching.

3. The frontier: is the approval gate buying anything

The intuition is strong and nearly universal: shipping less often, with a human approving each release, must be safer. DORA's research programme measures four things against that intuition. Change lead time, the time from commit to running in production. Deployment frequency. Change fail rate, the share of deployments needing immediate intervention. Failed deployment recovery time. Two of those are throughput and two are stability, and the finding is that they do not oppose each other: "DORA's research has repeatedly demonstrated that speed and stability are not tradeoffs", and "the real trade-off, over long periods of time, is between better software faster and worse software slower".

The mechanism is plausible once the pipeline is drawn. A small change deployed alone is easy to reason about, easy to attribute when something breaks, and cheap to reverse. A quarterly release is dozens of changes whose interactions nobody has seen, arriving together, with a rollback that undoes work nobody wanted undone. The approval gate does not inspect any of that. It delays it, and the delay makes the batch bigger.

Hold the finding at its real strength, though. This is observational research across many organisations, so what it establishes is that the four move together in practice, not that automating a release causes safety in yours. What it does refuse is the excuse: a team shipping rarely and breaking often cannot explain the second by the first.

A pipeline with production credentials is a service that can change every other service, and it is usually the least reviewed code in the organisation. Treat it as production: the build should not run untrusted code with deploy credentials attached, a dependency pulled at build time is code you are about to sign, and the ability to bypass the gate should be as visible as using it. The failure here is not a bad deploy, it is a supply chain.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A pipeline builds an image for staging and rebuilds from the same commit for production. What breaks?
Two builds of one commit are two artefacts, with their own base layers and their own resolved dependencies, so the gate examined the other one. The commit is still recorded, the cache cost is real but is not a correctness problem, and configuration is exactly what the release stage is supposed to add per environment.
What does DORA's research say about deploying more often and breaking things less often?
The repeated finding is that the throughput and stability metrics move together, which the programme states as speed and stability not being a trade-off. No precondition on failure rate is claimed, the metrics are explicitly related rather than independent, and batching behind an approval is the intuition the research contradicts.
