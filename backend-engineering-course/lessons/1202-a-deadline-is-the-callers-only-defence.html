A deadline is the caller's only defence, and it is worth nothing unless it travels

working 9 min The failure model of the synchronous branch. · gRPC - Deadlines

The one-minute version

  • A deadline is a point in time and a timeout is a duration. gRPC sets neither by default, so an unbounded client "can end up waiting for a response effectively forever".
  • An unbounded wait is not a slow request, it is a held resource, and enough of them is your service falling over because somebody else's did.
  • A deadline must propagate down the chain. gRPC sends it as a remaining timeout with the elapsed time already deducted, so no two machines have to agree on the clock.
  • When it expires the two ends disagree: client and server "make independent and local determinations of the success of the call, and their conclusions may not match".
  • What it costs: a retry after a deadline is a second attempt at work that may already have happened, and retries at every hop multiply.

1. The mental model: waiting is a resource you are spending

The instinct is to treat a slow dependency as a latency problem. It is not. While a request waits it is holding a goroutine or a thread, an open connection, a slot in a pool and whatever memory the half-finished work occupies. A dependency that has stopped answering does not make your service slow, it makes your service accumulate held requests until the pool is empty, at which point requests that need nothing from that dependency also start failing. The blast radius of one unavailable service becomes every service that waits on it.

The bound on that is a deadline, and gRPC is explicit that you do not get one for free: "by default, gRPC does not set a deadline which means it is possible for a client to end up waiting for a response effectively forever". The same is true of most HTTP clients. A default that is infinite is a default that only shows up in an incident.

The vocabulary is worth getting right because the two words are used interchangeably and mean different things. A deadline is an absolute point in time past which the caller is unwilling to wait. A timeout is a duration, converted to a deadline by adding it to now. The distinction matters the moment a call becomes a chain.

2. The mechanism: the budget travels, the clock does not

Propagation

A gateway that gives itself 500 milliseconds and then calls a service that gives itself 500 milliseconds has not set a bound at all - it has set two, and the inner one can outlive the outer. What is needed is one budget, spent as the request travels, so that a service asked to do work nobody is waiting for any more can stop instead of finishing.

gRPC does this by propagating the original deadline to outgoing calls, and it sends it as a timeout rather than as a timestamp, "converting the deadline to a timeout from which the already elapsed time is already deducted". That detail is the interesting one: two machines never have to agree on what time it is, only on how long is left, so clock skew cannot corrupt the budget.

The two ends disagree

Cancellation is available to both sides and takes effect immediately, and the gRPC documentation attaches a sentence to it that is easy to read past: "changes made before a cancellation are not rolled back". A cancelled call is not an undone call. The server may be most of the way through a database write when the client walks away.

This generalises into the property that makes the whole synchronous branch hard. Client and server "make independent and local determinations of the success of the call, and their conclusions may not match". The status list says the same thing about the specific case: DEADLINE_EXCEEDED "may be returned even if the operation has completed successfully". You have met this before under a different name - lesson 0605 called it the ambiguous failure, and the resolution is the same one.

3. The trade-off: retries make failures bigger before they make them smaller

A deadline turns a hang into an error, and the obvious response to an error is to try again. Some of those retries are correct: gRPC says UNAVAILABLE "is most likely a transient condition, which can be corrected by retrying with a backoff", and immediately adds that "it is not always safe to retry non-idempotent operations". Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing.

The half that gets left out of designs is what happens when every layer retries. Retries compose multiplicatively, and the deepest service is where the product lands.

  1. Set the policy. Each hop makes up to three attempts, which is one try plus two retries, and the chain is three hops deep.
  2. Count what the deepest service sees. One user request becomes 3 calls at the first hop, 3 × 3 = 9 at the second and 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 at the third.
  3. Read the failure mode. The retries fire precisely when a service is already struggling, so the policy meant to survive a wobble delivers 27 times the load into it. That is the assumption to check: retries help against an isolated failure and harm against an overloaded one.
A deadline that is never reached teaches you nothing, and one set to a round number nobody measured is usually either. The gRPC guidance is to start from "an educated guess based on what you know about your system - network latency, server processing time - validated by some load testing", and then to watch how often it fires. A deadline that never expires is not protecting anything, and one that expires under normal load is generating the ambiguous failures of the section above for no reason.

Why does gRPC propagate a deadline as a remaining timeout rather than as a timestamp?
Sending what is left rather than when to stop means clock skew between the two hosts cannot corrupt the budget, and the elapsed time is deducted before the value is sent. The second option describes the opposite of the design, and the first and fourth invent constraints the protocol does not have.
Your client sees DEADLINE_EXCEEDED on a charge. What do you know about the charge?
The status is a statement about the waiting, not about the work, and it "may be returned even if the operation has completed successfully". The second reads the client's own local conclusion as the server's; the third assumes a rollback the documentation explicitly denies; and the fourth is as wrong as the second in the other direction.
