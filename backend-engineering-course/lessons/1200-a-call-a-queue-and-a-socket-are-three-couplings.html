Module 12 · Inter-service communication · Lesson 1200

A call, a queue and a socket are three answers to one question: what has to be true at the same instant?

foundation 9 min The frame the rest of this module fills in. · Apache Kafka - Introduction

The one-minute version

  • The three transports of this module differ in temporal coupling: whether both parties have to be running at the same moment for the work to happen.
  • A synchronous call is tightly coupled in time. The callee must be up, and its unavailability becomes your unavailability, multiplied along the chain.
  • A broker is decoupled in time. Kafka states it plainly: events "are not deleted after consumption", so a consumer that is down is late rather than lost.
  • A persistent socket is the case where the server needs to speak first, which neither of the other two can do.
  • What it costs: decoupling in time means you cannot get an answer, and every broker delivers at least once, so the receiver must tolerate a repeat.

1. The mental model: three couplings, not three technologies

Engineers usually pick a transport by naming a product: gRPC because the team already writes protobuf, Kafka because it is on the platform, a WebSocket because the front end wants live updates. That is choosing by availability of the tool rather than by shape of the problem, and it is why services end up with a queue in front of work that needed an answer, and a synchronous call in front of work that nobody was waiting for.

There are only three questions, and the answers pick the transport for you. Does the caller need the result to continue? If yes, something has to wait, and waiting is a synchronous call. Must both parties be running at the same moment? If no, something has to hold the message in the meantime, and holding it is a broker. Does the receiving side ever need to speak without being asked? If yes, a connection has to stay open, and that is a socket.

2. The mechanism: what each one does with an outage

A synchronous call multiplies unavailability

When service A calls service B and waits, A is available only when B is. That is not a metaphor, it is multiplication, and it is the single most useful piece of arithmetic in this module. Give every service in a chain the same independent availability and the chain's availability is that number raised to the length of the chain.

  1. Start from one service. At 99.9% available, a 30-day month of 43,200 minutes carries 0.001 × 43,200 = 43.2 minutes of downtime.
  2. Chain five of them. 0.9995 = 0.99501, so the unavailability is 0.00499 and the downtime is 0.00499 × 43,200 = 215.6 minutes.
  3. Read what changed. Nothing got less reliable. The same five services, each meeting the same target, now fail the caller five times as often, purely because the caller waits on all of them.

A broker turns an outage into a backlog

Put a broker between the two and the arithmetic stops applying, because the sender never waits on the receiver. The sender's success now depends on the broker rather than on the consumer, and the consumer's being down changes the size of a backlog rather than the outcome of a request. Kafka states the enabling property directly: events "are not deleted after consumption", and how long they are kept is a per-topic retention setting rather than a consequence of anybody reading them.

That is a genuine change of failure mode, not a smaller failure. An unavailable consumer used to produce errors your caller saw; now it produces a queue that grows, and the symptom is latency followed, eventually, by whatever your broker does when it runs out of room or out of retention. You have moved the failure to a place where you have time to react, which is the whole benefit and the whole limit.

A socket is the only one where the server may speak first

Both arrangements above are started by the sender. Neither can deliver something the client did not ask for, which is why a browser showing live prices used to poll: RFC 6455 §1.1 was written because bidirectional traffic over HTTP meant "abusing HTTP through polling", one connection per message, with the overhead that implies. A WebSocket replaces that with a single connection that either side may write to at will. Lesson 1205 is about what you take on by keeping it open.

3. The trade-off: you cannot ask a queue a question

The reason not to put a broker everywhere is that a message is not a call. There is no return value, no status code and no moment at which the sender learns whether the work succeeded. If the caller genuinely needs the answer - a price to display, an authorisation to proceed - a broker makes it wait for a reply on a second channel it now has to correlate, which is a synchronous call rebuilt out of two asynchronous ones and worse in every dimension.

The second cost is repetition. Every broker in ordinary use delivers at least once: RabbitMQ requeues any delivery that was not acknowledged before the channel closed, and tells you outright that consumers "must be prepared to handle redeliveries and otherwise be implemented with idempotence in mind". A design that moves work behind a broker has, in the same stroke, made every handler responsible for surviving a repeat. Lesson 0605 already built the mechanism for that on the HTTP surface, and lessons 1203 and 1204 are where it becomes unavoidable rather than optional.

The cost that is easiest to leave out of a design document is the third system. A broker is a stateful cluster with its own disks, its own failure modes, its own upgrade path and its own on-call rota. Adding one to decouple two services replaces a dependency you understand with a dependency you have not operated yet, and for a chain of two services with a shared release cadence that trade is often a loss. Reach for a broker when the decoupling buys something you can name.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Five services, each independently 99.9% available, are called in a synchronous chain. What is the chain?
0.999 to the fifth power is 0.99501, so the chain spends about 216 minutes a month down against a single service's 43. The first option ignores the waiting entirely, the second treats dependent failures as if they cancelled out, and the fourth confuses latency with availability - a slow hop is not an unavailable one.
Which requirement rules out a broker and forces a synchronous call?
Needing the result in hand is the one thing a broker cannot give you, because a publish returns acceptance rather than an answer. The other three are the cases brokers are for: holding a message across an outage, spreading work over consumers, and fanning one event out to several readers.
