Methods differ by the guarantees they carry, not by how they are spelled

foundation 7 min Three properties the whole network reads. · RFC 9110 §9.2 - Common Method Properties

The one-minute version

  • A method carries three declared properties: safe, idempotent and cacheable. They are the whole reason the choice matters.
  • Safe means read-only in intent. Idempotent means the effect of many identical requests equals the effect of one. Every safe method is idempotent.
  • These are promises to intermediaries: a proxy may retry an idempotent request and must not retry a non-idempotent one.
  • PUT and DELETE are idempotent but not safe; POST is neither. That single row is why POST retries are dangerous.
  • What it costs: the guarantees are a contract your handler must actually honour, and nothing checks that it does.

1. The mental model: a method is a public promise

The reason a method is not just a label is that other software acts on it without asking you. A browser will re-issue a GET when a connection drops. A reverse proxy will hold a GET response and hand it to the next caller. A load balancer will send an idempotent request to a second backend when the first times out. None of them read your handler. They read the method and act on the property the specification attaches to it.

So the method is a promise you make to strangers about your own handler. If GET /orders/42 charges a card, nothing in the protocol stops you, and everything in the network will punish you: a prefetch, a crawler, or a retried timeout will charge the card again. The guarantee was never enforced. It was assumed, by everyone.

2. The mechanism: what each word actually claims

Safe

RFC 9110 defines a method as safe when its semantics are essentially read-only: the client does not request and does not expect any state change on the origin server. It does not say nothing happens - a log line is written, a counter moves - only that the client did not ask for it and cannot be held to it. GET, HEAD, OPTIONS and TRACE are safe. That is what makes prefetching, crawling and link previews possible at all.

Idempotent

A method is idempotent when the intended effect of several identical requests is the same as the effect of one. RFC 9110 names PUT, DELETE and the safe methods. Note what is being compared: the effect on the server, not the response the client gets back. Deleting the same resource twice is idempotent even though the second call may answer 404, because the server's state after two calls equals its state after one. The specification is explicit that a proxy must not automatically retry a non-idempotent request.

Cacheable

Cacheable means a response to this method may be stored and reused for a later request. RFC 9110 defines caching semantics for GET, HEAD and POST, and notes plainly that the overwhelming majority of cache implementations support only GET and HEAD. Treat POST responses as uncacheable in practice and say so in your design rather than relying on a corner of the specification that deployed caches largely do not implement.

3. The trade-off: the guarantee is yours to keep

Nothing verifies these properties. The specification defines them and your handler either honours them or quietly does not, and the cost lands on somebody else's retry. Two consequences follow, and both are design work rather than protocol work.

There is also a reading habit worth forming here. When a page of vendor documentation says an endpoint is "idempotent", ask which of the three properties it means, because the word is used loosely in product writing to mean "harmless" and the specification uses it to mean one precise thing about repeated identical requests. The three properties are independent claims and a source that blurs them is a source to check.

The first is that a non-idempotent operation you want to retry has to be made idempotent by hand, usually with a client-supplied key that the server records and de-duplicates against. That is an extra table, an extra index and a retention policy, and it is the price of a safe retry. The second is that choosing PUT over POST for a create is a real trade-off and not a style preference: PUT gives you idempotence because the client names the resource, which means the client must be able to generate the identifier.

Idempotent is not the same as concurrency-safe. Two identical PUT requests arriving at once are idempotent by the specification and can still interleave into a lost update, because idempotence constrains repetition and says nothing about ordering. Conditional requests, which lesson 0203 covers, are what turn that into a defined outcome.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which pair is idempotent but not safe?
PUT and DELETE change state, so they are not safe, and repeating either leaves the same state, so they are idempotent. GET and HEAD are safe as well as idempotent; POST is neither; and OPTIONS and TRACE are safe, which makes them idempotent too.
A DELETE is repeated and the second call answers 404. Is it idempotent?
Idempotence compares the effect on the server, and the resource is gone after one call and after two. The first two options compare the responses instead of the state, and the fourth invents a body condition the definition does not have.
