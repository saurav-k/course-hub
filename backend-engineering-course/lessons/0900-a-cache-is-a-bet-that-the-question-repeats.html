Module 09 · Caching · Lesson 0900

A cache is a bet that the same question will be asked again

foundation 9 min Why the thing exists, before any pattern is named. · Atikoglu et al., Workload Analysis of a Large-Scale Key-Value Store (SIGMETRICS 2012)

The one-minute version

  • A cache is a second copy of an answer, kept somewhere cheaper to reach than the thing that produced it.
  • It only earns anything when the same question comes back, so a cache is a bet on repetition and the hit rate is the bet paying off.
  • The economics fit in one line: you pay the lookup on every read and the origin only on the misses.
  • Read the same hit rates as load rather than as latency and the picture inverts - 17 points of hit rate is fourteen times the database traffic.
  • What it costs: a second copy of the truth that can disagree with the first, a miss path slower than having no cache, and one more thing that can fail.

1. The mental model: a store of answers, not of data

The definition that survives every implementation

A cache is a place to put an answer you already worked out, chosen because getting it back from there is cheaper than working it out again. That is the whole definition, and it holds for a CPU's L1, a browser's disk cache, the HTTP cache of lesson 0204, and the Redis instance your service talks to. What changes between them is only what "cheaper" means: fewer nanoseconds, fewer bytes on the wire, or fewer joins on a database that is already the constraint.

Two properties follow from the definition and they are what make a cache a cache. It is not the authoritative copy, so it is always allowed to have nothing, and a correct system must survive that. And it is allowed to throw things away, at any moment, without telling anyone. Facebook's memcache paper puts both in one sentence when it explains why writes delete rather than update: memcache "is not the authoritative source of the data and is therefore allowed to evict cached data" (Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, §2). A store you are not allowed to lose is a database wearing a cache's clothes, and it will be operated like one whatever you called it.

The bet, and how often it pays

The bet is repetition. Storing an answer costs memory and a write; it only returns anything if somebody asks the same question before the entry goes away. So a cache is worth having exactly when the request stream repeats itself, and worthless when every request is unique - which is why a cache in front of a search over free text usually disappoints, and a cache in front of a user profile usually does not.

That the stream repeats is measurable rather than assumed. Atikoglu and colleagues traced 284 billion requests across five of Facebook's Memcached pools and reported a mean GET hit rate for each: 98.7%, 98.2%, 93.7%, 92.9% and 81.4%. They also found a GET to SET ratio of 30 to 1, which is the same fact stated as traffic: these workloads ask far more often than they change.

2. The mechanism: the arithmetic a hit rate buys

One line, and what each term is

A read either finds the entry or does not. The lookup is paid either way, and the expensive path is paid only on the fraction that misses, so the mean cost of a read is a straight weighted average with one term.

mean read = tc + (1 − h) × to Read as: the average time a read takes is the cache lookup tc, which every single read pays, plus the origin cost to, which is paid only by the 1 − h share of reads that found nothing. h is the hit rate: the fraction of reads the cache answered by itself.
  1. Fix the two costs, and label them honestly. Take tc = 0.5 ms and to = 20 ms. The first is anchored: redis-benchmark on the loopback interface reports a median of 0.143 ms per SET, and a cache one host away in the same datacentre adds a network round trip on top. The second is an assumption about one indexed query, not a measurement, and every number below inherits it.
  2. Read it with no cache at all. h = 0 gives 0.5 + 1 × 20 = 20.5 ms. That is worse than not having a cache, by exactly the 0.5 ms you spent asking it.
  3. Read it at four hits in five. h = 0.8 gives 0.5 + 0.2 × 20 = 4.5 ms.
  4. Read it at ninety-nine in a hundred. h = 0.99 gives 0.5 + 0.01 × 20 = 0.7 ms.
  5. Now read the same three hit rates as load. At 10,000 reads a second the store sees 10,000, then 2,000, then 100 queries a second.

Those last two steps are the same arithmetic and they do not say the same thing. Between 4.5 ms and 0.7 ms a reader feels almost nothing; both are far below the moment anyone notices. Between 2,000 and 100 queries a second the database sees a factor of twenty, which is the difference between a replica and a fleet of them. Latency is what the reader feels and load is what the store survives, and past a certain hit rate you are no longer buying the first at all.

3. The trade-off: what the second copy costs you

The costs are not the memory. Memory is the one you can quote a price for, and it is rarely the one that hurts. The three that hurt are that the second copy can disagree with the first, that the miss path is now longer than no cache at all, and that a component your correctness does not depend on has quietly become one your capacity does.

That third one is the one teams meet in an incident. Once the store is provisioned for 100 queries a second because the cache absorbs the other 9,900, an empty cache is not a slow system, it is an outage - the store is asked for a hundred times the work it was sized for, by every process at once. Lesson 0905 is about that class of failure, and the important part is that it was created here, on the day the cache started working well.

A hit rate reported on its own is close to meaningless, and it is the number every cache dashboard shows first. It is an average over every key, so one enormous, perfectly cacheable key family can hold the number at 95% while the family you actually care about sits at 40% and the store carries all of it. Atikoglu's team found this inside one pool: about half of the general-purpose pool's keys appeared in only 1% of requests, a long tail that "benefit[s] little or not at all from a demand-filled cache" while contributing most of the misses. Measure the hit rate per key family, or you are measuring the family you were never worried about.

A read cache is at a 50% hit rate. What has it definitely bought you?
Only the misses reach the origin, so half of them do, and each of those now pays a lookup it would not otherwise have paid. The second option denies the miss penalty that the three-trip path makes real; the third has the mechanism backwards, since load falls with the miss rate rather than latency falling on its own; and the fourth invents a storage saving a cache never gives, because the origin still holds every row.
Which fact makes a workload a poor candidate for a cache?
A cache is a bet on repetition, so a stream of unique questions loses that bet on every request and pays the miss penalty for the privilege. A high read-to-write ratio and an expensive origin are the two things that make caching pay, not the reverse, and entry size is a capacity question you solve by caching fewer things rather than a reason not to cache.
