Module 05 · Proof · Chapter 5

Most of the improvements your team reported are inside the noise.

working 14 min Six decisions about ownership, statistics and organisational cost. · Miller, Adding Error Bars to Evals

The one-minute version

  • Not how to write an eval, which the hub already teaches. This is who owns the harness, whether the number moved, and what the programme costs.
  • An evaluation is an experiment, so its output is an estimate with an error bar. A 164-question suite carries a standard error of about 3.2 percent, so most three-point improvements are not results.
  • The rubric cannot be written first. A peer-reviewed result finds some criteria only become definable after you have seen the outputs.
  • A judge that returns the same score every time is not thereby a good judge. Reliability and validity come apart, and teams measure the cheap one.

1. Who owns the gate

What does a red eval block, and who is allowed to override it?

The call. Which failing cases stop a release outright, which a named person may waive, and whether the waiver carries an expiry date.

What you must know first. That an LLM regression suite is not a deterministic gate. Uber: "Due to the non-deterministic nature of LLMs, running the same evaluation with no changes... can result in different outcomes." Source: Uber QueryGPT (19 September 2024). This course's own reading: it is a statistical monitor, and teams that treat it as a gate either set it so loose it catches nothing or spend their lives triaging flakes.

The bill. Whoever holds the override becomes the release bottleneck, so the authority sits with a role, not a person. The recurring cost is triage: somebody decides weekly whether a red light is a regression or a flake.

How you find out you were wrong. Too loose, and the signal is an incident whose case was already in the suite and already amber. Too tight, and the team stops adding cases, because every case is a future blocker. The second failure is silent: the suite stops growing.

The reversal. Loosening a gate is an afternoon. Tightening one after people have learned to route around it is a cultural change costing a quarter. Set the blocking category small and defensible on day one rather than broad and negotiable.

Who owns the golden set, and what happens when they leave?

The call. Whether the evaluation set has a named owner with allocated time, or is a shared artefact everyone edits and nobody maintains.

What you must know first. That the rubric provably cannot be written first. Shankar and colleagues name it criteria drift: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria", and find "some criteria appears dependent on the specific LLM outputs observed". Source: Shankar, Zamfirescu-Pereira, Hartmann, Parameswaran and Arawjo (arXiv:2404.12272, UIST). Write-then-build-then-measure cannot work, and that is a peer-reviewed result rather than a preference.

The bill. A continuously revised artefact with an owner, not a deliverable written once and satisfied. LinkedIn's answer at their scale was 500 conversations a day assessed by linguists. A team that says it will add evals later is saying it will hire linguists later.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a suite unchanged for two months while the product changed, visible in the eval directory's commit history, which nobody reads. The second is an owner leaving and nobody able to say why a case is in the set.

The reversal. A golden set with a lost rationale cannot be repaired, only rebuilt from production traffic: a quarter of somebody's time. Recording why each case exists costs one line, and it is the cheapest insurance here.

2. Whether the number moved

Is the improvement real, or inside the noise?

The call. Whether to ship on a reported difference, and whether your suite can detect a difference of the size you care about at all.

What you must know first. Miller's five recommendations. Compute standard errors; cluster them when questions come in groups, where the adjustment reached "up to 3X" and published intervals are "likely anti-conservative"; resample, where two samples per question cut variance by a third against a two-thirds ceiling; compare paired per-question differences, cutting estimator variance by about a third at a correlation of 0.5; and run a power analysis first. Source: Evan Miller (arXiv:2411.00640), published by Anthropic.

The bill. Nearly nothing, which is what makes the omission remarkable. Paired analysis is free: same data, same runs, a third less noise. The statistics themselves are taught in Statistical Foundations and this course does not re-teach them.

The failure this chapter exists to prevent. Five questions from each of 100 documents gives roughly 100 independent observations, not 500, and the naive interval understates the error by up to a factor of three. Golden datasets are almost always built several questions per document, so a team that has never adjusted for clustering has been shipping on intervals that were too narrow.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is an aggregate score improving for six weeks while no user-facing metric moves. It reaches whoever owns the product metric long before whoever owns the eval, and the conversation between them is what this chapter is for.

The reversal. Recomputing an old comparison correctly is cheap, and worth doing once in public on a change the team was proud of. What cannot be reversed is the roadmap spent on differences that were not real.

Custom criteria or a generic metric?

The call. Whether to score with off-the-shelf metrics because they exist, or build criteria specific to the failures you actually have.

What you must know first. That the generic ones have been tested and found wanting. Eugene Yan: off-the-shelf metrics "barely correlate with application-specific performance", and "a model can have high ROC-AUC and PR-AUC but still not be suitable for production". Husain is blunter: "Generic metrics are worse than useless - they actively impede progress". Sources: Yan (March 2024) and Husain (24 March 2025).

The bill. Custom criteria cost error analysis, which Husain calls the single most valuable activity in AI development and which is a person reading outputs. Generic metrics cost nothing to adopt and charge you a quarter chasing a number that was never about your product.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a metric everybody quotes and nobody trusts. It arrives when somebody asks what a score of 0.82 would have to fall to before the team acted, and nobody can answer.

The reversal. Dropping a generic metric is free technically and awkward politically, because it has been on a slide. Adding custom criteria later usually reveals that half the generic ones measured the same thing.

3. The instrument, and what it costs

When does the judge need its own eval?

The call. Whether the model doing your scoring is a monitored dependency with its own regression suite, or a tool that either works or does not.

What you must know first. That reliability and validity come apart. A large-scale evaluation of judges reports high internal consistency alongside failure to correlate with human judgement: stability is cheap, agreement is expensive. Source: Norman, Rivera and Hughes; only the qualitative finding is used, the numeric detail being unextractable. The bias catalogue is CALM. Source: Ye et al.

The bill. A fixed set of human-labelled items, re-scored on every judge version change, plus a periodic alignment check. Discord's judge prompts ask for yes-or-no answers, and the practitioner consensus prefers binary or pairwise, because judges cluster on three and four.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is judge scores drifting from human ratings, with a lag of months, because nothing compares them unless somebody scheduled it. The person who notices is usually a new joiner reading outputs and disagreeing with the dashboard.

The reversal. A drifted judge invalidates every comparison made through it, so the reversal is re-scoring the history you still care about. Pinning the judge version and keeping the labelled set is the cheap prevention, and chapter 8 applies the same discipline underneath.

How much of the eval budget goes to looking at data by hand?

The call. What share of the programme is tooling, and what share is a person reading inputs and outputs on a schedule.

What you must know first. What the practitioners prescribe. Husain's strongest instructions are organisational: "You must remove all friction from the process of looking at data" and "You can never stop looking at data", alongside "Don't buy fancy LLM tools." Sources: Husain (29 March 2024) and applied-llms.org.

The bill. Half is a discount nobody claims: eval runs are your most latency-insensitive workload, and a batch API halves both input and output, most batches finishing inside an hour. Source: Anthropic. The other half is a standing role, and if it is not in the headcount plan it does not exist.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a team that talks about eval platforms and cannot show last week's failure taxonomy. Husain's observation: the teams who succeed barely talk about tools at all.

The reversal. Tooling is replaceable and the habit is not. A team that has been looking at data adopts any platform in a week; a team that bought a platform instead has to build the habit from nothing, and the platform will not help.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your 500-question suite draws five questions from each of 100 source documents. What does that do to your confidence interval?
Questions drawn in groups are not independent, so you have roughly 100 observations rather than 500, and the reported cluster adjustment reached three times the naive value. The first option is the mistake the correction exists for. The second inverts it: shared structure inflates the error rather than removing it. The third guesses a form for the adjustment that the source does not support and that understates the published case.
A team proposes writing the complete evaluation rubric before building, then measuring against it. What does the peer-reviewed evidence say?
Criteria drift is the finding: grading outputs is part of how the criteria become definable, so part of the rubric is not knowable in advance. The first option adds review, which does not create knowledge nobody has yet. The third is the plan the result invalidates. The fourth reaches the same conclusion for a reason the study does not support, and vagueness is a writing problem rather than an ordering one.
Your judge model scores the same output identically every single time you run it. What have you established?
Test-retest stability and agreement with human judgement come apart, and the published finding is judges high on the first and failing on the second. The second option is exactly the inference the result forbids. The third confuses two properties: a judge can be perfectly repeatable and still favour whichever answer it saw first. The fourth overstates the non-determinism point, which is about run-to-run variation in the system under test.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Your team wants to detect a two-point improvement on a suite whose current pass rate is about 0.8. Questions are written one per source document, so clustering is not an issue.

Work out roughly how many questions the suite needs for the standard error to fall below one point, and state the two cheaper moves you would try before commissioning that many.

Hint

The standard error of a proportion is the square root of p times one minus p, divided by n. Solve it for n at the error you want, then remember that Miller lists two variance reductions that cost no extra questions at all.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the expression. Standard error equals the square root of p(1 - p) / n. At p = 0.8, p(1 - p) = 0.16.
  2. Solve for n at a one-point error. 0.01 = square root of (0.16 / n), so 0.0001 = 0.16 / n, so n = 1,600.
  3. Sanity-check the direction. At 164 questions the error is about 3.2%, and error falls with the square root of n, so roughly a hundredfold more questions would be needed for a tenfold reduction. 1,600 for a threefold reduction is the right order.
  4. Name the two cheaper moves. Pair the comparison per question rather than comparing two averages, which cuts estimator variance by about a third at a correlation of 0.5. Then resample answers: two samples per question cut variance by a third and six by five ninths, approaching a two-thirds ceiling. Both spend compute rather than human labelling, and compute is the cheap input.

Sanity check. Your n should be well above 1,000 and well below 100,000. If you got a few hundred, you solved for a three-point error rather than a one-point one; if you got tens of thousands, you squared something that should not have been squared.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to an eval programme, and the trap in each.

  • "Your score went from 82.9 to 84.2." The trap is celebrating. Ask how many questions, whether drawn in groups, and whether the comparison was paired.
  • "Who owns the golden set?" The trap is naming a team. Name a person, their allocated time, and where each case's rationale is written.
  • "What does a red eval block?" The trap is saying everything. Name the small category nobody may override, and who signs a waiver on the rest.
  • "How do you know the judge is right?" The trap is quoting its consistency. Show the human-labelled set and when it was last re-scored.
  • "What does the programme cost?" The trap is quoting the tooling. Quote the standing role, and note that batch pricing halves the compute half.
Your teacher. Take the last improvement your team shipped on an eval number. Find the suite size, work out the standard error, and see whether the difference clears it. Most do not. Then check whether the comparison was paired.
