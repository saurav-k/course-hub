Module 06 · Money · Chapter 6

The cheapest reliable lever is a shorter answer.

working 13 min Five decisions that make unit economics a design input rather than a monthly report. · Anthropic, Prompt caching

The one-minute version

  • Output costs several times input at every vendor, clustering at five times, so the first lever is a shorter answer and max_tokens is a budget line.
  • A cached read costs a tenth of a fresh input token, and the five-minute cache pays back after one read. Below the minimum cacheable length it silently does nothing, and the API returns no error.
  • At the small-model tier, self-hosting loses on price, so the reasons that survive are residency, latency floors and pinning.
  • The same rented silicon spans a 21-fold price range across providers. Where you rent moves the answer more than whether you host.

1. The denominator, and the two cheapest levers

Cost per resolved user problem, not cost per call: what is the denominator?

The call. What unit your cost number is per. Cost per call is measurable and almost useless, because a system that answers badly and is asked again looks cheap on it.

What you must know first. What a cost number looks like when it is published with its conditions. Semgrep reported about 0.17 USD per vulnerability found, on one detection task, one dataset, one run, inside a purpose-built harness. That is a denominator a reader can argue with, and the arguing is the point. Source: Semgrep (22 June 2026).

The bill. Choosing the denominator costs one conversation with whoever owns the product metric and nothing else. Not choosing it costs a quarter of optimisation aimed at the wrong quantity, and every one of those optimisations changes behaviour.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is cost per call falling while the total bill rises. It shows up on a finance dashboard rather than an engineering one, and the two teams have no shared meeting until somebody escalates.

The reversal. Changing the denominator is free and instantly reframes everything already measured. What cannot be reversed is the work already spent optimising the old one, which is why this decision belongs before the first cost review rather than after it.

Which caching layer, and what does a silent miss cost?

The call. Whether to cache the reused prefix, at which time-to-live, and what you assert to prove the caching is actually happening.

What you must know first. The multipliers and the trap. Relative to base input price, a five-minute cache write costs 1.25x, a one-hour write 2x, and a read 0.1x. The vendor states the payback: the five-minute cache pays off after one read, the one-hour cache after two. The minimum cacheable prompt length is 512, 1,024, 2,048 or 4,096 tokens depending on model, and below it "the API returns no error". Source: Anthropic prompt caching.

The bill. Caching attacks the input side only, so its leverage is set by your input-to-output ratio. On a worked twenty-turn session with a 30,000-token reused prefix, this course's arithmetic gives 72.8% off overall and 82.4% off the input alone. Long-context, short-answer work is where caching pays; short-prompt, long-generation work is where it does almost nothing.

How you find out you were wrong. You do not, and that is the failure. Nothing errors, nothing alerts, and the bill simply fails to fall by the amount somebody promised in a planning document three months earlier. The person who notices is whoever reconciles the forecast.

The reversal. Trivially two-way: caching is a request flag. The irreversible part is the trust spent on a saving that never arrived, which is why the assertion goes in with the flag rather than after it.

2. Smaller, cheaper, and where the arithmetic actually lands

Route to a smaller model where, and what proves quality held?

The call. Which requests get the cheaper model, and what evidence is required before that route opens rather than after the bill improves.

What you must know first. The published procedure runs the opposite way to instinct. OpenAI advise building the prototype "with the most capable model for every task to establish a performance baseline. From there, try swapping in smaller models to see if they still achieve acceptable results." Starting cheap is diagnostically useless: a failure tells you nothing about whether the task is impossible, the prompt is bad, or the model is too small. Source: OpenAI, "A practical guide to building agents".

The bill. Two structural facts beat any individual price. Output costs four to eight times input across three vendors' tables, clustering at five, so a shorter answer is the cheapest saving. And a tokenizer change is a price change: Anthropic document a newer tokenizer producing about 30% more tokens for the same text. The interview track owns the whiteboard version.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a quality regression confined to the routed slice, which an aggregate score hides by construction. It arrives when somebody segments the eval by route, which for most teams is never.

The reversal. A route is a config change, so undoing it is an afternoon. What it costs to have been wrong is one segment of users who had a worse product for a quarter, and no record of when it started.

At what volume does self-hosting win, and what is the arithmetic?

cost per 1M tokens = gpu_hourly ÷ (T × 3600 × u) × 106 Read as: gpu_hourly is what one accelerator costs you per hour, T is the tokens per second it sustains under your real workload, 3600 converts hours to seconds, and u is the fraction of the hour it is actually generating rather than idle. The formula is the one the inference course derives; this chapter only puts today's prices into it.

The call. Whether to rent accelerators and serve the model yourself, or keep buying tokens, decided on quality-matched arithmetic rather than on a slogan about volume.

What you must know first. Today's inputs, and that they move weekly. The median on-demand H100 rate is $3.39 per GPU-hour across 54 tracked providers, with a 21-fold spread from $0.67 spot to $14.19 on demand for identical silicon. Source: GetDeploying, data date 28 August 2026. Blended hosted prices at an 80 percent input mix run from about $0.16 to $9.00 per million tokens.

Two honest limits on this arithmetic. First, it is only answerable quality-matched, and one accelerator does not serve frontier-class quality, so the comparison stops being fair at the top of the range. Second, every number in it goes stale in weeks: one vendor's own page documented an introductory price becoming standard days after the research read it. Published break-even token volumes circulate widely and every source found for them was a marketing post with unstated assumptions, so this course teaches you to derive your own rather than quoting theirs.

The bill. A serving operation: capacity planning on a twelve-month horizon, an on-call rotation for hardware, and the utilisation term you control least. The inference course owns how to run it.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a utilisation number well below the one in your business case, and it arrives about a month after go-live, on a dashboard the platform team owns. Nobody who signed the case is looking at it.

The reversal. Going back to an API is a routing change and a contract: weeks. Going the other way took a quarter and a hiring plan. The one-way part is the team you built to run it, which is why the surviving reasons are residency, latency floors, rate-limit independence and pinning.

3. The ceiling

What is the ceiling, and what enforces it while everyone is asleep?

The call. The maximum spend per hour and per task, and which component refuses the next call when it is reached, with no human in the path.

What you must know first. How much an agent multiplies a chat bill, and that the multiplier is a design choice. This course's own arithmetic on a twelve-step agent carrying 25,000 input tokens per step puts it at 76 times a single chat turn without caching and 34 times with a 20,000-token prefix cached. Read the assumption before the number: the multiplier is driven almost entirely by how much context each step carries, so halving the context roughly halves the bill.

The bill. The costs a per-token model silently omits: server-side web search priced per thousand searches, a fetched page becoming input tokens, tool definitions charged on every call, a rich toolset adding thousands of input tokens before the user's message, session runtime metered hourly, and a regional multiplier. For a tool-heavy agent none is a rounding error.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a bill, with a lag of up to a month. Nothing about a runaway loop looks like an outage: latency is fine, errors are zero, dashboards green while the meter runs. Finance sees it first. The build track owns the enforcement point.

The reversal. Raising a ceiling is a config change. There is no reversal for a weekend of unbounded spend, which is what makes this the one decision in the chapter that is worth building before the feature it protects.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You enable prompt caching, the configuration is right, and the bill does not change. What is the most likely explanation?
The vendor documents that prompts shorter than the minimum cannot be cached and the API returns no error, and the minimum varies by model. The first two are real cache misses and both would still report cache-creation tokens somewhere. The fourth is true of some workloads and would reduce the saving rather than remove it, and it is the reason to check your input-to-output ratio before promising a number.
Your team wants to cut cost and proposes starting the prototype on the cheapest model. What does the published guidance say?
Establish the baseline with the most capable model, then swap smaller ones in: the reason is diagnostic rather than generous, because a failure on a cheap model tells you nothing about which of three causes it was. The second option is the instinct the guidance exists to correct. The third splits the difference and keeps the diagnostic ambiguity. The fourth doubles the cost of the phase whose purpose is to establish what is achievable at all.
A business case says self-hosting will beat your hosted small model on price. Which number would you ask for first?
Utilisation moves the answer by a factor of three across the plausible range and is the term a team controls least, so it is the assumption most likely to be optimistic. The first option is the number business cases lead with and it does not appear in the formula. The third and fourth are both real and both matter, and each is the right second question: quality-matching decides whether the comparison is fair, and the rental spread decides where you rent rather than whether you host.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A session reuses a 30,000-token prefix across 20 turns, adding 500 new input tokens and producing 800 output tokens per turn. Input is $2 per million tokens and output is $10.

Work out the input cost with no caching and with a five-minute cache, reproducing the two percentages above, and say why the overall saving is smaller than the input-only saving.

Hint

With no cache the whole prefix is a fresh input on every turn. With a cache it is written once at 1.25 times the base rate and read at a tenth of it thereafter. Output is not cacheable at all.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the uncached input. 20 turns times 30,500 tokens is 610,000 tokens, at $2 per million, which is $1.22.
  2. Count the cached input. One write of 30,000 at 1.25 times base is equivalent to 37,500 tokens. Twenty reads at 0.1 times base is 20 times 3,000, which is 60,000 equivalent tokens. The 500 new tokens a turn add 10,000. Total 107,500 equivalent tokens, or $0.215.
  3. Add the output, which is identical either way. 20 times 800 is 16,000 tokens at $10 per million, which is $0.16.
  4. Compare the two totals. $1.38 against $0.375, which is the 72.8% and 82.4% quoted above.

Sanity check. The gap between the two percentages is the whole lesson: caching attacks only the input side, so the leverage is set by your input-to-output ratio. If your arithmetic gives the same percentage twice, you cached the output, which no vendor on the market lets you do.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to a cost model, and the trap in each.

  • "What does this cost?" The trap is answering per call. Answer per resolved user problem, and say what a re-ask does to the number.
  • "You said caching would halve it." The trap is checking the config. Show the usage fields, and the CI assertion that they are not both zero.
  • "Why not the cheaper model everywhere?" The trap is arguing on quality in general. Show the eval segmented by route.
  • "At what volume do we self-host?" The trap is quoting a break-even from a blog. Derive it, and lead with the utilisation you are assuming.
  • "What stops a runaway overnight?" The trap is naming an alert. Name the component that refuses the call, and the state it enters first.
Your teacher. Take one real request from your system and account for every token in it: the system prompt, the tool schemas, the retrieved passages, the user's message, and the answer. Most people find the tool schemas alone are larger than the message. That accounting is what turns cost from a monthly report into a design input, and it takes about twenty minutes.
