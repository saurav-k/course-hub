Module 08 · Supply · Chapter 8

Your dependency has an end-of-life date somebody else set.

frontier 14 min Six decisions about the supply under the system. · Anthropic model deprecations

The one-minute version

  • Notice periods are published and short. One vendor commits to at least 60 days; the other to six months for general models and about two weeks for previews.
  • Sampling parameters that were in every inference call for years became a 400 error on newer models. What breaks on upgrade is not the prompt.
  • The largest vendor in the field retired its own evaluation platform and agent builder, telling customers to move that logic into application code - which a practitioner had advised two years earlier.
  • Two agent protocols moved to foundation governance. That change, not the feature set, is what you are deciding about.

1. The calendar, and what upgrading actually breaks

What is the retirement date of every model in production, and who owns that calendar?

The call. Whether a named owner holds a list of every model identifier in production with its retirement date beside it, reviewed on a cadence rather than on a notice.

What you must know first. The published floors. Anthropic commits to "at least 60 days' notice before model retirement for publicly released models", and their table records Claude Opus 4.1 deprecated 5 June 2026 and retired 5 August 2026. OpenAI state "At least 6 months" for generally available models, "At least 3 months" for specialised variants, and that preview models "may be retired with much shorter notice, such as 2 weeks". Sources: Anthropic and OpenAI, live pages that change every few weeks.

The bill. One list and a recurring review. The migration is the real cost and it is not a code change: a re-run of chapter 5's suite against the successor, with the old scores kept.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is an email from a vendor and the lag is the notice period. With no owner it lands in a shared inbox and the discovery moment is a 404 in production. Two months is comfortable if you were watching and impossible if not.

The reversal. None: you cannot un-retire a model. What is reversible is your dependency on a specific identifier, and the cheap version is a gateway mapping a logical name to a pinned version, turning a migration into a config change plus an evaluation run.

What breaks on upgrade that is not the prompt?

The call. What your migration test covers, given that the interface changes as well as the behaviour.

What you must know first. That the interface changes too. Anthropic's deprecation page records temperature, top_p and top_k deprecated from Claude Opus 4.7 onward, and that a non-default value "Returns a 400 error". Chapter 6 carries the other one: a newer tokenizer producing about 30 percent more tokens is a price change wearing a model name.

The bill. A migration test exercising the request shape, not only output quality, against every model you might move to. Cheap, and nobody has it: the obvious test is an eval, and an eval never sends a malformed request.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a 400 in production on cutover day, which is the best case: loud and immediate. The bad case is the price change, which is silent, arrives on a bill, and looks like growth.

The reversal. Trivial while the old model still answers, which is the window the notice period gives you. After retirement there is nothing to roll back to, so the migration test runs inside the notice period rather than at the end of it.

2. When the version string lies, and who else you keep warm

How do we detect that a model changed under us when the version string did not?

The call. Whether a standing measurement runs against a pinned model, or you learn about a behaviour change from a user.

What you must know first. That it has been observed twice, from different directions. A study of March against June 2023 versions of the same named models reported GPT-4 falling from 84% to 51% on one prime-identification task. Separately, Voiceflow caught a 10% drop from a GPT-3.5 version upgrade using their own harness. Sources: Chen, Zaharia and Zou and Voiceflow via Eugene Yan.

Cite the drift paper with its caveat. It drew methodological criticism at the time: the prime task used only prime numbers, so a model that changed its default answer would swing wildly, and some reported code "errors" were formatting changes. The research could not adjudicate that debate, so the operational claim here leans on the corroborating Voiceflow observation rather than the headline number.

The bill. A scheduled run of a small fixed suite against the pinned production model. Chapter 6's batch discount applies to exactly this workload, halving the price of the thing teams say they cannot afford.

How you find out you were wrong. Without the standing run, from a complaint weeks late, attributed to a prompt change made in the same period. The confound is the expensive part: two changes and one signal, so nobody can say which caused it.

The reversal. If the version is pinned, roll back and re-measure. If not, there is nothing to roll back to, and the honest position is that you cannot say the system got worse, only that people say it did.

One vendor, two vendors, or an open-weight hedge?

The call. How many suppliers you keep working, and what "working" means: a passing eval on your suite, not a client library in the dependencies.

What you must know first. That a retired model does not always vanish. One vendor's pricing page marks retired models as still reachable through cloud marketplaces, turning a hard cutover into a longer tail on somebody else's terms.

The bill. A second vendor kept warm costs a second prompt, a second eval run and a second set of failure modes on call. Kept cold it costs nothing and buys nothing, because you first exercise it during the incident it was meant to cover.

How you find out you were wrong. During an outage or a retirement, when the fallback is exercised for the first time and does not pass. Everyone sees it at once, and the cost is whatever the primary was doing for you.

The reversal. Dropping the second supplier is easy and quiet. Adding one after a bad quarter is a project: prompts, tool schemas and eval expectations were all shaped by the first, and none of it is written down.

3. What you build on, and who governs it

Which vendor products are we allowed to build on?

The call. Which parts of your system may live inside a vendor's product rather than your own repository, knowing the product has a calendar too.

What you must know first. The best-documented case in the field. On 3 June 2026 OpenAI announced that its Evals platform, Agent Builder and reusable Prompts API retire on 30 November 2026, recommending a move to application code, Promptfoo and the Agents SDK. Hamel Husain had advised against buying eval tooling in March 2024. Sources: OpenAI and Husain (29 March 2024).

The bill. A vendor product buys weeks at the start and charges a migration at a moment you do not pick. Chapter 5's rule survives: the logic deciding whether you may ship belongs in your repository, versioned beside what it gates.

How you find out you were wrong. From a changelog entry with a date in it, at a lag equal to the notice. Nobody is watching, because the calendar you built was about models.

The reversal. Moving logic out of a vendor product is a rewrite on a deadline you did not set. Keeping it in your own code costs a little more up front and is the position no announcement can change.

What does committing to a protocol cost when its governance changes?

The call. Whether to build on an agent interoperability protocol now, and on what evidence, given that its governance rather than its features is what moved.

What you must know first. What is established and what is not. MCP was donated to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation on 9 December 2025, alongside Block's goose and OpenAI's AGENTS.md. A2A reached version 1.0 under the same governance on 9 April 2026, with more than 150 supporting organisations and SDKs in five languages. The press release names no company running it in production. Sources: Linux Foundation (9 December 2025) and Linux Foundation (9 April 2026).

The bill. The two sit at different layers, which the specification states: "A2A is about agents partnering on tasks, while MCP is more about agents using capabilities." The peer protocol buys discovery and a stateful task model, and costs serialisation and an opaque counterparty. Rost Glukhov's dissent is worth arguing with: A2A "is not dead, but it is also not universally useful", and "Production retention... separates genuine infrastructure from protocol theater". An attributed opinion, not a measurement.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a peer integration that could have been a function call, arriving when somebody new reads the code and asks why. Governance risk shows up much later, as a revision changing a normative requirement you built on.

The reversal. Dropping a protocol used inside your own system is a refactor. Dropping one you exposed to partners is a deprecation you owe them notice about, which puts you on the other side of this chapter.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You are planning a migration window from a published deprecation policy. Which figure should the plan be built on?
The policy language is "at least", so the floor is the commitment and everything above it is discretion. One published window came in one day above a 60-day floor, which is what a floor looks like in practice. The first option plans against generosity. The second averages tiers that apply to different models. The third generalises from a single case, which is exactly the error chapter 1 spends its pages on.
A vendor retires its own evaluation platform and tells customers to move that logic into application code. What is the transferable lesson?
The durable rule is about where the gating logic lives, not about the category. The first option over-generalises from one retirement to a whole market. The third is a true and useful fact and it is the mechanism rather than the lesson: the reason it hurts is that the logic was theirs. The fourth is a truism that gives you no rule for deciding which thing to buy.
Users report that quality dropped last month. Your model identifier never changed and you keep no historical scores. What can you honestly say?
Drift is a comparison, so without the earlier scores there is no claim to make in either direction. The first and second options both assert a direction the evidence cannot support, and the second additionally confuses a pinned identifier with pinned behaviour, which is the whole point of the published cases. The fourth picks the change you happen to remember, which is how a confound becomes a conclusion.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Build the supply plan for a system that calls one general model, one preview model for a new feature, and one specialised variant for classification.

Work out the shortest notice window you are exposed to, and state the two things that have to exist before that window opens for the plan to be usable.

Hint

Your exposure is the shortest window across every model in the path, not the average. Then ask what you would actually do on the day the notice arrives, and what has to already exist for that to be possible.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the floor for each tier. A generally available model gives at least six months; a specialised variant at least three; a preview model may give notice as short as about two weeks.
  2. Take the minimum, not the mean. The system is unavailable when any model in the path is, so the exposure is about two weeks, set by the preview model.
  3. Name the first prerequisite. An evaluation suite that can be run against a successor and produce a comparable number, which is chapter 5's work and cannot be built in a fortnight.
  4. Name the second. A gateway mapping a logical model name to a pinned version, so the cutover is a configuration change rather than a code change across every call site.

Sanity check. If your answer is six months, you took the maximum: the shortest window in the path governs, and a preview model in a production path is a fortnight of notice wearing a six-month plan. If your two prerequisites are both things you would build during the window, neither is a prerequisite.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to a supply plan, and the trap in each one.

  • "When does this model retire?" The trap is saying there is no notice yet. Give the policy floor for its tier, and who owns the calendar.
  • "What does the migration test cover?" The trap is showing the eval. Show the request-shape test too: a parameter can become a 400.
  • "How would we know it drifted?" The trap is naming a dashboard. Name the pinned version, the fixed suite and the kept scores.
  • "We have a second vendor." The trap is a client library. Ask when its eval last passed.
  • "Why is this in our repository?" The trap is defending the build. Gating logic is versioned beside what it gates.
Your teacher. Write the list: every model identifier in your production path, its tier, and the policy floor for that tier. Most people find one entry they did not know was a preview model, and that entry sets the exposure for the whole system.
