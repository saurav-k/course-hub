Module 03 · Shape · Chapter 3

Autonomy is a dial with a bill, not a product tier.

working 12 min Five decisions about shape, taken once the problem is settled. · Anthropic, Building Effective Agents

The one-minute version

  • Anthropic's own guidance says to add agentic systems only when simpler solutions fall short, and that a single call with retrieval and examples is usually enough. A vendor saying its own category is often unnecessary is the most credible move in the field.
  • Two practitioners reached the same arithmetic independently. Per-step reliability compounds multiplicatively: 90 percent over five steps is 59 percent, and 95 percent over ten is 60 percent.
  • Approval is a state, not an exception path. A protocol at version 1.0 models waiting for a human and waiting for authorisation as first-class task states, and a design that treats them as errors will lose them.
  • Teams reach a 70 to 80 percent quality bar with a framework and then find getting past it means reverse-engineering the framework, so they start over. Own your control flow before that happens.

1. How much autonomy, and what it costs

Single call, workflow, or agent?

The call. Which of three shapes this feature is, decided by a test rather than by ambition. The wrong answer here is not detected by anything, because all three shapes demo well.

What you must know first. Both published tests. Anthropic: use a workflow "when you can easily and cleanly decompose [tasks] into fixed subtasks", because workflows "offer predictability and consistency for well-defined tasks", and an agent "when it's difficult or impossible to predict the required number of steps". Source: Schluntz and Zhang, Anthropic (19 December 2024). OpenAI give the positive test: complex decision-making, rulesets that have become unwieldy, and heavy reliance on unstructured data, with the gate "Otherwise, a deterministic solution may suffice". Source: OpenAI, "A practical guide to building agents".

The bill. Anthropic state it plainly: "Agentic systems often trade latency and cost for better task performance, and you should consider when this tradeoff makes sense." Chapter 6 puts numbers on the trade. The attention cost is larger: an agent needs a trajectory eval, which the build track teaches, and a single call does not.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a latency complaint you cannot fix, because the steps are the product. It arrives at first real load, and the on-call engineer sees it first. The reverse error is quieter: a single call that should have been a workflow shows up as a long tail of failures nobody can group.

The reversal. Downward is a rewrite of the control flow and every eval built around it, so budget a sprint. Upward is easy, which is exactly the asymmetry that gets teams into trouble: the cheap direction is the one you regret.

How many hops before compounding error costs more than the autonomy buys?

The call. The step budget: how many model-mediated hops a task may take before the end-to-end success rate drops below the quality bar chapter 2 made you write down.

What you must know first. The arithmetic, and that two practitioners reached it independently. Honeycomb worked 90% accuracy across five chained calls to 59%, found "no tangible improvements" from chaining, and rejected it. Chip Huyen gives 95% per step as 60% over ten steps and 0.6% over one hundred. Sources: Carter, Honeycomb and Chip Huyen, "Agents" (7 January 2025).

The bill. Every hop is latency the user waits through and tokens you pay for, and the failures are not independent of each other in practice. The cheapest way to buy hops is to make a step deterministic, which removes it from the multiplication entirely rather than improving its factor.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a task success rate that nobody can attribute, because end-to-end scoring cannot localise which hop failed. It arrives as soon as you measure, and only whoever owns the eval sees it. Uber's answer was to evaluate each component with the correct input rather than the one the previous stage inferred.

The reversal. Removing a hop is usually cheap and is the first move nobody tries, because each hop was added for a reason somebody remembers. Adding hops is cheap in the moment and compounds silently, so the step budget belongs in the design document rather than in someone's head.

2. Where the human sits, and how many agents

Where does the human sit, and is approval a first-class state?

The call. Which actions require a person before they happen, and whether waiting for that person is a state your system can be in or an exception it recovers from.

What you must know first. That the protocols model it as a state. A2A's task lifecycle names INPUT_REQUIRED and AUTH_REQUIRED as interrupted states, distinct from the terminal FAILED and REJECTED. Source: A2A Protocol specification, version 1.0.0. Unit 42's mitigation for session smuggling is the same design from the security side: "Enforce out-of-band confirmation for sensitive actions". Source: Lu and Chen, Unit 42 (31 October 2025).

The bill. A human in the loop caps your throughput at their working hours and turns latency into a queueing problem rather than a serving one. The engineering cost is the durable state that survives the wait, which the build track teaches.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is retries firing on tasks that are merely waiting, and duplicate side effects behind them. It arrives the first time an approval takes longer than a timeout, at night, and on-call sees a spike with no failing dependency underneath it.

The reversal. Adding approval later is a schema change plus a state-machine change plus a backfill of in-flight tasks, so it is close to one-way. Removing it is trivial and is the change people make under delivery pressure without noticing what they removed.

One agent or several, and what does the second one cost in debuggability?

The call. Whether to split work across several agents or add tools to one, at the point where a single agent's prompt is getting long enough to feel uncomfortable.

What you must know first. That the published guidance says wait. OpenAI: "A single agent can handle many tasks by incrementally adding tools, keeping complexity manageable and simplifying evaluation and maintenance. Each new tool expands its capabilities without prematurely forcing you to orchestrate multiple agents." HumanLayer argue for small focused agents and for owning your control flow, which is a different claim: decompose the work, do not necessarily add autonomous peers. Sources: OpenAI and HumanLayer, 12-Factor Agents.

The bill. The second agent multiplies the eval surface rather than adding to it: you now need per-agent quality, handoff correctness, and end-to-end. Chapter 5 explains why end-to-end evaluation alone cannot localise a regression, which is the property you just lost.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a bug report nobody can reproduce because it depends on what the first agent handed the second. It arrives about a month in, and the engineer who owns neither agent sees it. This is the failure the typed handoff exists to prevent.

The reversal. Merging two agents back into one is a prompt rewrite and an eval rewrite, and it is genuinely done. Splitting is the cheap direction and the one that quietly removes your ability to localise anything, so make the split a written decision rather than a refactor.

3. What you build on

Framework or direct API?

The call. Whether to adopt an agent framework now, adopt one later, or call the model API directly and own the loop yourself.

What you must know first. The vendor's own warning and the pattern behind it. Anthropic advise "Start by using LLM APIs directly: many patterns can be implemented in a few lines of code", and warn that frameworks "often create extra layers of abstraction that can obscure the underlying prompts and responses, making them harder to debug". HumanLayer's twelve factors exist because teams reach a 70 to 80 percent quality bar, find "80% isn't good enough for most customer-facing features", discover that getting past it "requires reverse-engineering the framework, prompts, flow, etc.", and start over from scratch.

The bill. A framework buys you weeks at the start and charges the interest at exactly the point where you have users. Direct API costs you the loop, the retries and the state, all of which are ordinary engineering you have done before - see retries and idempotency.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is an incident where nobody on the call can say what was sent to the model. It arrives during your first real production debug, and everybody in the room sees it at once. That is the moment the abstraction is charged, and it is charged in minutes of downtime rather than in engineering days.

The reversal. Leaving a framework is a rewrite of the control flow and usually of the prompts, because both were shaped by it. This is the most one-way decision in the chapter and the one most often made by whoever wrote the prototype, which is a governance problem before it is a technical one.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A task decomposes cleanly into four steps that are the same every time. Which shape does the published guidance point to, and why?
The stated test is whether the task decomposes cleanly into fixed subtasks, and this one does. The first option treats step count as the trigger, which no source does. The second is the most common real argument and it prices a hypothetical change at the cost of permanent unpredictability. The third confuses what fits in a window with what should be one call, and a four-step task in one call has no place to put a retry.
Your agent takes ten model-mediated steps and each is right about 95 percent of the time. What end-to-end success should you plan for?
0.95 to the tenth power is roughly 0.60, which is the published figure. The second option is the intuition the arithmetic exists to kill: independence is why they multiply, not why they hold. The third invents a correlation the sources do not measure. The fourth assumes a retry knows a step went wrong, and in a chain the usual failure is a plausible wrong answer that no retry is triggered by.
Your design treats waiting for human approval as an error the system recovers from. What breaks first?
Anything modelled as an error inherits your retry and timeout machinery, so a human thinking becomes an outage the platform tries to fix. The first option is unrelated to how the state is modelled. The second is a real capacity problem and it arrives later, from volume rather than from modelling. The fourth is a consequence worth having, and it follows from the duplication rather than causing it.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Your quality bar from chapter 2 is 85 percent end to end. A proposed design has six model-mediated steps, and your measured per-step success is 96 percent.

Work out whether the design can meet the bar, then find the two changes that would, and say which one you would argue for.

Hint

End-to-end success is the per-step rate raised to the number of steps. There are only two variables in that expression, so there are only two changes available.

Show the worked solution
  1. Compute the design as proposed. 0.96 to the sixth power is about 0.78, so the design lands near 78 percent against a bar of 85.
  2. Solve for the per-step rate that would clear the bar at six steps. The sixth root of 0.85 is about 0.973, so every step would have to reach roughly 97.3 percent, which is 1.3 points above what you measured.
  3. Solve for the number of steps that clears the bar at 96 percent. Four steps gives about 0.85, so the design has to lose two hops.
  4. Argue for the one that is achievable. Removing two hops is a design change you control. Raising every step by 1.3 points is a model and prompt project with no stated finish, and chapter 2's curve says the last points are the expensive ones. Prefer making a hop deterministic, which removes it from the multiplication rather than improving its factor.

Sanity check. Your two answers should bracket the proposal: 0.96 to the fourth is above the bar and 0.96 to the sixth is below it, so the crossing point sits between four and five steps. If your arithmetic puts the crossing outside that range, you have raised the wrong number to the wrong power.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to a shape, and the trap in each.

  • "Why is this an agent?" The trap is answering with capability. Answer with the test: the number of steps cannot be predicted or hardcoded.
  • "What is your step budget?" The trap is not having one. Give the number, and the end-to-end success it implies at your measured per-step rate.
  • "What happens while a human is deciding?" The trap is describing the UI. Name the state, and say what your retries and timeouts do while the task is in it.
  • "Why two agents?" The trap is describing the split. Say what you gave up: end-to-end evaluation can no longer localise a regression.
  • "Why this framework?" The trap is listing features. Say what you will do at 80 percent, when getting further means reverse-engineering it.
Your teacher. Take the system you are building and count its model-mediated hops honestly, including the ones inside a library you did not write. Raise your measured per-step rate to that power. Most people find the number lands below the bar they promised. Bring me both numbers and we will work out which hop to make deterministic first.
