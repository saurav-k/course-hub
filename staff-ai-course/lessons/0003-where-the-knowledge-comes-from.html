Module 04 · Knowledge · Chapter 4

More context is not safer, and a vendor published the table.

working 14 min Six decisions about where the facts come from. · OpenAI, Optimizing model accuracy

The one-minute version

  • In OpenAI's own case study, adding retrieval to a fine-tuned model lowered the score by four points. Their words: "RAG actually confounded the model".
  • Diagnose before picking the tool. Missing knowledge is a context problem; wrong format or reasoning is a behaviour problem, and each axis has its own instrument.
  • On financial documents, plain BM25 beat a strong dense embedding model, hybrid beat both, and reranking added more than either.
  • Graph retrieval loses on lookups, wins on sensemaking, and costs roughly three hundred times the tokens per query. An image is a token budget too: one token per patch.

1. Diagnose the failure, then pick the instrument

Retrieval, fine-tuning, a longer window, or a tool call?

The call. Which of four instruments this failure needs, decided by the kind of failure rather than by which instrument the team already owns.

What you must know first. The diagnosis and the counter-example. OpenAI's Icelandic correction case study, on 1,000 examples: zero-shot 62, few-shot 70, fine-tuned smaller model 78, fine-tuned larger model 87, fine-tuned plus retrieval 83. Their words: "RAG actually confounded the model". Source: OpenAI, "Optimizing model accuracy" (DevDay, 6 November 2023). The smaller fine-tuned model beating the larger prompted one is the crux of chapter 6.

The bill. The fine-tuning gate is two conditions: an MVP proving base models insufficient, and clear evidence that unique training data exists. The applied-llms heading for skipping it is "Pre-PMF: no GPUs". Source: Yan et al. (8 June 2024). Mechanism: the papers course.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is quality falling after you add context, which almost nobody tests for, because the reflex says more context cannot hurt. It arrives on the next eval run if you kept the previous scores, and never otherwise.

The reversal. Retrieval is two-way: remove the passages and re-measure. A fine-tune is one-way in practice, because the data, the eval set and the serving path were built around it, and the base model has a retirement date somebody else set.

2. Which retrieval, and how you would know

Vector, keyword, hybrid, or graph?

The call. Which retrieval arms to run and in what combination, and whether graph retrieval earns a place on the questions your users ask.

What you must know first. Two independent studies agreeing on the ladder. Anthropic measured retrieval failure as one minus recall@20 across codebases, fiction and papers: 5.7% baseline, 3.7% with contextual embeddings, 2.9% adding contextual BM25, 1.9% adding reranking, at about $1.02 per million document tokens. Source: Anthropic (19 September 2024), a vendor selling its own model. T2-RAGBench reaches the same ordering elsewhere. Source: Akarsu, Karaman and Mierbach (April 2026).

The bill. Every arm is an index to keep correct, and correctness is the recurring cost. Two published negatives cut the search space first: semantic chunking does not consistently beat fixed-size chunking, and leaderboard rank does not transfer. Sources: Qu, Tu and Bao, Findings of NAACL 2025 and chapter 1.

How you find out you were wrong. There is no signal, and that is the point: a bad chunk size produces plausible answers built on the wrong passages, which read as model failure. Only a domain set of a few hundred query-to-chunk pairs, measured at recall@k, separates the two.

The reversal. Adding an arm is two-way and cheap. Changing chunk size or encoder means re-indexing and re-measuring: a day rather than a deploy, and expensive enough that teams do it once and then defend the result.

3. When retrieval is the wrong answer, and where it lives

When is retrieval the wrong answer entirely?

The call. Whether the failing questions are retrieval failures at all, or a different task in retrieval's clothes.

What you must know first. The measured split. GraphRAG-Bench puts graph methods ahead by roughly ten points on complex reasoning (42.93 against 53.38) and thirteen on contextual summarisation (51.30 against 64.40), and behind on fact retrieval, at about 331,000 prompt tokens per global query against about 900. Source: Xiang et al., GraphRAG-Bench (ICLR 2026). Mechanism: the papers course.

The bill. Graph indexing is model cost and scales with the corpus, not the traffic. The killer is re-indexing: a corpus that changes daily pays daily.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is an answer right about a document and wrong about the corpus, which no retrieval metric scores. It arrives from a user who knew the answer, and nobody sees it in aggregate.

The reversal. Keeping the vector baseline live and routing between the two is the reversible design, and it costs one router. Replacing retrieval with a graph is a re-index and a re-evaluation.

Is this a multimodal problem, and what does that change?

The call. Whether the non-text content is load-bearing enough to pay a per-image token bill, and at what resolution, decided before the product accepts uploads.

What you must know first. That an image is a token budget. A vision transformer spends one token per patch, so the count is the patch grid squared: 196 tokens at 224 pixels with 16-pixel patches, 576 for LLaVA-1.5's encoder at 336. Tile four crops of a page and thousands of tokens go before the question. Sources: Dosovitskiy et al. and LLaVA-1.5; the counts are arithmetic on the patch grid. Mechanism: the papers course.

The bill. Per request, not per corpus: resolution and tiling multiply every call. For generation, the step count is the cost model, which is the one number to hold about diffusion. The research behind this course reads SigLIP 2 as today's default vision tower rather than original CLIP, and flags that as a claim with a shelf life. Source: Tschannen et al.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a cost per request tracking page count rather than question count, on the first bill after a customer starts uploading scans. Finance sees it before engineering. For generation the diagnostic is sharper: washed colours or mushy detail point at the autoencoder, not the sampler.

The reversal. Resolution and tiling are configuration, two-way in an afternoon. Accepting images at all is close to one-way: once users send them, removing the capability is a product regression, and your evaluation set now carries a modality a text-only replacement cannot score.

Do we need a dedicated vector store?

The call. Whether to run a second database, with its own backups, failure modes and on-call, or keep vectors in the one the team already operates.

What you must know first. What the general-purpose option can do. pgvector 0.8.6 ships HNSW and IVFFlat indexes, four vector types to 16,000 dimensions, six distance functions, binary quantization, multi-tenant isolation through partial indexes, and iterative scans that fix filtered search. Source: pgvector. Qdrant's docs are candid about the category's limit: "embeddings won't capture attributes such as price". Source: Qdrant.

Read a vendor benchmark including the columns it loses. Tiger Data compared their own Postgres extension with Qdrant on 50 million 768-dimension embeddings. At 99 percent recall theirs reached 471.57 queries per second against 41.47 - and Qdrant's p95 was 36.73 ms against 60.42, p99 38.71 against 74.60, index build 3.3 hours against 11.1. Which column matters is a property of your workload. Source: Tiger Data (29 April 2025), a vendor benchmarking its own extension.

The bill. A second store is a second backup, a second upgrade path, a second set of alerts and a second thing down at three in the morning. Against that, a join and a transaction disappear when vectors live beside their rows.

How you find out you were wrong. If you kept Postgres, the signal is a ceiling you actually hit: vector count, sustained queries per second, a tail-latency budget. If you added a store, a consistency bug between the copies, seen by whoever debugs a stale answer.

The reversal. Moving between stores is a re-index and a dual-write window: known, finite work. Prefer the option you can name a ceiling for. "We might scale later" is not a ceiling.

4. Whose index is it

Whose permissions does the retrieval run under?

The call. Whether retrieval runs with the asking user's grants or a service identity that reads everything, decided before the index is built.

What you must know first. That the risk has a name. OWASP's 2025 LLM list carries Vector and Embedding Weaknesses as LLM08: recovery of source text from stored vectors, cross-tenant leakage through a shared index, and corpus poisoning. Source: OWASP Gen AI Security Project (12 March 2025). The isolation mechanism is in the build track, the whiteboard version in the interview track.

The bill. Per-user filtering inside the search costs index design and query latency. A shared index with post-filtering costs nothing today and is what chapter 7 defends.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a user quoting something back that they should not have. It arrives without warning, through a channel with a legal address on it, and the first person to see it is not on your team.

The reversal. Retro-fitting per-user grants means re-indexing with tenancy in the schema and re-verifying every historical answer: the most expensive reversal here. A one-way door, usually walked through by accident in week one by whoever wrote the ingestion script.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A fine-tuned model scores 87 on your task. You add retrieval and it scores 83. What does the published case study say is happening?
OpenAI's phrase is that RAG "confounded" the model: this was a behaviour problem, and retrieval is the instrument for a knowledge problem. The first and fourth options are retrieval-quality explanations, which would be the right diagnosis on a knowledge problem and are the wrong axis here. The third is a real failure and it would show as truncation rather than as a four-point drop with the same output shape.
On a corpus of financial filings, a strong dense embedding model is beaten by plain BM25. What is the most likely reason?
Tickers, line-item names and years are exact tokens, and lexical matching is built for them. The second option sounds plausible and the benchmark does not test it. The third is a comparison error rather than a corpus property, and the study ran the unreranked arms against each other deliberately. The fourth is unsupported and would harm both arms rather than only one.
Your users ask "what is the notice period in contract 47" all day. What does the measured evidence say about adding graph retrieval?
These are lookups, and on fact retrieval basic RAG measured ahead of every graph method tested. The first option inverts the finding. The second inverts the cost: global graph search was measured at roughly 331,000 prompt tokens per query against about 900, and the index is re-run whenever the corpus changes. The fourth invents a limitation nothing in the evidence supports.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Your corpus is 40 million document tokens and changes about 5 percent a month. A colleague proposes contextual retrieval, quoting the published failure-rate ladder.

Estimate the one-off and the recurring index cost from the published per-token figure, and say what you would ask for before approving it.

Hint

The published figure is a price per million document tokens with caching. The recurring part is whatever fraction of the corpus changes, re-run at the same rate.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the published unit price. About $1.02 per million document tokens with prompt caching.
  2. Price the one-off pass. 40 million tokens is 40 units, so about 40 x 1.02 = roughly $41 to index the corpus once.
  3. Price the recurring pass. 5 percent of 40 million is 2 million tokens a month, so about 2 x 1.02 = roughly $2 a month, assuming only changed documents are re-processed.
  4. Say what you would ask for. Not the cost, which is trivial, but the measurement: recall@k on a domain set before and after, because the published ladder was measured on codebases, fiction and papers, and chapter 1 says a ranking on one corpus does not transfer to another.

Sanity check. If your monthly figure is anywhere near your one-off figure, you have re-priced the whole corpus rather than the changed 5 percent, which is what happens if the pipeline cannot process documents incrementally. That is a design question worth asking out loud, because it is the difference between $2 and $41 every month.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to a knowledge path, and the trap in each one.

  • "Why fine-tune?" The trap is answering with accuracy. Say which axis the failure sits on.
  • "Did adding retrieval help?" The trap is assuming it did. Show before and after on the same set: the published case study shows it can hurt.
  • "Why this chunk size?" The trap is naming a tutorial. Show recall@k on your own corpus.
  • "What does an image cost us?" The trap is answering per model. Answer per patch grid, at the resolution you actually send.
  • "Do we need the second database?" The trap is answering on features. Name the ceiling you hit, in a unit somebody can measure.
  • "Whose permissions does this run under?" The trap is describing the application's checks. Say whether the grant reaches the index or only the result set.
Your teacher. Take twenty answered questions from your retrieval logs and read the passages beside the answers rather than the scores. Count how many are right for the wrong reason. Nothing in your dashboard measures that, and it decides which decision to re-open first.
