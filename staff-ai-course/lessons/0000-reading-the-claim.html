Module 01 · Evidence · Chapter 1

A number measures a harness, not a model.

foundation 13 min Five decisions about evidence, because every later chapter spends it. · Berkeley RDI, April 2026

The one-minute version

  • Semgrep published a headline saying one model beat another, then spent the post taking it apart. Their conclusion: the harness matters more than the model, and the largest gap was between configurations.
  • A leaderboard is a measurement instrument with its own sampling process, and that process has been quantified. Selecting on rank delegates your most consequential architectural decision to it.
  • Eight prominent agent benchmarks were driven to near-perfect scores without solving a single task. Berkeley RDI's rule: do not trust the number, trust the methodology.
  • METR published a famous productivity number in 2025, then published in 2026 why it can no longer be read that way. A result has an expiry date, and somebody has to own it.

1. What the number is actually a measurement of

Evan Miller's framing holds for the whole course: "Fundamentally, evaluations are experiments; but the literature on evaluations has largely ignored the literature from other sciences on experiment analysis and planning." An experiment has a treatment, a sample and a protocol. Most published AI numbers name only the treatment. Source: Evan Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (arXiv:2411.00640, submitted 1 November 2024).

Is this number about the model, or about the harness around it?

The call. Whether to read a comparison as evidence about a component you could swap in on Friday, or about a pipeline you would have to build first. Those lead to opposite decisions, and the headline rarely says which you are holding.

What you must know first. What each competitor was given. Semgrep published "GLM 5.2 beats Claude in our cyber benchmarks" on 22 June 2026: 39% F1 against Claude Code's 32% on one IDOR detection task, at about 0.17 USD per vulnerability found. Their own qualification is that the open-weight models got "nothing but a prompt" while their own pipeline ran inside a purpose-built harness, so the post "is not an apples-to-apples comparison of raw model ability". Source: Semgrep, Paxton-Fear, Jaksik, Noblitt and Buchanan (22 June 2026).

The bill. Half an hour finding the harness paragraph, which is never in the summary. Against that, adopting a model on a harness result buys a migration, a prompt rewrite and a scaffolding project you did not scope.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is that your own numbers do not move after the swap, about one sprint after the model reaches production. Whoever runs the internal eval sees it first, not whoever chose the model, and that gap costs another cycle.

The reversal. Two-way and cheap while the model is one configuration value behind a gateway. It stops being cheap once the harness has been reshaped around one vendor's tool-calling behaviour, usually by the second sprint.

What was the sample, and who chose what to report?

The call. Whether a public ranking is admissible evidence for your model choice at all, when you can see the scores and not the process behind them.

What you must know first. That the process has been measured. On Chatbot Arena, The Leaderboard Illusion estimates Google at 19.2% and OpenAI at 20.4% of total arena data while a combined 83 open-weight models received an estimated 29.7%. It reports 27 private variants tested by one provider before a release, with selective disclosure, and gains of up to 112% from arena data on the arena distribution. Source: Singh et al., "The Leaderboard Illusion" (arXiv:2504.20879, submitted 29 April 2025, revised 12 May 2025).

The bill. Nothing, if the answer is to stop using rank as evidence. The real bill is the alternative: your own evaluation set, which chapter 5 prices as a staffed function rather than a one-off.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a model that ranks well and loses to a cheaper one on your traffic. It arrives when somebody finally runs the head-to-head, which in most teams is never, because the ranking settled the argument. This failure has no natural discovery date.

The reversal. Reversing the model is easy. Reversing the habit is not: once a leaderboard decides one architectural question it decides the next three, and the team stops building the instrument that would contradict it.

2. Whether the measurement can be gamed, and whether it transfers

Is this benchmark exploitable, and has anyone checked?

The call. Whether a benchmark score is evidence of capability or evidence that the scoring harness can be satisfied without the capability.

What you must know first. That this has been tested and the answer was ugly. Berkeley RDI drove eight prominent agent benchmarks to near-perfect scores without solving any task: SWE-bench Verified and Pro, WebArena, Terminal-Bench, FieldWorkArena, OSWorld, GAIA and CAR-bench. A pytest hook forcing every test to report as passing beats SWE-bench. A file:// URL reads WebArena's gold answers out of the task configuration. FieldWorkArena's validator checked only whether the last message came from the assistant. Their rule: "Don't trust the number. Trust the methodology." Source: Wang, Mang, Cheung, Sen and Song, UC Berkeley RDI (April 2026).

The bill. One engineer, one afternoon, reading the scorer rather than the score. On your own suite the same audit is cheaper still and nobody runs it, which is how a suite drifts into measuring whether the pipeline returned a well-formed answer.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a suite that only goes up. A benchmark nothing has failed in two months is one you have optimised against. Whoever triages production complaints sees that months before the dashboard does.

The reversal. Free for a public benchmark you can simply stop citing. Expensive for an internal one, because everything shipped since it was written passed it, and rewriting the scorer means re-adjudicating every one of those releases.

Does the number survive on our task, and what does finding out cost?

The call. Whether to treat a general benchmark's ranking as a prior for your corpus, or spend the half-day that produces a ranking on your own data.

What you must know first. That non-transfer has been measured. Tang and Yang tested seven state-of-the-art embedding models on FinMTEB, a financial-domain benchmark, and found MTEB performance not correlated with FinMTEB performance. Leaderboard rank tells you close to nothing about rank on your corpus. Source: Tang and Yang, "Do We Need Domain-Specific Embedding Models?" (arXiv:2409.18511, submitted 27 September 2024, revised 18 February 2025). Chapter 4 turns this into a build instruction.

The bill. A few hundred labelled items from your own traffic: a half-day for one engineer, plus an hour of domain expertise. Skipping it is why so many retrieval systems are tuned by folklore, and the folklore is billed monthly.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a quality complaint that survives every configuration change, because configuration was never the problem. It reaches support before monitoring, and takes about a quarter to be recognised as one cause rather than five tickets.

The reversal. Two-way at the component level. The reason to spend the half-day is not that swapping back is hard, it is that without a domain set you never learn you should.

3. When the result stops being true

When does a published result stop being usable, and who is watching?

The call. Whether the result you are about to build an argument on is current, and who owns noticing when it stops being.

What you must know first. The canonical pair. In July 2025 METR reported 16 experienced open-source developers 19% slower on 246 tasks in their own mature repositories when allowed AI tools, while believing themselves 20% faster and having forecast a 24% speedup. In February 2026 METR published that they are changing the design, because a later round suffered selection effects. Sources: METR (10 July 2025) and METR (24 February 2026).

The honest limit, and why the pair is here. METR's own words about their newer numbers are "our data is only very weak evidence for the size of this increase", and their 2025 report carries an explicit list of what the result is not evidence for, including that "AI systems do not currently speed up many or most software developers". Quoting the 19% figure without the 2026 update is the failure this chapter exists to prevent, and it is easy to do by accident: the 2025 post is still live and still correct about what it measured.

The bill. One named owner and a recurring calendar entry. The cost is attention rather than money, and it is the cheapest line in the course: a quarterly re-read of the five sources your architecture rests on.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is somebody in a review producing the newer version of your own citation. It arrives in public, in front of the people you were persuading, and the credibility cost lands on you rather than on the decision.

The reversal. The citation is trivially replaceable. What is not reversible is the decision it carried, which is why this belongs in chapter zero: an expiry date is a property of the decision, not of the footnote.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A vendor post reports one model at 39 percent F1 and another at 32 percent on a single detection task, and states that the weaker one was given only a prompt while theirs ran inside a purpose-built pipeline. What is the number evidence about?
Only one variable was genuinely varied, and it was not the model. The first option is the mistake the headline invites: same task does not mean same treatment. The third answers a real and different question, about sample size, which is the next audit rather than this one. The fourth is true and irrelevant to what the comparison establishes, because the cost was incurred by the harness too.
Your internal agent benchmark has not failed a release in two months, and the team reads that as the pipeline being stable. What is the more likely reading?
A benchmark nothing fails has stopped separating candidates, which is exactly what Berkeley RDI demonstrated at the extreme by driving eight benchmarks to near-perfect scores without solving a task. The first option confuses a larger sample with a discriminating one. The second is a real failure taught in chapter 5 and would show as scores rising, not as failures vanishing. The third is the one benign reading, and a suite that tracks a moving distribution gets harder rather than easier.
You want to cite a 2025 productivity study whose authors published a redesign in 2026. What does this course require you to do?
The pair is the teaching artefact: publish the number, publish the conditions, publish the change when the conditions change. The second option overstates what happened, because a redesign is not a retraction and the 2025 measurement stands for what it measured. The third is the failure this chapter names. The fourth throws away the best-documented evidence in the field because it is honest about itself, which would leave you citing only work that never revisited its own method.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A colleague brings you a slide: "our new prompt raised the pass rate from 82.9 to 84.2 percent on our 164-question suite, so we should ship it." You have not yet read chapter 5, but you have the one number this chapter gave you.

Work out whether that 1.3-point difference is distinguishable from nothing, using the standard error a suite of this size carries. Then say what you would ask for instead of the slide.

Hint

An evaluation is an experiment, so the score is an estimate and estimates have standard errors. Compare the size of the reported movement with the size of the error on a single measurement of that suite.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the standard error the source gives for a suite this size. Miller's illustrative reporting table puts an evaluation of 164 questions, which is HumanEval's size, at a standard error of about 3.2%.
  2. Compare the movement with the error. The reported difference is 84.2 minus 82.9, which is 1.3 points. That is well under half of one standard error on either measurement.
  3. State the conclusion in the form the meeting needs. The suite cannot resolve a difference this small. The slide reports a movement, not a result.
  4. Name what you would ask for. The per-question paired comparison rather than two averages, and the number of questions the suite would need to detect the effect size the team actually cares about. Both are chapter 5, and both are asked for before the run rather than after it.

Sanity check. If a 1.3-point move were resolvable on 164 questions, so would a 0.6-point move be, and the team would be shipping on differences of one question in 164. One question is about 0.6 points on this suite, which is the reductio: the arithmetic has to say no, or it says yes to noise.

Practice 2working

You are handed a vendor blog post claiming a retrieval technique cuts failures by two thirds. Before you read a word of the method, write the audit.

List the five questions from this chapter in the order you would ask them, and for each one name the specific published case that shows what happens when nobody asks it.

Show the worked solution
  1. Model or harness. What did each arm actually get? Semgrep, whose largest gap was between configurations rather than models.
  2. Sample and selection. How many items, and who chose which runs became public? The Leaderboard Illusion, where one provider tested 27 private variants with selective disclosure.
  3. Exploitable. Could the scorer be satisfied without the capability? Berkeley RDI, where a validator checked only whether the last message came from the assistant.
  4. Transfer. Does the ranking hold on my corpus? Tang and Yang, where MTEB rank did not predict FinMTEB rank across seven models.
  5. Expiry. Have the authors revisited it? METR, who changed their own experiment design and said their newer data is only very weak evidence for the size of the effect.

Sanity check. The order matters and it is not arbitrary: the first two questions can invalidate the number outright, the third can invalidate the instrument, and the last two only tell you whether a valid number applies to you. An audit that starts at transfer has already conceded the first three.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to a decision made on evidence, and the trap in each.

  • "Where did that number come from?" The trap is naming the publisher. Answer with the harness, the sample, the runs, and what the authors said it does not show.
  • "It is the top-ranked model, what more do you want?" The trap is arguing the leaderboard is wrong. It is not wrong. It is an instrument with a measured sampling process, and your task is not its distribution.
  • "Our suite has been green for two months." The trap is hearing that as stability. Ask when it last failed something, and what changed in it since.
  • "No time for a domain eval set." The trap is agreeing on the cost. Half a day, against a folklore-tuned pipeline billed monthly forever.
  • "That study is well known." The trap is treating fame as currency. Ask who owns re-reading it, and what the authors have published since.
Your teacher. Take the last AI number you repeated to somebody more senior than you and run the five audits on it before chapter 2. Usually the number survives and the sentence wrapped around it does not. Bring me the sentence and we will find which audit it failed.
