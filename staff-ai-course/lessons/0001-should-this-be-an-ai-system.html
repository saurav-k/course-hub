Module 02 · Restraint · Chapter 2

The demo you just watched is the first eighty percent.

foundation 13 min Five decisions before a model is chosen. · Zinkevich, Rules of Machine Learning

The one-minute version

  • Google's oldest published rule here is rule one: do not be afraid to launch without machine learning. The trigger for a model is a heuristic that became harder to maintain than a model, not a problem that is interesting.
  • No published practitioner claims a hallucination-free grounded system. The reported floor is 5 to 10 percent factual inconsistency even after retrieval, so somebody has to be named as the person who eats it.
  • LinkedIn reached 80 percent in a month and 95 in five. Airbnb reached 75 percent in four hours and 97 in four days. A mechanical verifier is the difference, and it decides whether the last stretch is ruinous.
  • Nobody publishes the retreat. A search for a named company that removed an LLM from production found nothing, so weight the restraint cases you have more heavily than their count.

1. Whether a model belongs here at all

Is this a foundation-model problem, a classical machine-learning problem, or a rules problem?

The call. Which rung this feature starts on. Starting one rung too high is the chapter's most expensive mistake, and it stays invisible for a quarter because the demo works.

What you must know first. The triggers, published and old. Zinkevich's rule 1 is "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning", closing with "don't use it until you have data". Rule 3 gives the exit: adopt a model once the heuristic is unmaintainable. Rule 7 says turn the heuristics you replace into features. Source: Martin Zinkevich, "Rules of Machine Learning" (Google). For what a classical model is, see Probability You Build.

The bill. An afternoon. Discord publish the list they fill in before prototyping: latency, task complexity, prompt length, quality bar, safety, language support and queries per second. Their framing is the sentence for the room: generative AI "shouldn't be applied when other tools are a better fit". Source: Shannon Phu, Discord (12 April 2024).

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is an evaluation set filling with cases a regular expression would have caught, around the second cost review. Whoever labels failures sees it first, and nobody escalates: by then the model is the feature.

The reversal. One rung down is a rewrite, not a rollback: prompt, harness, index and latency budget were built for the higher rung. A quarter of work for a permanently lower bill, which is why almost nobody does it.

What error rate is acceptable, who bears it, and is that number written down first?

The call. The quality bar, agreed in writing with whoever owns the outcome, before a prototype exists to anchor expectations.

What you must know first. Where the floor is. Eugene Yan reports factual inconsistency running 5 to 10% even after grounding through retrieval, with industry estimates that below 2% is "prohibitively hard". Nobody publishing under their own name claims a hallucination-free grounded system. Source: Eugene Yan, "Task-Specific LLM Evals that Do and Don't Work" (March 2024).

The bill. The bar sets everything downstream, and closing the gap is rarely model work. LinkedIn drove YAML parsing errors from about 10% to 0.01% with defensive parsing, on a model producing correct API parameters about 90% of the time. Source: Bottaro and R., LinkedIn (25 April 2024).

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is the argument about whether a given output counts as a failure, starting the first week real users arrive. Support and the product owner see it, in a thread you are not on. A bar agreed after that argument starts is a negotiation.

The reversal. Cheap to renegotiate behind a flag. After launch it is not: the bar becomes whatever users were shown, and lowering it is a product regression even when the number was always aspirational.

2. Buy, build, wait, and what the last stretch costs

Buy, build, or wait for the next model generation?

The call. Whether the capability is generic enough to purchase, specific enough to build, or simply too expensive this quarter and cheap enough next year to defer in writing.

What you must know first. Both sides. Six named practitioners state the default as "Don't Build LLM Features You Can Buy", listing custom text-to-SQL, documentation chatbots and knowledge-base support, and add "The model isn't the product, the system around it is." Source: Yan, Bischof, Frye, Husain, Liu and Shankar (8 June 2024). Ramp is the counter-case: they rejected vendors because "Ramp has unique needs with complex data", then constrained the output to a code list so the prediction became multiple choice. Source: Ramp engineering (15 January 2025).

Read the counter-case with its limits. The Ramp page carries no author name and publishes relative gains only: up to 60% in acc@k from retrieval work and 5 to 15% fuzzy-accuracy improvement at selection, with no absolute accuracy anywhere. Good evidence that constraining the output space works, weak evidence about how well the system performs.

The bill. Building costs the feature plus permanent maintenance. Waiting costs the quarter, and applied-llms argue that is often the right price: capability has improved at roughly six-month cost-halving intervals, so read "too expensive" as time-limited and write the date you re-check.

How you find out you were wrong. If you bought, the signal is a support queue full of things the vendor will not change, one renewal cycle later. If you built, it is maintenance creeping past a quarter of team capacity, which nobody sees unless somebody measures it.

The reversal. Buying stays two-way while your data has not moved into the vendor's schema. Building is one-way in practice: the code can be deleted, the two quarters cannot, and the team will defend it.

What does the last twenty percent cost, and are we willing to pay it?

The call. Whether to commit to a target the demo does not reach, knowing what closing that gap cost teams who published their numbers.

What you must know first. Both published curves. LinkedIn: 80% in one month, then four more months to 95%, because "that last 20% took most of our work". Airbnb migrated nearly 3,500 test files against a manual estimate of 1.5 years, in 6 weeks: 75% in four hours, 97% after four days of sample-tune-and-sweep, the last 3% a further week by hand. Sources: LinkedIn (25 April 2024) and Covey-Brandt, Airbnb (2025, page undated).

The bill. Ask the verifier question in the first design meeting: what tells us an output is wrong without a person reading it? If the answer is "a human reads it", budget LinkedIn's curve, which meant 500 conversations a day assessed by linguists. Airbnb's prompts carried 40,000 to 100,000 tokens, which is a retrieval design rather than a prompt.

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is the review where somebody asks why quality has not moved in three weeks, about a month after the demo. The engineer grinding the tail sees it first and reads it as personal failure. LinkedIn's own note: progress was "discouraging".

The reversal. Lower the target, which is somebody else's decision, or build the verifier late, which costs more than building it first and is the only move that changes the slope. Abandoning at 80 percent is cheapest and hardest to get agreed, because the demo has been shown.

3. The exit you have to design in advance

What would make us stop?

The call. The stop conditions, written before the first prototype, beside the quality bar. Not a risk register: two or three observations that would end the project, agreed by whoever accepts the ending.

What you must know first. That this is what a frontier lab buys at staff level. Anthropic's Staff Software Engineer, Labs: Applied AI posting asks for "8+ years of experience building full-stack applications", no machine-learning depth, and that you "Can articulate learnings from failed or killed projects without defensiveness". Source: Anthropic job board, quoted as a posting live in August 2026, because a posting is pulled when it is filled.

The bill. An hour of a meeting nobody wants. Honeycomb published what they chose not to do - no chat interface, no database access, no agents in their infrastructure - and named the gates on the critical path, including audits where "only OpenAI could meet our requirements". Source: Phillip Carter, Honeycomb (26 May 2023, modified 26 August 2024).

How you find out you were wrong. The signal is a project three weeks from ready for two quarters. Everyone sees it, nobody can name the observation that would end it, so it ends by budget instead, which costs a person rather than a decision.

The reversal. None in the usual sense. A stop condition agreed in advance is the cheapest thing here, and one invented afterwards is a post mortem. Restarting a killed project is normal and cheap. Un-shipping one that was never stopped is what the next two quarters are for.

An absence worth teaching. The research behind this course searched for published accounts of a team removing an LLM from production and going back to rules, and found none from a named company. The field publishes its adoptions and not its retreats. Do not read that absence as evidence that nobody retreats.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A team proposes replacing a large, gnarly rules engine with a model. Which argument actually clears Zinkevich's bar for moving up a rung?
Rule 3 names maintenance cost, not difficulty, as the trigger. The first option is the argument the rules explicitly reject: hard is not a reason. The second is a hope rather than an observation, and it is the one that produces an evaluation set full of regular-expression cases. The fourth is a precondition and not a trigger, and having the stack available is exactly how a team ends up one rung too high.
Two projects both reach the high seventies on their first pass. One closes the gap in four days, the other takes four months. What best explains the difference?
A migrated test either passes or it does not, which is what made the retry loop cheap and the last stretch affordable. The first option is the usual guess and neither source supports it. The third is true of the fast project, whose prompts ran to 40,000 to 100,000 tokens, and it is a consequence of having a verifier rather than the cause of the speed. The fourth is a real failure taught in chapter 5, and it would hide a change rather than slow one down.
Your search for published accounts of teams that removed an LLM from production and went back to rules turns up nothing. What follows?
An absence in the published record is evidence about what gets published, not about what happens. The first option reads publication bias as a base rate, which is the error. The second overcorrects in the opposite direction and claims knowledge the absence cannot supply. The third throws away a finding worth stating precisely because it is a tested negative rather than an untested assumption.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A product owner wants an assistant that answers policy questions from your internal handbook, and wants it at 99 percent correctness. The handbook is 400 pages and changes weekly.

Work out whether the target is reachable from published evidence, and write the two sentences you would put in the document instead of "we will aim for 99 percent".

Hint

Start from the published floor for factual inconsistency in a grounded system, then ask the verifier question. There is no arithmetic to do until you know whether a machine can decide correctness here.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the reported floor. Factual inconsistency typically runs 5 to 10% even after grounding through retrieval, and going below 2% is reported as prohibitively hard. So 99 percent correctness sits below the reported floor by a factor of at least two.
  2. Ask the verifier question. Nothing decides whether a policy answer is right except a person who knows the policy. That is LinkedIn's curve, not Airbnb's, so the last stretch is priced in human review.
  3. Price the review. LinkedIn's published answer at their scale was 500 conversations a day assessed by linguist specialists. Whatever your scale, this is a standing role and not a launch task.
  4. Write the two sentences. First: the achievable target is an error rate inside the published band, and the feature is viable only if the product survives one wrong answer in twenty. Second: correctness is not machine-checkable here, so review is a permanent staffed function, and if it is not funded the target is not a target.

Sanity check. If your answer lets the product owner keep 99 percent, re-read step 1: you have promised something no practitioner in the published record claims on a grounded system, and the weekly handbook change makes the retrieval index a moving target on top of it.

Decision drill

Questions a reviewer puts to a build decision, and the trap in each.

  • "Why a model and not a rule?" The trap is answering with difficulty. Answer with the maintenance cost of the rules you replace.
  • "What is the quality bar?" The trap is quoting the demo. Answer with a number agreed in writing, and say who eats the errors below it.
  • "Can we just buy this?" The trap is defending the build on capability. Defend it on your data not being generic, and accept the burden of proof.
  • "How long to production?" The trap is promising the first month. Answer the verifier question first: it decides which curve you are on.
  • "What would make you stop?" The trap is naming a risk. Name an observation, with a threshold, agreed by whoever would accept the ending.
Your teacher. Take a feature your team is building now and write its stop condition in one sentence, with a number in it. If you cannot, the useful thing is the reason. Bring me that, and we will find whether it is a missing metric, owner or decision.

Primary source to go deeper

Read Zinkevich's "Rules of Machine Learning" first: it predates large language models, which is why it is not selling you anything. Then read "What We've Learned From A Year of Building with LLMs" for build against buy.
