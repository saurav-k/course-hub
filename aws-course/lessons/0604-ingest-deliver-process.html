Holding a stream, delivering it, and computing over it are three services

working 7 min Capability keys stream-ingest and stream-analytics. · Kinesis Data Streams

The one-minute version

  • Kinesis Data Streams holds records. Its unit is the shard: 1 MB per second or 1,000 records per second in, and 2 MB per second out shared across up to five read calls a second.
  • Adding consumers does not add read bandwidth. Enhanced fan-out does, giving each consumer its own dedicated 2 MB per second.
  • Amazon Data Firehose delivers rather than holds: S3, Redshift, OpenSearch, Iceberg tables and third-party endpoints, converting to Parquet or ORC and partitioning on the way.
  • MSK is managed Kafka for estates that want Kafka's own semantics, in a provisioned form with Express brokers or a serverless form that accepts IAM authentication only.
  • Managed Service for Apache Flink is the compute: windowed SQL, the DataStream and Table APIs, checkpointed state and exactly-once sinks, bought in Kinesis Processing Units.

1. The mental model: three jobs, three services

2. The mechanism: what one shard is worth

Two more numbers shape designs here. A single record may be up to 10 MiB before encoding, which is large enough that a stream is occasionally used for payloads it should not be. And retention runs from 24 hours to 365 days, charged first as extra shard-hours and then per gigabyte-month, which turns "keep it just in case" into a line on the bill.

3. Delivering, and computing

Both streaming services carry a rename or a retirement worth knowing. Kinesis Data Firehose is now Amazon Data Firehose, and Kinesis Data Analytics is now Managed Service for Apache Flink, whose legacy SQL application type has been retired - new work uses Flink SQL, Java, Python or Scala, with capacity bought in Kinesis Processing Units and a Studio notebook mode for interactive development (Managed Service for Apache Flink).

MSK is the alternative to all of this for an estate that wants Kafka itself: a provisioned form whose Express brokers are claimed at three times the throughput per broker and twenty times faster scaling, and a serverless form which accepts IAM authentication only - a constraint that decides the choice outright for a client that cannot use it (MSK Serverless).

The reframing A stream is not a queue with history. It is a sized pipe with a stated read budget, and every consumer you attach spends from that budget unless you buy each one its own. Design the consumer list first: it is the number that decides both the shard count and whether enhanced fan-out is optional.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A third consumer is added to a Kinesis stream and the two existing ones begin to lag. Why?
A shard's 2 MB per second of read capacity is shared across standard consumers, so a third reader takes a share from the first two. Consumers do not block one another by acknowledgement, ingest capacity is stated separately from read capacity, and retention length changes cost rather than read speed.
Records must land in S3 as Parquet in partitioned paths, with nothing to replay later. Which service fits?
A delivery stream converts to Parquet or ORC and writes into dynamic partitions, which is precisely the requirement, and nothing needs replaying. The two log services exist to be replayed, which the requirement says is unnecessary, and Flink is compute rather than a delivery path.
A Kafka client cannot use IAM credentials. What does that rule out?
The serverless form of MSK accepts IAM authentication only, so a client that cannot use it needs the provisioned form. MSK as a whole is not ruled out, Express brokers are a provisioned broker type rather than an authentication mode, and the schema registry is not what the client authenticates against to produce or consume.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A telemetry pipeline takes 6 MB per second of device records at peak. Four consumers read it: a fraud detector needing records within a second, a lake loader, a dashboard feed, and a replay tool used during incidents that reads the last three days as fast as it can.

Size the stream in shards, decide which consumers need dedicated read capacity, and name the one consumer that probably should not be on this stream at all.

Hint

Do the write side first, from the ingest rate alone. Then check the read side against the same shard count, remembering that the shared budget is per shard and is divided among the standard consumers rather than granted to each.

Show the worked solution
  1. Size for ingest. A shard takes 1 MB per second, so 6 MB per second needs at least six shards, assuming the partition key spreads records evenly. Uneven keys need more, exactly as in lesson 0502.
  2. Check the shared read budget. Six shards give 12 MB per second of shared read in total. Three standard consumers each reading the full 6 MB per second stream would want 18 MB per second between them, which is more than the shard count provides.
  3. Give the latency-sensitive consumer its own capacity. The fraud detector needs records within a second and cannot afford to queue behind the others, so it gets enhanced fan-out and its own dedicated 2 MB per second per shard.
  4. Decide the rest. With the fraud detector off the shared budget, the lake loader and dashboard feed share 12 MB per second across six shards. That fits, but with little headroom, so either a seventh shard or a second enhanced-fan-out consumer is the cheaper insurance depending on which consumer is more sensitive to lag.
  5. Name the odd one out. The replay tool. It reads three days of history as fast as it can, which is a burst that competes with live consumers for the same shared budget at exactly the moment - an incident - when the live consumers matter most. Land the records in the lake through a delivery stream and let the replay tool read objects from S3, where its bandwidth is nobody else's problem.

Sanity check. Your shard count should be at least six and driven by ingest, and your answer should move exactly one consumer off the stream for a reason about contention rather than about cost. If you sized the stream from the number of consumers, you did the read side first and the write side never constrained anything.
