Three things can tell you something is wrong, and one of them can act

working 7 min Capability keys alerting, dashboards, synthetic-monitoring and service-health-dashboard. · CloudWatch alarms

The one-minute version

  • An alarm is not a threshold check. It is a state that changes when a condition is sustained, which is what stops a single noisy data point paging anybody.
  • Composite alarms combine child alarms with AND, OR and NOT, which is the mechanism for turning an alarm storm into one page.
  • Alarm actions reach further than notification: scaling groups, instance recovery, functions and operational items are all valid targets.
  • A canary is a scripted probe that runs as often as once a minute, takes screenshots and emits heartbeat metrics, so a failure is noticed even when no real user is looking.
  • What it costs: the Health feed tells you about AWS's own events and names your affected resources, but its API is gated behind a Business or higher support plan.

1. The mechanism: an alarm is a state

2. A user who is always there

The third source is AWS itself. The Health feed is account-scoped and names the resources of yours that an event affects, rather than describing a Region in general, and an organisational view aggregates every account. Events can be routed to the platform event bus for automation. Two access notes matter: the Health API needs a Business, Enterprise On-Ramp or Enterprise support plan, while the public status page needs no account at all (AWS Health).

3. Dashboards, and what they can honestly show

Dashboards are widgets over metrics, alarms and log queries, and they carry two properties worth knowing before an escalation. They render metrics down to one-minute resolution, or ten seconds where the underlying metric is high-resolution, so a dashboard cannot show a finer picture than the metric behind it. And a dashboard is a regional object: a cross-account or cross-region view is arranged through sharing or cross-account observability rather than being the default (CloudWatch dashboards).

The reframing Sort your monitoring by who notices first. Your own metrics notice load problems; a canary notices that the thing is broken for users; the Health feed notices that the problem is not yours at all. An estate missing any one of the three has a class of incident it will always learn about from a customer.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An alarm pages every night during a batch job that is expected to be slow. What is the cleanest fix?
A composite alarm can suppress on a known condition, which keeps the original sensitivity for every other hour of the day. Raising the threshold blinds the alarm to real problems, deleting it removes the signal entirely, and shortening the periods makes it fire more readily rather than less.
Which source notices that a sign-in page is broken when no customers are online?
A synthetic probe generates its own traffic, so it fails whether or not anyone is using the service. An error-rate metric needs requests to exist, the Health feed reports AWS's events rather than your application's, and a dashboard needs somebody looking at it.
What limits how quickly a canary can detect a failure?
A canary cannot run more often than once a minute, so that is the floor on its detection time. Alarms have no five-minute floor of their own here, screenshots are an artefact of the run rather than a delay in front of the metric, and being regional affects where it runs rather than how often.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A checkout service has one alarm: error rate above one percent for one period. It pages during every deployment, during a nightly reconciliation job, and occasionally at random. It did not page during a two-hour outage in which the load balancer returned errors before requests ever reached the application, and nobody noticed an AWS event affecting the database until a customer complained.

Redesign the monitoring, saying what you change about the existing alarm, what you add, and which of the three sources each failure in the story exposes as missing.

Hint

Three symptoms, three different causes: one is about how the alarm evaluates, one is about who generates the traffic, and one is about who owns the problem. Deal with them in that order.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fix the random pages first. One period is a single data point, so any spike pages. Require the condition to be sustained across several periods, which removes the random pages without touching the threshold.
  2. Fix the known events. Deployments and the nightly job are expected, so express them as child alarms and build a composite alarm that fires on the error condition AND NOT during either. The original sensitivity is preserved for every other hour.
  3. Name the first missing source and add it. The outage produced no application errors because requests never arrived, so a metric emitted by the application could never see it. A canary that walks the checkout journey from outside would have failed on the first run, and its heartbeat metric is what the alarm should also watch.
  4. Name the second missing source and add it. An AWS event affecting the database is not your metric and not your canary - it is the Health feed. Route those events to the platform event bus so the on-call channel receives them automatically, and remember that programmatic access to that feed depends on the support plan.
  5. Say what each failure exposed. The random pages exposed an evaluation problem. The unnoticed outage exposed a missing synthetic source. The unnoticed AWS event exposed a missing platform source. Only the first was ever an alarm-tuning question.

Sanity check. Your redesign should still contain the original error-rate condition rather than replacing it, and you should have added exactly two new sources. If you fixed the false pages by raising the threshold, check what that does to the one percent the business actually cares about.
