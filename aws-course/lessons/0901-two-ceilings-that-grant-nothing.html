One guardrail caps your callers, the other caps everyone else's

working 7 min Capability key org-guardrail-policy. · Service control policies

The one-minute version

  • Neither policy grants anything. Both are ceilings: they can only remove from what identity policies already allow.
  • A service control policy caps what principals your organisation manages may do - including each member account's root user.
  • A resource control policy caps what may be done to your resources regardless of who is calling, including principals outside your organisation. That is the gap SCPs cannot cover.
  • Both skip the same two things: the management account and service-linked roles. Resource control policies also skip AWS-managed KMS keys.
  • What it costs: ten SCPs of 10,240 characters and five RCPs of 5,120 characters per entity, and a non-detachable full-access default sits in both sets.

1. The mental model: two sides of one request

2. The gap only one of them closes

Both policy types skip service-linked roles, and the resource control policy documentation says so in as many words: they do not affect the effective permissions of any service-linked role, and a service-linked role's trust policy is unaffected too. Both also skip the management account, and resource control policies additionally skip AWS-managed KMS keys. A guardrail that appears not to be working is very often meeting one of these four exclusions.

3. The budget you are actually writing into

The reframing Stop reading these as "org-level IAM". They are two ceilings over two different populations: one over the people and machines you employ, one over anybody in the world who points at your resources. Most data-perimeter requirements need both, and writing only the first is the most common way an organisation believes it has a perimeter that it does not have.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A partner's role in another organisation reads one of your buckets. Which guardrail can stop it?
Service control policies apply only to principals the organisation manages, so an outside caller is untouched by them. Resource control policies cap what may be done to your resources whoever is asking, which is exactly this case, so the last two answers are both wrong in opposite directions.
A guardrail denying an action appears to have no effect on one identity. What should you check first?
Both guardrail types skip service-linked roles, which is one of the documented exclusions and the usual explanation. An identity allow cannot override a ceiling, an over-length policy fails to save rather than applying silently, and Region has no bearing on whether a guardrail applies.
What happens to permissions in an account whose parent unit's guardrail omits a service?
An account receives only what every unit above it allows, so an omission at a parent removes the service below. Identity policies cannot exceed the ceiling, inheritance is continuous rather than applied once, and the root user of a member account is explicitly covered by service control policies.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A security team is asked to guarantee three things across the organisation: nobody inside the company may disable logging, no company data in S3 may be read by a principal outside the organisation, and workloads must not be created outside two approved Regions. They plan to write all three as service control policies attached to the root.

Review the plan, say which of the three that instrument cannot deliver, and name two exclusions that could still leave a hole in the parts it can.

Hint

For each requirement, ask whose principal is being constrained. One of the three is about callers you do not employ, which decides the instrument immediately.

Show the worked solution
  1. Disabling logging - a service control policy, correctly. The population being constrained is the company's own principals, which is precisely the caller-side ceiling. Note that this also covers each member account's root user, which is the part that makes it a real guarantee rather than an administrative convention.
  2. Region restriction - a service control policy, correctly. Again the constrained population is your own principals. Write it as a condition on the requested Region rather than by enumerating services, or the character budget will run out long before the policy is complete.
  3. Outside readers of S3 - not a service control policy. An outside principal is not managed by your organisation, so the caller-side ceiling says nothing about them. This requirement needs a resource control policy, and S3 is one of the roughly forty service prefixes covered.
  4. Name the first exclusion. The management account. Neither ceiling applies to it, so all three guarantees are false inside it - which is a second reason nothing should run there.
  5. Name the second exclusion. Service-linked roles. Both policy types skip them, so a service acting through one is outside the guarantee. Add that AWS-managed KMS keys are outside resource control policies too, which matters if any part of the data-perimeter argument rests on key access.

Sanity check. Exactly one of the three requirements should change instrument, and your exclusions should be about who the policy does not reach rather than about how it is written. If you proposed denying the outside partner by naming their account in a service control policy, re-read which principals that policy type governs.
