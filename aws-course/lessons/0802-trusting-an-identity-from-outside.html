Module 08 · Identity and access · Lesson 0802

A trust policy is the only door in, and one word in it is misread constantly

working 7 min Capability keys cross-account-assumption and workload-identity-federation. · External ID

The one-minute version

  • Four kinds of outsider assume a role the same way: another account, an OIDC issuer such as a CI system, a SAML provider, and your own certificate authority through IAM Roles Anywhere.
  • The word root in a trust policy principal does not mean the root user. An account ARN ending in :root delegates to the entire account, and a bare account ID behaves identically.
  • External ID defends against the confused deputy when one intermediary serves many customers. Its protection is uniqueness, not secrecy.
  • Where an AWS service acts on your behalf, the condition keys aws:SourceArn, aws:SourceAccount and aws:PrincipalOrgID do the same job.
  • What it costs: the delegating account writes the trust policy and the calling account still needs its own identity policy allowing the assumption - both halves, as always.

1. The mechanism: four issuers, one role

The most misread string in AWS. A trust policy principal of arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root does not restrict access to that account's root user. It delegates to the entire account: any principal in it whose own identity policy allows the assumption may use the role, and a bare account ID means exactly the same thing (the Principal element). Naming a specific role ARN is what actually narrows it.

2. The confused deputy, and what fixes it

Where the intermediary is an AWS service rather than a third party, the same defence is written with condition keys instead: aws:SourceArn and aws:SourceAccount pin which resource and account may cause the service to act, and aws:PrincipalOrgID restricts a resource policy to principals inside your own organisation (global condition keys).

3. Policies that stop naming ARNs

The reframing Federation is not a way of importing users. It is a statement about which issuer you believe, written once in a trust policy, after which every credential is minted by STS and expires on its own. The security question is therefore never "where is the key kept" - it is "what does the trust policy actually accept".

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A trust policy names arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root as its principal. Who can assume the role?
The account ARN delegates to the whole account, so any principal there whose own identity policy allows the assumption may use it. It is not restricted to the root user despite the spelling, creation order is irrelevant, and it reaches one account rather than an organisation.
What kind of protection does an External ID provide?
External ID defeats confused-deputy replay by being different for every customer, which is a uniqueness property rather than a secret. It does not encrypt anything, and certificate-based proof is what Roles Anywhere provides through a trust anchor.
A build pipeline outside AWS must deploy without any stored credential. What is registered?
A CI system that issues OIDC tokens is registered as an OIDC provider and the role's trust policy conditions on the token's claims. A trust anchor is for servers presenting X.509 certificates, SAML is the enterprise sign-in path for people, and a stored key pair is precisely what the requirement excludes.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You are onboarding a cost-analysis vendor. Their documentation asks you to create a role whose trust policy names their account ARN ending in :root, with no other condition, and to attach a broad read-only policy. Separately, your own build pipeline runs on a hosted CI platform and currently deploys using an access key stored in the platform's secret store.

Review both, say what is wrong with each, and write down what you would ask the vendor for before proceeding.

Hint

For the vendor, ask what the trust policy actually accepts rather than what the vendor intends. For the pipeline, ask what the platform can already prove about itself.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the vendor's trust policy literally. The account ARN delegates to their entire account, so any principal there that is allowed to assume roles can use yours - including one created later by someone at the vendor with no connection to your engagement.
  2. Name the missing condition. There is no External ID, so if the vendor serves many customers, an identifier or a mistake in their orchestration can point your role at the wrong customer's request. This is the confused deputy, and the condition is the standard defence.
  3. Write down what to ask for. The specific role ARN on their side rather than the account, an External ID that the vendor generates uniquely for you, and confirmation of exactly which read actions they need so the attached policy can be narrowed from a broad read-only one.
  4. Turn the pipeline into federation. If the CI platform issues OIDC tokens, register it as an OIDC provider and condition the role's trust policy on the claims that identify your repository and branch. The stored access key then has nothing to do and can be deleted.
  5. Say what remains your job either way. Both halves still have to allow: your trust policy on this side, and the caller's own permission to assume on the other. A vendor whose engineers cannot assume the role after you have written a correct trust policy has a problem in their account, not in yours.

Sanity check. Your vendor answer should ask for two things - a narrower principal and an External ID - and your pipeline answer should end with a credential being deleted rather than rotated. If your objection to the trust policy was that it names the root user, re-read what that ARN means.
