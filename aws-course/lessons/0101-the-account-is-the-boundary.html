An AWS account is a blast-radius unit before it is a billing unit

foundation 7 min Capability area org-hierarchy, with cross-account-resource-sharing. · AWS Organizations concepts

The one-minute version

  • An account is not a folder. It is the strongest isolation AWS gives you: no access flows between two accounts by default, in either direction, and everything that does flow is granted explicitly.
  • An account isolates four separate things at once: security, billing, quotas and regional scope. Splitting one workload out of another splits all four.
  • AWS Organizations is the tree the accounts hang in: one root, organisational units nested up to five levels below it, and accounts as the leaves.
  • The tree exists so that policy and cost inherit downwards. That is why the shape of the tree is an architecture decision and not an administrative one.
  • What it costs: isolation is real, so sharing has to be built. Resource Access Manager is how a subnet or a transit gateway crosses the wall without being copied.

1. The mental model: four isolations in one object

Most cloud platforms hand you a container for grouping resources. AWS hands you a container that is simultaneously a security perimeter, a bill, a quota pool and a regional scope. The reason multi-account designs are so common on AWS is that you cannot buy any one of those four separately.

What an account isolatesWhat that means in practice
SecurityNo implicit access either direction. A principal in one account reaches another only through a grant that names it.
BillingConsolidated at the organisation, still attributable per account, so a team's spend is a fact rather than an estimate.
QuotasEach account carries its own per-service limits, per region. A noisy neighbour in another account cannot exhaust yours.
Regional scopeWhole regions can be denied centrally for accounts below a point in the tree.

AWS states the consequence plainly in its own landing-zone guidance: an account is a unit of security protection, and risks can be contained inside one without affecting the others (multi-account landing zone strategy).

2. The mechanism: one tree, five levels deep

Three mechanics surprise people on the first organisation they build. A member account's root email address must be globally unique across all of AWS, so an address plan is needed before the first account is vended (creating a member account). Every account created by the organisation gets an OrganizationAccountAccessRole automatically, which is the management account's way back in. And the management account can update a member account's root email centrally, rather than chasing whoever owns that mailbox (central root email update).

4. Crossing the wall on purpose

Because isolation is the default, sharing is a service. AWS Resource Access Manager shares the resource itself - a subnet, a transit gateway, a dedicated host, a licence configuration - with another account, an organisational unit, or the whole organisation, instead of duplicating it or handing out broad permissions. Consumers see what was shared read-only, and sharing inside an organisation carries no charge. It is the mechanism that makes the Infrastructure unit above work: one team owns the network and every workload account borrows subnets from it.

The reframing The question "how many accounts should we have?" has no general answer, but it has a general test: two workloads belong in different accounts when you would not want one to be able to exhaust the other's quota, read the other's data by accident, or hide inside the other's bill. All three are properties of the account object, so all three are decided by the same split.

5. What it costs, and the account you must not use

The management account is the exception to every containment story on this page. It owns the organisation, it owns the bill, and the organisation's own guardrail policies do not apply to it (service control policies). A mistake made there is not capped by anything the organisation can express about itself.

Treat the management account as radioactive: no workloads, no shared tooling, no convenience deployments. Every argument for putting something there ends at the one account in the estate that your own guardrails cannot constrain. How those guardrails are written, and the order they are evaluated in, is Module 09.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two teams share one account today. Which of these does splitting them into two accounts not give you?
An account boundary is a security, billing, quota and regional-scope boundary. It says nothing about physical placement, so latency between the two workloads is decided by region and zone rather than by account. The other three are exactly what the split buys.
Which statement about the AWS Organizations hierarchy is correct?
An organisation has exactly one root, created for you, and organisational units nest up to five levels beneath it. Roots are not regional and there is never more than one. Units do nest, which is precisely what makes policy inheritance useful.
A network team owns one VPC and every workload account needs subnets in it. What is the AWS-native way to arrange that?
Resource Access Manager shares the resource itself across accounts or organisational units, read-only to the consumer and free inside an organisation. Copying and peering duplicates address space and management. Collapsing everything into one account throws away the isolation the split was for. Broad permissions grant far more than the subnets.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A company runs one production application and two teams building the next one. Central security needs an immutable copy of every audit log that no application team can reach. The network team wants to own one address plan for everybody. One team wants somewhere to break things without a review.

Draw the organisation: name the organisational units, say which accounts sit in each, and say for each unit which of the four isolations is the reason it exists.

Hint

Four requirements, four units. For each one ask which of security, billing, quota or regional scope is the reason the accounts underneath must not simply be folders in a bigger account.

Show the worked solution
  1. Security unit, holding a log archive account and a security tooling account. The reason is security isolation: an application team has no implicit access into another account, so the archive is unreachable from the workloads by construction rather than by policy.
  2. Infrastructure unit, holding a network account. The reason is ownership rather than separation: the address plan lives in one account and every consumer borrows subnets from it through Resource Access Manager, so the consumers never hold write access to the network.
  3. Workloads unit, split into production and non-production accounts. The reason is quota and billing isolation: a runaway non-production experiment must not exhaust production's per-service limits, and each side's spend has to be attributable on its own.
  4. Sandbox unit, holding one account per experimenter. The reason is blast-radius scope: the unit is drawn so it can be detached from the organisation without breaking anything above it, which is only true if nothing else depends on what is inside it.
  5. Check the depth. Four units directly under the root is one level of nesting, well inside the five-level limit, so there is room to nest production further later without a restructure.

Sanity check. Every unit in your answer should be justified by something an account isolates, not by who reports to whom. If a unit exists because two teams have different managers, it will be wrong the next time the company reorganises, and the guardrails attached to it will move with the wrong thing.
