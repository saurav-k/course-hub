Module 04 · Storage · Lesson 0402

A volume is pinned to one zone, and nothing backs it up unless you say so

working 6 min Capability key block-storage. · EBS volume types

The one-minute version

  • An EBS volume is scoped to one Availability Zone, exactly like a subnet, so a volume and the instance that mounts it always share a zone.
  • The modern general-purpose type separates performance from capacity: a baseline is included, and more throughput or more operations per second is bought independently of size.
  • The provisioned type goes far higher, and its top form also raises the durability commitment as well as the ceiling.
  • Snapshots are incremental and are held in storage the service manages. They are also the only backup a volume has, and none of them happen automatically.
  • What it costs: local instance storage is faster and ephemeral, so it is a cache and a scratch area rather than a place data lives.

1. The mental model: a disk with a zone stamped on it

2. The mechanism: buying performance separately from size

The older general-purpose type tied performance to capacity, so the way to buy more operations per second was to buy a bigger disk than you needed. The current general-purpose type does not: it includes a baseline and lets you raise throughput and operations per second on their own (general purpose volumes).

The reframing Sizing a volume is now three numbers rather than one: how many gigabytes, how many operations per second, and how much throughput. Old habits size the first and hope the other two follow, which was true of the previous generation and is not true of this one.

3. What it costs: the backup nobody switched on

Snapshots are incremental and land in storage the service manages, so a second snapshot of a barely-changed volume is cheap (EBS snapshots). They are also the entire backup story for a volume, and nothing takes one for you. A volume with no snapshot has no recovery path that does not involve rebuilding it.

Restoring from a snapshot is not instant. A volume created from a snapshot fills its blocks as they are first read, so early reads are slower than a warm volume. Fast snapshot restore removes that penalty, and it is itself a limited resource - roughly five per region by default. A recovery plan that assumes every restored volume is immediately at full speed has not been tested.

4. The volume that is not really storage

Instance store is local NVMe attached to the machine rather than reached over the network. It is the fastest storage an instance can have and it is ephemeral, so it is the right home for a cache, a scratch area or a spill file, and the wrong home for anything whose loss would matter. The rule that keeps this straight is simple: if the data has to survive the instance, it is not instance store's job.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A database instance in one zone must be recovered in another zone. What actually moves?
A volume is scoped to one zone and cannot be attached across zones, so the snapshot is what crosses and a new volume is created from it. Instance store is ephemeral and local, and no volume spans zones - which is exactly why the snapshot schedule sets the recovery point.
A workload needs more operations per second than the included baseline. What is the modern answer?
The current general-purpose type buys performance independently of capacity, so the rate is raised directly. Growing the volume to buy performance was the previous generation's model. Striping is a workaround for that older constraint, and instance store is ephemeral, so it changes the durability story rather than the sizing one.
What backs up an EBS volume by default?
Volumes are not backed up automatically, so a volume with no snapshot policy has no recovery path but a rebuild. There is no default snapshot schedule, volumes do not replicate across zones, and a machine image is not created when an instance stops.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A self-managed database runs on one instance with a single large volume. The business states a recovery point objective of fifteen minutes and a recovery time objective of one hour, and it wants to survive the loss of an Availability Zone. Nobody has configured any snapshots.

Write what has to change to meet both objectives, and identify the one part of the plan most likely to fail its first real test.

Hint

The recovery point is set by one mechanism and the recovery time by a different one. Work them separately, then ask what a freshly restored volume actually performs like.

Show the worked solution
  1. The recovery point is set by the snapshot interval. Fifteen minutes of acceptable loss means snapshots at least every fifteen minutes, and today there are none, so the current recovery point is unbounded. Snapshots are incremental, so a frequent schedule on a slowly-changing volume is far cheaper than the first one suggests.
  2. The zone requirement is met by the snapshot, not by the volume. A volume is pinned to its zone, so surviving the loss of that zone means being able to create a new volume from a snapshot in a different zone, and that capability comes with the snapshot rather than with any volume setting.
  3. The recovery time is the restore plus the start-up. One hour has to cover creating the volume from the snapshot, attaching it to a new instance in the surviving zone, and bringing the database up. Write those three down as separate numbers rather than as one estimate.
  4. Find the weak step. The restored volume. Blocks are fetched as they are first read, so a database that starts on a freshly restored volume runs slower than the same database on a warm one, and a recovery-time estimate measured on a warm volume will be wrong.
  5. Answer it explicitly. Either use fast snapshot restore for this volume, remembering that it is itself a limited resource of roughly five per region by default, or measure the restore with cold blocks and set the recovery time objective from that measurement rather than from a hope.

Sanity check. Your plan should have a snapshot interval that matches the recovery point exactly, and a recovery time built from three measured steps rather than one guess. If your recovery time came out well under an hour on paper, check whether you measured a warm volume - that is the step that turns a plan into a surprise.
