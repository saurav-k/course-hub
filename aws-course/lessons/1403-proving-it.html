A resilience claim is a hypothesis until something has tried to break it

working 7 min Capability keys resilience-assessment and well-architected-framework. · AWS Fault Injection Service

The one-minute version

  • A chaos experiment is a template of three things: actions, targets, and stop conditions - and the stop conditions are alarms.
  • Always set a stop condition. It is the safety net that halts a runaway experiment, and an experiment without one has no way to stop itself.
  • Experiment templates need permission to pass a role to the service, which is where a first attempt usually fails.
  • Resilience Hub holds the objectives, scores an application out of 100 against them, recommends alarms, procedures and tests, and can launch the experiments.
  • What it costs: the assessment service does not execute a failover. It scores and recommends, and the recovery itself lives in the replication and backup services.

1. The mechanism: three parts, and one of them is the brake

2. The loop: hypothesis, experiment, evidence

The assessment service does not execute failover. It defines objectives, scores against them, recommends and can launch experiments - and the recovery itself is performed by the replication and backup services of the previous two lessons. A plan that names it as the failover mechanism has named the wrong thing.

3. The framework the argument happens in

The reframing Resilience is a claim about the future, so it can only be supported by evidence from the past. Write the objective, score against it, break something on purpose, and record what happened. An estate that has never deliberately broken anything knows only that it has not failed yet, which is a different statement from being resilient.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What halts a chaos experiment that is causing more damage than expected?
Stop conditions are alarms and they are the documented safety net, which is why an experiment should never be defined without one. There is no assessment-service veto, and a role does not lose permissions on its own.
What does the resilience assessment service do when an objective is not met?
It assesses, scores out of 100 and recommends alarms, procedures and tests, and can launch experiments. It does not execute a failover or provision replication, and its output is a score and recommendations rather than a security finding.
A first experiment fails to start with a permissions error. What is the likely cause?
The template needs permission to pass a role to the service, and missing that is the usual first failure. Tag-based targeting is normal and supported, and neither a cross-account experiment nor an alarm's placement produces a pass-role error.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A team claims their service survives the loss of an Availability Zone. The architecture has instances in three zones behind a load balancer, a Multi-AZ database, and a NAT gateway in the first zone that every subnet routes to. Nobody has tested the claim. The business objective is fifteen minutes of downtime at most.

Design the experiment, name the stop condition, predict what will actually happen, and say what evidence the run produces either way.

Hint

Read the architecture for a component that exists in one zone but is used by all three. That component is the hypothesis worth testing, and its failure is what the experiment should cause.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the suspicious component. The NAT gateway is in the first zone and every subnet routes to it, which is lesson 0702's shared-dependency trap. The instances and the database survive a zone loss; outbound traffic from all three zones does not.
  2. Write the hypothesis. "Losing the first Availability Zone leaves the service available, with outbound calls to third parties continuing." Stating it as a sentence is what makes the result meaningful.
  3. Define the experiment. Actions that make the first zone's resources unavailable, targets selected by tag so the blast radius is explicit, and a run in a pre-production environment first. The permission to pass the service role has to exist before any of this starts.
  4. Name the stop condition. The service's own error-rate alarm - the same one that pages a human. If the experiment trips it, two things have been proved at once: the dependency is real, and the alarm works.
  5. Predict the outcome and name the evidence. The prediction is that the load balancer removes the failed zone and requests continue, while anything calling a third party begins to fail because egress is gone. Either way the run produces evidence: a confirmed hypothesis with a timestamped record, or a named single point of failure with a known fix - a NAT gateway per zone - and a measured impact to compare against the fifteen-minute objective.

Sanity check. Your experiment should target the first zone specifically rather than a random one, and your stop condition should be an alarm that already exists. If you predicted a clean survival, re-read which resources the subnets route through.
