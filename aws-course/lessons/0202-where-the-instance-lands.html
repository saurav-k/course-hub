Placement is three questions, and only the last one changes who shares your hardware

working 6 min Capability keys dedicated-hosts, bare-metal and confidential-compute. · EC2 placement groups

The one-minute version

  • "Where does it run?" is really three questions: which zone, how instances sit relative to one another, and who else is on the physical host.
  • The second is answered by a placement group, and AWS offers three strategies with different restrictions: cluster, partition and spread.
  • The third is a ladder: shared hosts, Dedicated Instances, Dedicated Hosts, and finally bare metal. The rungs differ in what you can see and what you are billed for.
  • Dedicated capacity is a licensing decision before it is a security one: a Dedicated Host exposes sockets and cores, which is what per-core licensing needs.
  • What it costs: the rungs interact. A Dedicated Host cannot be used inside a placement group, so two of the three questions cannot both be answered the way you wanted.

1. Question one: which zone

A subnet lives in exactly one Availability Zone, so choosing the subnet answers the zone question. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why a fleet that should survive a zone loss is configured by giving it subnets in several zones rather than by setting a resilience option. Module 01 argued why the zone is the right boundary for that.

2. Question two: how instances sit relative to one another

A placement group is how you say something about instances relative to each other rather than absolutely. AWS documents three strategies - cluster, partition and spread - and each one carries its own restrictions. The vendor page is the authority for what each strategy does and what it restricts, and it is worth reading before a design depends on one, because the restrictions are the part that changes.

The reframing A placement group is a request about correlation, not a location. You are telling the platform whether you want these instances to share fate and share proximity, or to avoid sharing either. Everything else about where they land stays the platform's decision.

3. Question three: who else is on the host

The two answers collide. A Dedicated Host cannot be used inside a placement group, so a design that wants both per-core licensing and a placement strategy has to give one of them up. Discover that on paper rather than in a launch failure, because the fix is usually a different licensing arrangement rather than a different flag.

4. The fourth answer: a room beside the instance

There is one isolation requirement none of the ladder rungs meets: keeping data unreadable by the operating system that is processing it, including by whoever holds administrator rights on that machine. AWS answers it with a shape that is not a rung at all.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A database licence is priced per physical core. Which rung of the tenancy ladder does that requirement select?
Per-core licensing needs the socket and core count to be visible, and that visibility is what separates a Dedicated Host from a Dedicated Instance. Dedicated Instances give you the hardware without the visibility. A placement group says nothing about tenancy. And the default tenancy is a shared host.
A design calls for Dedicated Hosts and a cluster placement group together. What happens?
Dedicated Hosts are not usable inside a placement group, so the combination is not available at all and the design has to drop one requirement. The three wrong options each describe a partial combination, which is exactly the assumption that produces a launch failure late in a project.
What is the defining constraint of a Nitro Enclave compared with an ordinary instance?
An enclave has no persistent storage, no external networking and no interactive login, and reaches its parent only through a local socket. It is processor agnostic across Intel, AMD and Graviton Nitro instances, it sits beside the parent rather than replacing it, and it is a carved-out room rather than whole-machine memory encryption.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A payments team brings three requirements. The commercial database is licensed per physical core. The application tier must survive the loss of one Availability Zone. And a card-tokenisation routine must be unreadable by anyone holding administrator rights on the machine that runs it.

Answer each requirement with a specific placement or tenancy decision, then find the one pair in your answer that cannot both be satisfied and say what you would trade.

Hint

Three requirements, three different questions from this page. Once you have all three answers written down, check them against the one documented restriction between rungs.

Show the worked solution
  1. The licence selects Dedicated Hosts. Per-core licensing needs sockets and cores to be visible, and that is the property that separates a Dedicated Host from a Dedicated Instance. It also changes the billing unit from the instance to the host, so the density calculation moves onto your side of the line.
  2. Zone survival selects subnets in several zones. A subnet is confined to one zone, so this is answered by giving the application tier subnets in at least two of them rather than by any tenancy setting.
  3. The tokenisation routine selects a Nitro Enclave. No storage, no external network, no interactive access, and the parent's root user cannot read enclave memory. The key it needs can be held under a KMS key policy that requires the enclave's attestation before unwrapping.
  4. Find the collision. If the database tier also wanted a placement group - for instance to keep its nodes close together - that cannot be combined with Dedicated Hosts. The two are documented as mutually exclusive.
  5. Trade deliberately. The licence is a contractual obligation and the placement strategy is a performance preference, so the placement group is what gives way. Record that as a decision with its reason rather than as a setting somebody removed.

Sanity check. Each requirement should be answered by a different one of the three questions, and the collision you found should involve the tenancy rung rather than the zone. If your answer used a placement group to satisfy the zone requirement, re-read what a placement group actually promises: correlation between instances, not location.
