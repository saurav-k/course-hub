A backup restores data, and disaster recovery restores a service

working 6 min Capability key backup-service. · AWS Backup

The one-minute version

  • One policy-driven service backs up more than twenty service families, so backup stops being a per-service habit and becomes an estate-wide plan.
  • Copies can be made across Regions and across accounts, which is what turns a backup into something a Region failure or a compromised account cannot take with it.
  • Vault Lock in compliance mode makes recovery points immutable after a grace period - even to the root user - which is the strongest anti-ransomware control on the platform.
  • A logically air-gapped vault holds malware-resistant copies, separated from the account that produced them.
  • What it costs: an untested backup is a hypothesis, which is why restore testing exists as a feature rather than as a quarterly fire drill somebody forgets.

1. The mental model: two different questions

2. The mechanism: a lock the root user cannot undo

Two placements complete the picture. Cross-account and cross-Region copies mean the backup does not share a failure domain with what it protects, and a logically air-gapped vault holds malware-resistant copies separated from the producing account. An attacker with full control of one account should still not be able to reach the copies.

3. Turning a hypothesis into evidence

The reframing Judge a backup design by three questions: can an attacker who owns the account also reach the copies, is the copy in the same failure domain as the original, and when did somebody last restore one. The service has an answer for each - cross-account copies, cross-Region copies, and restore testing - and an estate that has enabled none of them has a backup job rather than a backup strategy.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which control prevents a compromised root user from deleting recovery points early?
Compliance-mode vault lock makes recovery points immutable even to the root user once the grace period has passed. A cross-Region copy protects against losing a Region rather than against deletion, guardrails skip the management account and are policy rather than immutability, and a long retention can still be shortened without the lock.
What is the most useful output of a scheduled restore test?
How long a restore actually takes is the number a recovery-time objective must be checked against. A pass says the data came back but not how quickly, and neither storage cost nor plan coverage is what the test exists to measure.
Why copy backups into another account?
Separating the copies from the account that produced them removes the shared failure domain that a compromise creates. Retention is set by the plan rather than by the account, one service covers many families, and restores go to a test environment regardless of which account holds the copy.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A company runs nightly backups of its databases and file systems into the same account and Region as the workloads. Retention is 35 days. Nobody has restored anything in two years. The board asks whether the company would survive a ransomware attack in which an administrator's credentials are stolen, and what the recovery time would be.

Answer both questions honestly, then propose the four changes that would let a different answer be given, and say which one has a deadline attached.

Hint

For the first question, ask what a stolen administrator credential can reach. For the second, notice that nobody has measured it. One of your four changes cannot be undone after a certain moment.

Show the worked solution
  1. Answer the ransomware question. No. A stolen administrator credential in the same account reaches the backups, because they are in that account with no immutability. An attacker deletes or encrypts both the data and its copies, and 35 days of retention protects nothing that can be deleted.
  2. Answer the recovery-time question. Unknown. Nobody has restored in two years, so any figure offered would be a guess. Saying so plainly is more useful to a board than an estimate that has never been tested.
  3. Change one - immutability. A vault lock in compliance mode, so recovery points cannot be deleted early by anyone, including the root user. This is the change with a deadline: after the grace period the settings cannot be altered, so the retention has to be right before it closes.
  4. Change two and three - separate the failure domains. Copy recovery points into a different account, and into a different Region. The first removes the compromised-account failure domain, the second removes the Region one. A logically air-gapped vault strengthens the first further.
  5. Change four - measure it. Scheduled restore testing, so the recovery time becomes a recorded number that improves or degrades visibly. Then the board's second question has an answer, and the next lesson's strategy choice has something real to be checked against.

Sanity check. Your first answer should be no and your second should be unknown - if either was reassuring, re-read what a stolen administrator credential can reach. The deadline belongs to the vault lock's grace period.
