Only the Auto Scaling group replaces a failed instance - a fleet does not

working 6 min Capability keys autoscaling-group and instance-template. · Auto Scaling groups

The one-minute version

  • An Auto Scaling group holds a fleet at a minimum, maximum and desired size, replaces members that fail a health check, and balances them across the zones you gave it.
  • It decides the desired size three different ways: dynamic policies react to a metric, predictive policies forecast from history, and scheduled actions change it at a stated time.
  • Cooldown applies only to simple scaling. Target tracking, which is what AWS recommends, does not use it, and expecting it to is a common misreading of a fleet that seems too eager.
  • Instance refresh is how a fleet moves onto a new launch-template version, one batch at a time, with a rollback if it goes wrong.
  • What it costs: EC2 Fleet and Spot Fleet are not scaling groups. They diversify launch pools and replace reclaimed capacity, and they do not health-check and replace failed instances.

1. The mental model: a set point with a controller

A scaling group is a thermostat. You give it a range and a target, and it acts to keep the fleet there: launching when the desired size rises, terminating when it falls, and replacing a member the moment a health check says that member is no longer serving. The interesting design work is in how the desired size is decided.

When launching is slow, a warm pool keeps pre-initialised instances stopped beside the group so a scale-out event starts them instead of building them. It is the answer to a long boot, not to a slow decision.

2. The mechanism: rolling a fleet forward

The reframing A scaling group is not primarily a cost-saving device. Most estates get more value from the replacement behaviour than from the elasticity: a group with a minimum, a maximum and a desired size all set to three is a perfectly good design, and it is the cheapest way to make "three healthy instances" a thing the platform maintains rather than a thing a human notices.

3. What it costs: the construct that looks the same and is not

EC2 Fleet and Spot Fleet launch thousands of instances in one request across several types, zones and purchase options, and they replace reclaimed capacity. They look like a scaling group and they are not one: they do not health-check members and replace the ones that fail. A design that needs steady-state replacement pairs a fleet with a scaling group rather than choosing between them.

This is the failure that reads as a mystery in an incident review. Instances in a fleet stop serving, nothing replaces them, and the fleet still reports the capacity it was asked for, because from its point of view the instances exist. Ask of any EC2 construct one question - does it health-check and replace? - and only the Auto Scaling group answers yes.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A target tracking policy is scaling more often than expected, and someone suggests raising the cooldown. What is wrong with that?
Cooldown is a property of simple scaling and does not govern target tracking, so raising it changes nothing and the real cause stays unfound. The three wrong options each invent a plausible-sounding scope, which is how this misreading survives review meetings.
Instances launched by a Spot Fleet stop serving traffic but are still running. What replaces them?
Fleets diversify launch pools and replace reclaimed capacity, and they do not health-check and replace failed members, so nothing acts. A launch template is a specification and launches nothing itself. A load balancer removes an unhealthy target from rotation but never creates capacity.
A fleet must be at full size before a predictable morning peak rather than after it. Which policy shape fits?
Predictive scaling forecasts from load history and raises capacity ahead of a recurring pattern, which is exactly the stated requirement. Both reactive options still act after load arrives, however they are tuned. Instance refresh replaces members with a new template version and does not change the size at all.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A service runs on ten instances. Load is flat all week except for a sharp, reliable peak every weekday morning. Each instance takes about six minutes to boot and warm its cache. The platform team also ships a new hardened image every week and wants fleets to pick it up without downtime.

Specify the group: which scaling policies you would attach and why, what you would do about the six-minute boot, and how the weekly image reaches the fleet safely.

Hint

Separate three problems: deciding the number, making the number arrive fast enough, and changing what each member is. Each has its own mechanism on this page.

Show the worked solution
  1. Reactive policy as the floor. Attach a target tracking policy so the fleet responds to anything the forecast did not see. Do not attach a cooldown expectation to it - cooldown governs simple scaling only.
  2. Predictive policy for the known peak. The morning peak is reliable and recurring, which is the case predictive scaling exists for. It needs load history before it forecasts, and it can be left forecasting without acting while you compare its forecast against what actually happened.
  3. Warm pool for the six-minute boot. Reactive scaling that decides in one minute and delivers in seven is still seven minutes late. A warm pool keeps pre-initialised instances stopped beside the group so a scale-out starts them rather than building them.
  4. Instance refresh for the weekly image. The image lands in a new launch-template version, and instance refresh replaces members in batches against a minimum healthy percentage, rolling back to the previous version if the new batch fails its health checks.
  5. Check the quota underneath it all. The maximum size multiplied by the vCPUs per instance has to fit inside the family quota for this account and region, or the group stops growing before it reaches its maximum and says nothing.

Sanity check. Your answer should use three different mechanisms for three different problems, and the six-minute boot should be solved by pre-warming rather than by tuning a policy. If your answer solved the slow boot by scaling earlier, you have made the fleet permanently larger rather than faster.
