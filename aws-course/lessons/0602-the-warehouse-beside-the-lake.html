The warehouse keeps its own storage and still reads yours

working 7 min Capability key data-warehouse. · Amazon Redshift

The one-minute version

  • Redshift Managed Storage separates storage from compute, so a warehouse grows in two directions independently and neither growth forces the other.
  • Compute is bought in one of two shapes: provisioned nodes - the RA3 family and the Graviton RG family - or Serverless, sized in Redshift Processing Units.
  • Serverless capacity ranges from a base of 4 to 1,024 RPUs, billed per second with a sixty-second minimum, which makes very short bursts more expensive per unit of work than they look.
  • Concurrency scaling adds temporary capacity when queries queue, so the shape of the day matters as much as the size of the data.
  • Two doors lead outside its own storage: Spectrum reads lake tables in S3, and zero-ETL integrations pull from operational databases without a pipeline - within the same Region and inside published quotas.

1. The mental model: one storage layer, two ways to buy compute

2. The mechanism: what a sixty-second minimum does

The other half of the capacity story is the peak rather than the floor. A Serverless base runs from 4 to 1,024 RPUs, and concurrency scaling adds temporary capacity to either shape when queries begin to queue, so a warehouse can be sized for the median day rather than for the worst hour of the month.

3. The two doors out of its own storage

The reframing The lake-versus-warehouse question is usually asked as if one had to win. On AWS the useful question is narrower: which tables are worth laying out for the queries you already know, and which are better left where they fell. Spectrum exists so that answering it wrongly is recoverable.
Zero-ETL is not a general replication service. It carries a same-Region requirement and published quotas, so a design that assumed cross-Region replication into a warehouse has to name a different mechanism. Check the quota page for the integration you intend to use before the design depends on it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A Redshift Serverless bill is far higher than the total query time suggests. What explains it best?
Per-second billing carries a sixty-second minimum, so short queries are billed for a full minute each and frequency drives the bill. Storage is priced separately from compute by design, concurrency scaling adds capacity when queries queue rather than doubling every charge, and 4 RPUs is the bottom of the range rather than something you can go under.
A query must join a warehouse table to two years of raw events sitting in S3. What makes that possible?
Spectrum resolves external tables through the Glue Data Catalog and reads the objects in place, so one query spans both. Zero-ETL brings changes from operational databases rather than from S3, Managed Storage is the warehouse's own storage layer, and concurrency scaling adds capacity without changing what the query can reach.
Which requirement rules out a zero-ETL integration as designed?
Zero-ETL carries a same-Region requirement, so a cross-Region source needs a different mechanism. A same-account Aurora source is the ordinary case, the compute shape of the warehouse is not what the integration depends on, and Spectrum reading other tables is unrelated.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A warehouse serves two workloads. Overnight, forty reporting jobs each run for six to twelve minutes. During the day, an internal tool issues roughly 3,000 small lookups, each taking about four seconds, spread across eight working hours. The team is on Redshift Serverless and the daytime cost is four times the overnight cost, which nobody expected.

Work out where the daytime cost comes from, and propose two changes: one that keeps the tool on Redshift, and one that moves it off.

Hint

Convert both workloads into billed seconds rather than elapsed seconds, and apply the minimum to each billed period separately. The gap between the two numbers is the whole answer.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the overnight work. Forty jobs averaging nine minutes is 40 × 540 = 21,600 seconds. Every one is far above the minimum, so billed seconds equal actual seconds: about 21,600.
  2. Count the daytime work as run. 3,000 lookups at four seconds is 12,000 seconds of actual work - roughly half the overnight total, which is why the bill was a surprise.
  3. Count the daytime work as billed. If each lookup is its own billed period, the sixty-second minimum applies to each: 3,000 × 60 = 180,000 seconds. That is more than eight times the overnight figure, and it explains a daytime cost several times larger despite less real work.
  4. Keep it on Redshift - stop paying the floor per lookup. Batch the lookups so that many are answered within one billed period rather than each starting its own, and keep the endpoint warm through the working day rather than letting it idle between calls. The aim is to make the number of billed periods look like the number of hours, not the number of lookups.
  5. Move it off - put the lookups on a path with no minimum. Small, frequent, key-shaped reads are not warehouse work. Lesson 0601's index path or module 05's key-value store both answer this shape without a per-query floor, and the warehouse goes back to doing the overnight aggregation it is good at.

Sanity check. Your billed daytime figure should be about fifteen times your actual daytime figure, because sixty divided by four is fifteen. If the two came out close together, you applied the minimum to the day rather than to each billed period.
