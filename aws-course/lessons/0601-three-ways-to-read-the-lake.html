The read path you choose is the cost model you accept

working 7 min Capability keys serverless-query-engine, managed-search and bi-dashboards. · Amazon Athena

The one-minute version

  • Three services read the lake and they charge for three different things: bytes scanned, capacity held, and people who log in.
  • Athena runs serverless SQL and Spark directly against S3 and bills per terabyte scanned, so columnar formats and partitions are the cost control, not a tuning nicety.
  • OpenSearch Service is the answer when the access pattern is search rather than scan, in domains or in serverless collections of three kinds: search, time-series and vector.
  • Quick Sight is the dashboard layer, with SPICE holding an in-memory copy so a dashboard does not re-query the source on every view.
  • What it costs: two of the three duplicate the data, so freshness becomes a scheduling problem rather than a property you get for free.

2. The mechanism: what a scan actually costs

Two guardrails follow from that. Workgroups can enforce a per-query scan limit, which turns a runaway query into a failed one rather than an invoice, and query results land in S3, so the output of every query is itself an object with a lifecycle and a cost of its own.

3. The paths that keep a second copy

Two naming traps sit on this page. QuickSight is now written Quick Sight and lives inside Amazon Quick, while the APIs keep their old names (Quick Sight welcome). And OpenSearch Service still carries legacy Elasticsearch OSS engines capped at 7.10 beside current OpenSearch 3.x, so "which engine and which major version" is a real question on an existing domain rather than a formality (What is OpenSearch Service).

OpenSearch also splits by deployment shape in a way worth holding: a domain is capacity you size, with UltraWarm and cold tiers for older indices, while serverless collections are bought in OpenSearch Compute Units that decouple indexing capacity from search capacity, and a collection is created as one of three kinds - search, time-series or vector (OpenSearch Serverless overview).

The reframing These three are not tiers of the same product. They are three different answers to "who is asking, how often, and how fresh must it be". An occasional deep question is cheapest read in place; a constant lookup is cheapest from an index; a summary many people open every morning is cheapest held in memory and refreshed on a schedule.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An Athena bill is rising while query counts are flat. What is the most productive thing to change?
Athena bills per terabyte scanned, so columnar formats and partitions are what move the number. There is no instance behind Athena to size, the cost driver is bytes rather than a per-Region rate, and registering a table is not what the query charge is measured against.
Which read path holds no second copy of the data?
Athena reads the objects in place, which is why nothing goes stale and why the cost is measured in bytes scanned. SPICE is an in-memory copy by design, and both OpenSearch shapes require the data to be ingested and indexed before it can be searched.
A team wants free-text relevance ranking over support tickets. Which service fits, and why?
Relevance ranking over text is the search access pattern, which is the job an index does. Athena can filter text but scans to do it and returns no ranking, a dashboard presents results rather than producing them, and Lake Formation is a permission layer rather than a query engine.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A retailer holds two years of order lines in S3 as gzipped JSON, about 900 GB, partitioned by nothing. Three demands arrive. Finance runs about five ad hoc queries a week, each touching three of forty fields for one quarter. Support needs to find an order by a customer's free-text note, in under a second, hundreds of times an hour. The board wants one page of numbers, refreshed daily, opened by about forty people each morning.

Assign a read path to each, and for the finance case estimate the bytes scanned before and after a layout change, stating your assumptions.

Hint

Match each demand against the three questions the reframing asks: who is asking, how often, and how fresh must it be. For the arithmetic, take column pruning first and partition pruning second, and say out loud what you are assuming about even sizes.

Show the worked solution
  1. Finance - Athena, after a layout change. Five queries a week is occasional and ad hoc, which is what read-in-place is cheapest at. Nothing needs a second copy.
  2. Estimate the current scan. Gzipped JSON is row-oriented, so a query touching three fields still reads every field of every row it looks at, and with no partitions it looks at everything: about 900 GB per query, roughly 4.5 TB a week.
  3. Estimate it after the change. Assume forty fields of equal size and eight quarters of equal size, which is the assumption doing the work here. Columnar format with three of forty fields gives 900 × 3 ÷ 40 = 67.5 GB. Partitioning by quarter and reading one of eight gives 67.5 ÷ 8 = about 8.4 GB - a little over one percent of the original.
  4. Support - OpenSearch Service. Free-text lookup, hundreds an hour, under a second, is the search access pattern rather than the scan one. A serverless collection of the search kind avoids sizing a domain, and the cost of the choice is that order notes must be ingested and indexed, so the index lags the lake by however often you feed it.
  5. The board - Quick Sight with SPICE. One page, forty readers, daily freshness. Holding the summary in memory means forty people opening the page do not run forty queries, and a daily refresh is exactly the schedule the requirement already stated.

Sanity check. Three demands, three different services, and one arithmetic answer between about 1 and 2 percent of 900 GB. If your finance estimate did not fall by roughly two orders of magnitude, check that you applied both prunings - column first, then partition - rather than only one.
