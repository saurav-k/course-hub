Entropy is the average of your own surprise

working 11 min Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (Bell System Technical Journal, 1948). · the paper

The one-minute version

  • Learning that something unlikely happened tells you more than learning that something likely did, and -log p is the number that says how much more.
  • Entropy is the average of that number over everything that could happen. Nothing else. Every other quantity in this module is entropy rearranged.
  • Read in base 2 it is a count of yes/no questions, and it is the floor on how short any code for those outcomes can be.
  • A certainty scores zero. The uniform distribution scores the maximum, log n. Everything real sits between.
  • Shannon did not choose -log p. He wrote down three requirements any sensible measure must meet, and they leave exactly one function standing.

1. Surprise, before entropy

Start with one outcome rather than a whole distribution. A weather service tells you it rained in Manchester in November. You shrug. It tells you it snowed in Chennai. You sit up. The same number of words arrived, and the second sentence carried far more.

So whatever measures information has to grow as the probability shrinks. Two more demands settle the form. An outcome that was certain must carry none, because you already knew. And two independent pieces of news must add: hearing that two separate coins both came up heads should cost exactly twice what one costs.

I(x) = −log2 p(x) Read as: the surprise, or self-information, of an outcome x is minus the base-two logarithm of the probability p(x) that it happens. The minus sign is there because a probability is at most 1, so its logarithm is at most 0, and we want a positive answer.

Check the three demands against it. As p falls towards zero the logarithm falls without limit, so surprise grows without limit. At p = 1 the logarithm is zero, so a certainty costs nothing. And because independent probabilities multiply while logarithms turn products into sums, two independent pieces of news add rather than multiply. That last property is the whole reason a logarithm appears at all.

In base two the unit is the bit, a word Shannon credits to J. W. Tukey in a footnote on his second page. An outcome with probability one half costs exactly one bit, which is one yes/no answer. One in eight costs three. This course teaches in bits, because "how many yes/no questions" is a thing you can picture, and quotes training losses in nats, base e, because that is what every framework prints. One nat is 1.4427 bits.

2. Entropy is that average

A single outcome has a surprise. A distribution has many outcomes, each with its own surprise and its own probability of occurring. Average them, weighting each by how often it happens, and you have scored the whole distribution.

H(p) = E[−log2 p(x)] = −∑i pi log2 pi Read as: the entropy H of a distribution p is the expected value, written E, of the surprise. Written out, it is the sum over every possible outcome i of that outcome's probability pi multiplied by its surprise. The sum runs over the whole alphabet of outcomes, and the probabilities add to one.

An outcome with probability zero never occurs, so it should contribute nothing, but 0 × log 0 is not defined. The convention is to read it as zero, and the convention is licensed rather than convenient: as x falls to zero, x log x falls to zero too, so zero is the only continuous choice.

3. What the number is, operationally

The definition tells you how to compute entropy. It does not yet tell you what the answer means. The code reading does, and it is Shannon's own frame, because he was designing telegraphy rather than statistics.

You have to send one symbol at a time down a wire. You are free to invent the code. Common symbols should get short codes and rare ones long codes, because the average length is what you pay. The entropy of the source is the shortest average length any code can achieve, and the code that achieves it gives each symbol a length equal to its own surprise.

That is what the number is for. Entropy is not a metaphor about disorder borrowed from thermodynamics. It is a length, in bits, that a real encoder can hit and cannot beat.

4. Why minus log, and nothing else

An engineer is right to be suspicious of a formula that arrives fully formed. Shannon was too, so he did it the other way round: he wrote down what a measure of uncertainty would have to satisfy, and then asked which functions survive.

His three requirements, in his own words in section 6 of the 1948 paper, are that H should be continuous in the probabilities; that for equally likely outcomes it should increase with how many there are, because more options means more uncertainty; and that a choice broken into two successive choices should give the weighted sum of the two.

That third one is the interesting one, and it is checkable. Take a plan column with three values. You can ask "which plan?" in one go, or you can ask "basic, or one of the other two?" and then, only in the cases where it was one of the other two, ask which. The total uncertainty must come out the same either way, with the second question weighted by how often you have to ask it.

The reframing Shannon's Theorem 2 says that those three requirements leave exactly one function standing: H = −K ∑ p log p, for some positive constant K that only fixes the unit. Entropy is not a definition somebody chose. It is what you are forced into once you say what you want.

The shape of his argument is worth carrying even though the full proof is in his Appendix 2 and not reproduced here. Write A(n) for the entropy of n equally likely outcomes. Apply the grouping requirement to a choice among sm options, broken into m successive choices among s, and it forces A(sm) = m A(s). A function on the whole numbers that satisfies that and increases with n has to be K log n. Continuity then carries the result from equal probabilities to all the rest. The step that does the real work is turning the grouping axiom into that functional equation; everything after it is squeezing.

Shannon is disarming about how much this matters. He writes that the theorem "and the assumptions required for its proof, are in no way necessary for the present theory... The real justification of these definitions, however, will reside in their implications." That is the right attitude for an engineering course too. The uniqueness theorem is reassurance. The reason to care about entropy is the ten pages after it.

5. The two properties you will use

Two facts get used constantly, and both are worth proving rather than remembering.

Entropy is zero exactly when the outcome is certain. Every term pi log pi is zero or negative, so the sum can only be zero when every term is. A term is zero when pi is 0 or 1, and the probabilities add to one, so the only way to have every term zero is to put all the mass on a single outcome.

Entropy is largest for the uniform distribution. This is the maximum-entropy bound, and here it is in full. Compare p against the uniform distribution ui = 1/n:

  1. Write the constant as a sum. Because the probabilities add to one, log2 n = ∑i pi log2 n. This is the step that does the real work: it turns a comparison of two numbers into one sum over outcomes.
  2. Subtract. log2 n − H(p) = ∑i pi log2 n + ∑i pi log2 pi.
  3. Collect the logarithms. That is ∑i pi log2(n pi), which is ∑i pi log2(pi / ui).
  4. Name it. That last expression is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from p to the uniform distribution, which lesson 03 proves is never negative and is zero only when the two distributions agree. So log2 n − H(p) ≥ 0.

Using a later result is deliberate and safe here: lesson 03 proves that divergence is non-negative without using this bound, so nothing is circular. It also tells you what the gap means, which is the better reason to write the proof this way. The distance from your distribution to the flat one is exactly how many bits you have already been given for free.

Everything above is about a discrete distribution. The continuous analogue, differential entropy, is not the limit of this quantity: the derivation drops a term that diverges, so the continuous version can be negative. A Gaussian's is ½(1 + ln 2πσ2), which is below zero whenever σ2 < 1/(2πe). If you meet a negative entropy in a paper on variational inference, this is why, and nothing has gone wrong.

6. Worked: Shannon's noisy channel

This is Shannon's own example from page 21 of the paper, and it is the shortest demonstration that entropy measures something the obvious arithmetic misses.

A source sends 1,000 binary symbols per second, zeros and ones equally likely, so it produces 1,000 bits per second. Noise flips one symbol in a hundred. What rate of information actually gets through? The instinct is 990 bits per second: subtract the errors. Shannon's own words are that this "is not satisfactory".

  1. Ask what the receiver still does not know. Not "how many symbols were wrong" but "which ones". That uncertainty is the entropy of the error pattern, one symbol at a time.
  2. Compute the first term. −0.01 log2 0.01 = 0.01 × 6.6439 = 0.0664 bits.
  3. Compute the second. −0.99 log2 0.99 = 0.99 × 0.0145 = 0.0144 bits.
  4. Add. H(0.01) = 0.0808 bits per symbol. This is the equivocation: what is still unknown about the input after seeing the output.
  5. Scale to the second. 1,000 × 0.0808 = 80.8, so the rate is 1000 − 81 = 919 bits per second, which is the figure Shannon prints.
  6. Sanity check. Push the error rate to one half. Then H(0.5) = 1 bit per symbol, the equivocation eats the whole thousand, and the rate is exactly zero. That is right: at a 50 per cent flip rate the output tells you nothing about the input at all. A formula that did not give zero there would be wrong.
  7. What changes if the error rate drops tenfold, to 0.001? H(0.001) = 0.0114 bits, so the rate becomes 989 bits per second. Ten times fewer errors bought back only about nine tenths of the loss, because −p log p does not shrink in proportion to p. Rare errors are individually very expensive.
  8. Derived against quoted. The 919 is Shannon's. The 0.08079 in step 4 is this page recomputing it, and the two agree, which is the only way to know a transcription was faithful.

A second, smaller number to carry: a dataset split 90/10 between two classes has entropy 0.4690 bits against a balanced set's 1 bit. More than half the label entropy is gone before any model has looked at a single feature, which is why accuracy on an imbalanced problem flatters so badly.

7. The same arithmetic at a scale you cannot check by hand

Eight numbers can be done on paper. Twenty thousand cannot, and the point of the code is that the definition does not change when the data gets large.

code/9001-entropy.py · runnable Python, numpy and pandas only
def entropy_from_definition(probabilities):
    """H(p) = -sum_i p_i log2(p_i), one term at a time."""
    total = 0.0
    for p in probabilities:
        if p > 0.0:                      # the 0 log 0 = 0 convention
            total += p * np.log2(p)
    return -total if total != 0.0 else 0.0

shares = signals["plan"].value_counts(normalize=True).sort_index()
slow = entropy_from_definition(shares.to_numpy())
fast = float(-(shares[shares > 0] * np.log2(shares[shares > 0])).sum())
assert abs(slow - fast) < 1e-12          # the loop and the vector agree

Run against the committed 12,000-row table, the plan column scores 1.4416 bits against a ceiling of log2 3 = 1.5850, and the churned label scores 0.8902 bits. The program also checks the grouping axiom numerically, splitting plan into "basic against the rest" and then resolving the rest: 0.999944 + 0.6572 × 0.891111 comes to 1.441564, and computing H(plan) directly gives 1.441564. They agree to twelve decimal places, which is the axiom in section 4 holding on real data rather than in a proof.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A dataset has two equally likely classes. You add a third class, also equally likely. What happens to the entropy?
Three equally likely outcomes cost log2 3 = 1.585 bits. The first option confuses balance with count: balance says entropy is at its maximum for that many outcomes, and the maximum itself grows with the count. The second is the disorder metaphor doing damage, since more options is more uncertainty, not less. The fourth counts the classes instead of taking their logarithm, which would only be right at four.
Which of these distributions over four outcomes has the highest entropy?
The uniform distribution attains log2 4 = 2 bits, the maximum available over four outcomes. The second scores 0.2419 bits: it is almost a certainty. The third scores exactly 1 bit, because two of its outcomes never occur, so it is really a two-outcome distribution and its own ceiling is 1. The fourth scores 1.3346 bits, higher than the second and still short, because it is spread but not evenly.

Practice

A fraud model's training set contains 998 legitimate and 2 fraudulent transactions per thousand.

(a) Compute the entropy of the label column in bits. (b) A colleague reports that a baseline model "explains 99.8 per cent of the labels". Say what their number measures and what yours measures.

Hint

Both terms matter, and one of them is not the one you expect. The rare class has a tiny probability and an enormous surprise. Work out which factor wins before deciding the answer is near zero.

Solution

(a) The two terms:

  1. −0.998 log2 0.998 = 0.998 × 0.00289 = 0.00288 bits.
  2. −0.002 log2 0.002 = 0.002 × 8.9658 = 0.01793 bits.
  3. Total: 0.0208 bits. Notice that the rare class supplies 86 per cent of it, on 0.2 per cent of the rows.

(b) The 99.8 per cent is the accuracy of a model that always predicts "legitimate". It measures how often a constant is right. Your 0.0208 bits measures how much uncertainty there ever was to remove. A model that removes none of it still scores 99.8 per cent accuracy, which is exactly why the metric and the loss disagree on an imbalanced problem, and why a training loop optimises the second.

Sanity check: your answer must lie between 0 and 1 bit, because there are two classes and one bit is the ceiling for two. Anything above 1 means you summed the two surprises instead of averaging them by their probabilities.
Your teacher. If the code reading in section 3 did not land, ask an agent to build you a Huffman code for the distribution 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125 and then for 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, and to print the average code length beside the entropy for each. The first matches exactly and the second does not quite, and asking why is the fastest route into what the floor really claims.
