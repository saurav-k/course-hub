The design decides the answer before the data arrives

The one-minute version

  • Randomisation is what licenses the causal claim. Everything downstream is measurement.
  • Fix the one metric that decides the call before you look, or lesson 168's multiplicity arrives by the back door.
  • Power the test first. Halving the effect you want to detect costs four times the traffic.
  • Peeking daily and stopping when green took a 0.05 error rate to 0.2188, measured.
  • The fix is to spend alpha across the looks rather than at each one.

1. Where the causal claim comes from

This is also why the OEC, the one criterion that decides the call, is chosen before the experiment starts. Twenty metrics and a threshold gives you a significant one about two times in three when nothing changed, which lesson 168 measured. Deciding afterwards which metric mattered is that same problem wearing a different hat.

2. Power, and the theorem that sizes the experiment

Theorem To detect a difference δ = pB - pA at two-sided significance α with power 1 - β, the required size per arm is approximately n = (zα/2√(2p̄(1-p̄)) + zβ√(pA(1-pA) + pB(1-pB)))2 / δ2.

What is assumed: two independent arms of equal size, a two-proportion test as on lesson 167, and samples large enough for the normal approximation. The shape of the argument is to draw two distributions, one under the null and one under the effect you care about, and demand that the rejection threshold sit far enough into the first that the second still clears it most of the time.

Under the null the statistic is centred at 0 with standard error se0 = √(2p̄(1-p̄)/n). Under the alternative it is centred at δ with standard error se1. Rejection needs the observed difference to exceed zα/2se0, and requiring that to happen with probability 1 - β under the alternative gives

δ = zα/2 se0 + zβ se1 Read as: the gap you are trying to detect has to cover two things at once. The first term is how far out the threshold sits under the null, and the second is how far the alternative's own spread reaches back towards it. Both standard errors carry a 1/√n, which is what lets the equation be solved for n.

The step that does the real work is solving that for n. Both standard errors carry 1/√n, so n comes out with δ squared underneath it. That square is the entire practical content of the theorem: the cost of an experiment grows as the inverse square of the effect you want to see.

3. The committed experiment was under-powered, and it was knowable

The experiment in datasets/experiment.csv ran 12,000 per arm against a 5 per cent baseline, looking for a lift to 5.6 per cent. Its power at that size was 0.5456. Detecting the effect it was built to detect was close to a coin flip, and reaching 80 per cent power would have needed 21,885 per arm. What actually happened was 596 against 643 with z = 1.3711: a failure to reject that tells you almost nothing, because the test was never likely to succeed.

The reframing A negative result from an under-powered experiment is not evidence of no effect. It is evidence that the experiment could not have found one. The power calculation costs five minutes and is the difference between the two readings.

4. Peeking, and what it does to the error rate

A fixed-horizon p-value assumes exactly one look, at a sample size committed to in advance. Watching a dashboard daily and stopping the moment it goes green is a different procedure, and it has a different error rate.

The fix is not to stop looking. It is to spend the alpha across the looks rather than at each one, using a sequential boundary that is very strict early and relaxes towards the end. In an O'Brien-Fleming design over a seven-day experiment the day-one cutoff is around 5e-8 and the final one is about 0.040, slightly under 0.05 because some of the budget was already spent.

5. Worked in full

  1. The setting. A 5 per cent baseline conversion, and you want to detect a 10 per cent relative lift, so pA = 0.05 and pB = 0.055.
  2. The effect in absolute terms. δ = 0.005. This is the number that gets squared, and it is small.
  3. The pooled rate. p̄ = (0.05 + 0.055)/2 = 0.0525.
  4. The null term. z0.025√(2 × 0.0525 × 0.9475) = 1.96 × 0.31544 = 0.61826.
  5. The alternative term. z0.20√(0.05 × 0.95 + 0.055 × 0.945) = 0.8416 × 0.31534 = 0.26539.
  6. Combine and square. (0.61826 + 0.26539)2 = 0.78084, and dividing by δ2 = 0.000025 gives 31,233 per arm.
  7. Sanity check. The programme's table gives 31,233 for exactly this case, and the 5 per cent lift row is 122,123, almost exactly four times it. Halving the effect quadrupling the cost is the square in the denominator, visible in two rows of a table.
  8. What changes if. Ask for 90 per cent power instead of 80. The second term grows and the requirement rises to 41,812, about a third more traffic for ten more percentage points of power.
  9. What it means here. At 62,466 users total this experiment is feasible for a large site and impossible for a small one. If your traffic cannot reach it, the honest options are to test a bigger change, accept lower power and say so, or not run the test.

6. The honest limits

Three things a clean test still will not tell you. Novelty and primacy: a change can win in week one because it is new and lose in week four, or the reverse, so a short experiment measures the reaction rather than the effect. Twyman's law: any figure that looks interesting is usually wrong, and a surprisingly large lift is more often instrumentation than insight. The base rate: most ideas do not work, so a significant result on a low-prior idea is more likely to be a false positive than the p-value alone suggests. Lesson 171 is where that last sentence gets the machinery to be made precise.

7. The programme

code/0169-ab-testing.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
once   = 0   # one look at the pre-committed size
daily  = 0   # a look every day, stop the moment p <= 0.05
for _ in range(4_000):
    arm_a, arm_b = simulate_null_days(days=14, per_day=900)
    once  += two_prop_p(arm_a.sum(), arm_b.sum()) <= 0.05
    daily += any(two_prop_p(*cum) <= 0.05 for cum in cumulative_days(arm_a, arm_b))
print(once/4000, daily/4000)      # -> 0.0520 and 0.2188

Nothing in that loop is a mistake in the test. The only difference between the two counters is when they were allowed to stop.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You halve the size of the effect you want to be able to detect. What happens to the traffic needed?
The sample-size formula divides by delta squared, so halving delta multiplies n by four. The table on this page shows it directly: a 10 per cent lift needs 31,233 and a 5 per cent lift needs 122,123. Option 4 is the true answer to a different question, namely what going from 80 to 90 per cent power costs.
Peeking daily and stopping when significant took the false positive rate from 0.052 to 0.2188. Which test was computed wrongly?
Every individual p-value was computed correctly. A fixed-horizon p-value assumes a single look at a pre-committed size, and taking fourteen chances to cross the line is a different procedure with a different error rate. Options 1, 2 and 4 all look for a broken calculation, and the calculations were fine.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Your site converts at 8 per cent and gets 40,000 visitors per arm per month. Using the page's numbers as anchors rather than recomputing the formula, decide roughly what relative lift you can detect in one month at 80 per cent power, and say what you would do if the change you want to test is smaller than that.

Hint

The requirement scales as one over delta squared, and it also depends on the baseline through p(1-p). Compare 8 per cent against the page's 5 per cent baseline on that factor first.

Show the worked solution
  1. Baseline factor. At 5 per cent, p(1-p) = 0.0475. At 8 per cent it is 0.0736, a factor of 1.549 more variance per user.
  2. But delta grows too. A given relative lift r gives δ = r × p, so δ2 scales as p2, which is (0.08/0.05)2 = 2.56 times larger.
  3. Net effect. The requirement scales as p(1-p)/δ2, so it changes by 1.549 / 2.56 = 0.605. A higher baseline is easier for the same relative lift.
  4. Anchor. At 5 per cent a 5 per cent relative lift needed 122,123 per arm. At 8 per cent it needs about 122,123 × 0.605 = 73,884.
  5. Against your traffic. 40,000 per arm is roughly half of that, and the requirement scales as 1/r2, so the detectable lift grows by √(73,884/40,000) = 1.36, giving about a 7 per cent relative lift in one month.
  6. If your change is smaller. Run for longer, test a bolder change, or accept lower power and report it. What you must not do is run it anyway at 40 per cent power and read a null result as no effect.

Sanity check. The answer should land between the page's 5 per cent row (122,123, more than your monthly traffic) and its 10 per cent row (31,233, comfortably under it), and 7 per cent does. If you concluded you could detect a 1 per cent lift in a month on 40,000 users, a factor of δ2 went in the wrong direction.

Your teacher. If the peeking result feels like a trick, ask an agent to run the same simulation but stop early only when p is below 0.01, and report the final error rate. It falls, but not to 0.01, and watching it land somewhere in between is what makes a sequential boundary feel necessary rather than bureaucratic.
