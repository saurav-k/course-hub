The curse is real, and it has a hypothesis your data may not meet

frontier 12 min Blum, Hopcroft and Kannan, Foundations of Data Science, chapter 2; Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim, ICDT 2001.

The one-minute version

  • In high dimensions almost all of a body's volume sits in a thin skin at its surface, and the effect is violent rather than gradual.
  • Distances converge: among a thousand points in a thousand dimensions the farthest is ten per cent further than the nearest.
  • Two random directions are almost orthogonal, with the angle concentrating at ninety degrees like one over the square root of the dimension.
  • The theorem behind all this has an if, and it is about the coordinates being close to independent.
  • Trained embeddings violate that if, which is why the vector database you use at 768 dimensions works. Measured here, both ways.

1. Almost all of it is skin

Take any body in d dimensions and shrink it towards its centre by one per cent. How much of the volume did you lose?

Partition the body into tiny cubes. Shrinking each side by a factor (1 − ε) shrinks a d-dimensional cube's volume by (1 − ε)d, because a cube has d sides and volume is their product. Volume adds over the partition, so the whole body shrinks by the same factor.

volume((1 − ε)A) = (1 − ε)d volume(A) Read as: shrinking any body A towards the origin by a fraction ε multiplies its volume by (1 − ε) raised to the power of the dimension. The share of the original volume left outside the shrunken copy is therefore 1 − (1 − ε)d.

The step that does the real work is the exponent d, and it is worth pausing on how little else there is. Nothing in that argument is about spheres, or probability, or data. It is about a product of d side lengths, and that is why the effect is violent instead of gradual.

2. Distances stop discriminating

If the points are all near the surface, they are all near the same distance from the centre, and from each other. That is not a metaphor: it is measurable, and the measurement has a name.

Relative contrast is (Dmax − Dmin) / Dmin, the gap between the farthest and nearest point as a fraction of the nearest. Large contrast means "nearest" picks something out. Contrast near zero means everything is equidistant and the word has stopped working.

Two more facts, both from Blum, Hopcroft and Kannan's chapter 2, both stated rather than proved here because their proofs are Module 08's tail bounds.

The Gaussian Annulus Theorem. For a d-dimensional Gaussian with unit variance per coordinate, all but at most 3e−cβ2 of the probability mass lies in the shell √d − β ≤ |x| ≤ √d + β. The intuition is one line: E[|x|2] = ∑E[xi2] = d, so the typical radius is √d. What the theorem adds is that the spread around that radius does not grow with d. The program measures exactly that: the standard deviation of |x| stays near 0.70 from ten dimensions to ten thousand, while the radius grows from 3.16 to 100.

Near-orthogonality. Draw n points from the unit ball and, with probability 1 − O(1/n), every pair has |xi · xj| at most √(6 ln n) / √(d − 1). Measured, the mean absolute cosine between two random directions is 0.0820 at a hundred dimensions and 0.0290 at 768, against a closed form of √(2/πd) giving 0.0798 and 0.0288.

A result most treatments skip, and it changes a default. Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim show that Dmax − Dmin grows like d1/k − 1/2 for the Lk norm, and that relative contrast decays like one over root d for every norm but with a constant √(1/(2k+1)) that favours small k. Their conclusion is that the Manhattan norm is preferable in high dimensions, then Euclidean, then L3, which is the opposite of most people's instinct and is in the title of their paper.

3. The theorem has an if

Here is the statement everyone quotes, in the form Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim give it as their Theorem 1, restating Beyer and colleagues.

IF   limd→∞ var( |Xd| / E[|Xd|] ) = 0   THEN   (Dmax − Dmin) / Dmin → 0 Read as: if the variance of the length of a random point, divided by that length's average, goes to zero as the dimension grows, then the relative contrast goes to zero in probability. The quantity on the left is a scaled spread: it asks whether all your points have roughly the same length, once you allow for the fact that lengths grow with dimension.

The hypothesis is doing all the work and almost every retelling drops it. It is a statement about the coordinates being close to independent, and it is checkable: measure it on your own data before assuming your problem is cursed.

4. Why your vector database works

Bishop names the two escapes in his own words: "real data will often be confined to a region of the space having lower effective dimensionality", and "real data will typically exhibit some smoothness properties".

The committed embedding matrix lets us check the first directly. It has forty-eight columns, but it was built from six latent topics, so the points sit near a low-dimensional surface inside that space. Centring removes one degree of freedom from six topic means, so five directions should carry almost everything, and they do: five of forty-eight directions carry 67.10 per cent of the variance.

The consequence, measured on the same file. Relative contrast on the embeddings at d = 48 is 1.9656. On uniform points at the same d = 48 it is 0.8229. And a one-nearest-neighbour query agrees with the topic label on 100 per cent of three hundred trials. Same dimension, opposite outcome.

code/0188-curse-of-dimensionality.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def scaled_norm_variance(points):
    """The hypothesis of Theorem 1, so measure it rather than assume it."""
    norms = np.linalg.norm(points, axis=1)
    return float(np.var(norms / norms.mean()))
# uniform points:  d=100 -> 0.002124   d=1000 -> 0.000196   (going to zero)
# the embeddings:  d=48  -> 0.008406   uniform at d=48 -> 0.004460
Be precise about which number is the evidence. The scaled-norm variance on the embeddings is only about twice the uniform value at the same dimension, which is suggestive rather than decisive. The decisive number is the spectrum: five directions of forty-eight carrying 67 per cent means the effective dimension the geometry sees is around five, not forty-eight. Quoting the weaker statistic as though it settled the matter would be the same overreach this page is about.

5. Two curses, one phrase

The term is used for two different arguments and the literature rarely separates them.

The statistical curse is Bellman's, and Bengio's in the language-modelling setting: the number of distinguishable configurations grows exponentially, so no dataset can cover the space. Its fix is to share parameters across similar inputs, which is exactly what an embedding does. How Language Models Happened, lesson 18 uses the phrase in this sense and is right to.

The geometric curse is this page: concentration of measure, volume in the skin, distances converging. Its fix is to reduce the dimension, or to rely on the intrinsic dimension already being low.

Both are correct and they are different claims with different remedies. Naming which one you mean is worth doing every time, because a team that reaches for dimensionality reduction to fix a data-sparsity problem has applied the wrong medicine to a real illness.

6. Worked: the shell, and the escape

  1. Take the formula. The share of volume in the outer ε of the radius is 1 − (1 − ε)d.
  2. Three dimensions, one per cent skin. 1 − 0.993 = 1 − 0.970299 = 2.97 per cent. Intuition holds.
  3. A hundred dimensions. 1 − 0.99100 = 1 − 0.366032 = 63.40 per cent.
  4. Five hundred. 1 − 0.99500 = 1 − 0.006570 = 99.34 per cent. The interior has effectively ceased to exist.
  5. Sanity check. All three must lie between 0 and 100 per cent and must increase with d. If the largest d gave the smallest answer you raised 0.01 to the power d rather than 0.99.
  6. Now the awkward question. A team stores 768-dimensional sentence embeddings and finds brute-force nearest-neighbour search returns useful results. Reconcile that with step 4.
  7. The answer, and it is not about size. Step 4 describes the ambient space, and the convergence theorem in section 3 needs the scaled-norm variance to vanish, which needs the coordinates to be close to independent. A trained embedding's 768 coordinates are strongly dependent, the points lie near a much lower-dimensional surface, and the hypothesis fails.
  8. What changes if you replace those embeddings with 768 columns of independent noise? Everything: the hypothesis now holds, contrast collapses, and nearest-neighbour search returns arbitrary rows. The dimension did not change. The dependence did.

An answer of "because 768 is not large enough" is wrong, and it is the most common one. There is no threshold dimension. There is a hypothesis, and you can measure whether your data satisfies it in four lines.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which condition must hold for the "all distances converge" theorem to apply?
The hypothesis is about the spread of the lengths, and it is checkable in four lines on your own data. The first option invents a threshold dimension, which is the most common wrong answer and the reason a team dismisses the curse at 50 columns and panics at 800. The second confuses one convenient case with the requirement; the theorem is stated for arbitrary distributions satisfying the hypothesis. The fourth is wrong in an interesting way: the norm changes the constant, as the callout in section 2 shows, but not whether the hypothesis is needed.
In high dimensions, which Lk norm preserves the most contrast between near and far?
Contrast decays like one over root d for every norm, but with a constant that favours smaller k, so L1 keeps the most. The second option is what almost everyone assumes and it is what the Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim title is pushing back on. The third goes the wrong way along the same axis: L-infinity is the k-to-infinity limit and is the worst of the family. The fourth is half right, since the rate is shared, and misses that the constant is not.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Find the share of a ball's volume lying in the outer one per cent of its radius, for d = 3, d = 100 and d = 500.

Then: a team stores 768-dimensional sentence embeddings and finds brute-force nearest-neighbour search still returns useful results. Reconcile that with your three numbers, naming the specific assumption that fails.

Hint

For the second part, re-read the hypothesis in section 3 before reaching for an explanation about 768 not being large enough. The theorem does not mention a threshold dimension anywhere.

Show the worked solution
  1. d = 3. 1 − 0.993 = 1 − 0.970299 = 2.97 per cent.
  2. d = 100. 1 − 0.99100 = 1 − 0.366032 = 63.40 per cent.
  3. d = 500. 1 − 0.99500 = 1 − 0.006570 = 99.34 per cent.
  4. Separate the two claims. Those figures are about the ambient space. The claim about search is about where the data sits inside it, and the two are only linked when the data fills the space.
  5. Name the assumption. The convergence theorem needs var(|X| / E|X|) to vanish, which needs the coordinates to be close to independent.
  6. Say why it fails here. A trained embedding's coordinates are strongly dependent by construction, since the model was trained to place related inputs near each other. So the points sit near a much lower-dimensional surface and the variance does not vanish.
  7. Give the check. Measure the scaled-norm variance, and take the singular values of the centred matrix to see how many directions carry the variance. On the dataset here, five of forty-eight carry 67 per cent.
  8. State what would break it. Replace the embeddings with 768 columns of independent noise and search stops working, at the same dimension. Dimension was never the variable.

Sanity check. All three percentages must lie between 0 and 100 and must increase with d. If your d = 500 answer came out smaller than your d = 100 one, you raised 0.01 rather than 0.99 to the power d, which gives a number so small it rounds to a share of 100 per cent and looks plausible until you compare the three.

Your teacher. If you want to feel section 1 rather than read it, ask an agent to compute the volume of the unit ball in d dimensions for d from 1 to 30 and print it. It peaks at five dimensions and then falls towards zero, which is the single most counter-intuitive fact in this module and it takes two lines to see.
