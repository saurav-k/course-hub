The data matrix, and how a thing that has no numbers becomes a vector

working 11 min Everything in this module has been preparing one object. This page hands it over and says what it is for.

The one-minute version

  • A dataset is a matrix: rows are samples, columns are features. Every library and every paper assumes it.
  • Centre the columns and scale them, and one matrix product gives every pairwise correlation at once.
  • A category has no distance until you invent one. One-hot coding makes every pair equally far apart, which is a table, not a geometry.
  • An embedding gives each item a learned row, so similarity becomes lesson 43's cosine. The lookup is a matrix product nobody computes.

The convention, and why it is worth stating

The convention is not this course's invention and it is worth quoting so that it feels like a fact rather than a preference. scikit-learn's own getting-started page puts it plainly: "The size of X is typically (n_samples, n_features), which means that samples are represented as rows and features are represented as columns."

Read that back through the module. One row is a point in feature space, so lesson 42's distance and lesson 43's cosine are questions you can now ask about two samples. One column is one measurement across the whole dataset, so its mean and its spread are questions about a variable. Lesson 44's product Xw is one prediction per row, and XWT is a whole batched layer.

Getting the two mixed up is the most common error in this territory, and lesson 40 showed why it is dangerous: centring on the wrong axis raises no error and quietly destroys the data.

Centre, scale, and one product does everything

Subtract each column's mean and divide by its standard deviation. Call the result Z. Then a single matrix product recovers every pairwise relationship in the table.

Why ZTZ divided by n − 1 is the correlation matrix

What is assumed. X has n rows and d columns. Z is X with each column centred to mean zero and divided by that column's sample standard deviation, the one with n − 1 in the denominator.

The shape of the argument. Look at a single entry of the product and recognise it. Everything else is the definition of matrix multiplication.

The step that does the real work is that entry (i, j) of ZTZ is the dot product of column i of Z with column j, which is a sum over samples.

(ZTZ)ij / (n − 1) = Σk zkizkj / (n − 1) Read as: the sum runs over k = 1 to n, the samples. zki is the standardised value of feature i in sample k. Each factor is already a centred value divided by its own standard deviation, so the sum is the covariance of the two features divided by the product of their standard deviations, and that quotient is the definition of the correlation coefficient.

So every entry of the product is the correlation between two features, the diagonal is all ones because a column is perfectly correlated with itself, and the matrix is symmetric because the dot product does not care about order. The program checks all of it against pandas' own .corr() and the two agree to 4.1 × 10−15. ■

Module 08 owns covariance and correlation as statistical objects, and module 04 decomposes this matrix. What lesson 45 adds is the price: forming it costs about 2nd2 flops, and because it is symmetric only half the entries need computing.

A category has no distance

The sensor table has a column that is not a number at all: which of four machines produced the reading. There is no arithmetic on the letter A, and no sense in which machine B sits between A and C.

The standard first move is one-hot coding: give each category its own column, with a 1 in it and zeros elsewhere. Those vectors are exactly the standard basis of lesson 48, and that has a consequence worth noticing rather than skipping past.

An embedding, and the product nobody computes

An embedding gives each item a row in a matrix E with far fewer columns than there are items, and those rows are learned rather than chosen. Once they exist, lesson 42's distance and lesson 43's cosine apply to words, users, products or machines.

The connection to this module is exact. Multiplying a one-hot vector by E selects one row of E, because lesson 44's column reading weights the rows by the entries of the input and every weight is zero but one. So an embedding layer is a matrix product. It is also never computed as one: the answer is row i, so the implementation is an index.

The size difference is the reason. The Transformer's base model uses a shared vocabulary of about 37,000 tokens with a model width of 512. Written out as the standard basis that is 37,000 × 37,000 = 1,369,000,000 numbers. The embedding matrix is 37,000 × 512 = 18,944,000, about 72 times smaller, and the product would cost about 37.9 million flops per token against nothing at all for the lookup.

The analogy is not an equation. Mikolov and colleagues report that vector("King") − vector("Man") + vector("Woman") "results in a vector that is closest to" vector("Queen"), searching "by cosine distance". Linzen points out what that leaves unsaid: the raw result "is often trivial (typically equal to b)", so "in practice, most studies exclude a, a* and b from consideration". Measured on this course's own corpus, the nearest word to the offset is one of the three inputs 26.5% of the time, and 52.1% of the time when the offset is small. The exclusion is doing real work, and a page that says "equals Queen" has skipped it.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. X is the sensor matrix, 12,000 rows by 8 columns, and Z is X standardised column by column.
  2. Read the two axes. Row 0 is one moment described by eight sensors. Column 0 is vibration_x across all 12,000 moments.
  3. Centre and scale. Each column has its mean subtracted and is divided by its sample standard deviation, so every column of Z has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  4. Form the Gram matrix. ZTZ divided by 11,999 is 8 by 8, and it is symmetric to 10−16.
  5. Check it a second way. Comparing against pandas' .corr() entry by entry, the largest disagreement is 4.1 × 10−15. One matrix product reproduced all 28 pairwise correlations.
  6. Read one entry. The correlation between the two vibration sensors is 0.9874, which lesson 49 already used: near-dependence you can see in a single number.
  7. Handle the category. The machine column has four values. One-hot coded, all six pairwise distances are the same number, √2, so the coding carries no notion of two machines being alike.
  8. Say what would change. Replace the one-hot columns with four learned rows of width 8 and the distances stop being equal. Two machines that behave alike can be placed close together, and what "alike" means is decided by the data rather than by the person who wrote the column.

Sanity check. The diagonal of the correlation matrix in step 4 must be exactly 1 in every position, because a standardised column dotted with itself is the sum of n squared z-scores, which is n − 1 by construction. If a diagonal entry comes out at 0.9999 the standard deviation was computed with n instead of n − 1.

What it means. Everything downstream reads this matrix. Module 04 decomposes it to find the directions the data actually varies in. Module 08 reads the same Gram matrix as a covariance. Module 10 asks what similarity means once the columns run into the hundreds. All of them assume rows are samples, and all of them assume you know what a matrix does to a vector, which is what the twelve pages before this one were for.

code/0052-the-data-matrix.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/sensors.csv")
SENSORS = ["vibration_x", "vibration_y", "acoustic_db", "current_amp",
           "humidity_pct", "dust_index", "temp_c", "pressure_kpa"]
X = frame[SENSORS].to_numpy(float)
n = len(X)

Z = (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
G = Z.T @ Z / (n - 1)                       # one product, every correlation
assert np.allclose(G, frame[SENSORS].corr().to_numpy())
print(round(G[0, 1], 4))                    # 0.9874, the two vibration sensors

E = np.random.default_rng(1).normal(0, 0.02, size=(200, 8))
e = np.zeros(200); e[137] = 1.0
assert np.array_equal(e @ E, E[137])        # the lookup IS the product

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In the standard machine-learning convention, what is a column of X?
The convention is (n_samples, n_features), so a column holds one measurement taken across every sample. The first names the row and is the mix-up this page exists to prevent. The second is an entry of the target vector, which is not part of X at all. The fourth belongs to the model rather than to the data, and has length equal to the number of features rather than the number of samples.
Why does one-hot coding make every pair of distinct categories equally similar?
One-hot vectors are the standard basis, so any two distinct ones have a dot product of zero and a distance of exactly root two, the same for every pair. The second is false: every one-hot vector has length exactly 1, which is part of the problem rather than a way out of it. The third ignores the single 1 that makes each one distinct. The fourth is a fact about the corpus and would not change the geometry even if it were true.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A vocabulary holds 37,000 tokens and the model has a width of 512.

  1. How many numbers would the one-hot basis take if you wrote it out as a matrix?
  2. How many numbers are in the embedding matrix E?
  3. What is the ratio, and roughly how many flops would the product cost per token?
  4. A colleague says the word analogy "equals" its answer. Correct the sentence in one line, and say what the correction is doing.
Hint

For part 3, use the 2mnp rule from lesson 45 with a one-row left operand. For part 4, the two things to name are the search and what is removed from it before the answer is read.

Show the worked solution
  1. Square the vocabulary. The one-hot basis is the identity of size 37,000, so 37,000 × 37,000 = 1,369,000,000 numbers.
  2. Multiply out E. 37,000 × 512 = 18,944,000 numbers.
  3. Take the ratio. 1,369,000,000 ÷ 18,944,000 = about 72 times smaller, which is just the vocabulary divided by the width.
  4. Cost the product. A 1 by 37,000 times a 37,000 by 512 product is 2 × 1 × 512 × 37,000 = 37,888,000 flops per token, to produce an answer that is a row of E.
  5. Replace it with the lookup. Because the input is one-hot, the answer is row i, so the implementation indexes instead and the 37.9 million flops become zero.
  6. Correct the sentence. "It does not equal it: the offset produces a vector whose nearest neighbour by cosine is that word, and only after the three input words are excluded from the search."
  7. Say what the correction does. It replaces an equality with a ranking, and it names an exclusion that is doing real work: without it, the nearest word is one of the three inputs 26.5 per cent of the time on this course's corpus.

Sanity check. The ratio in step 3 must come out as vocabulary divided by width, because both counts share a factor of 37,000. If it did not, one of the two multiplications is wrong, and 37,000 ÷ 512 = 72.3 is the check.
