The wait so far tells you nothing about the wait remaining

working 12 min Trials until the first success, a mean of one over p, and no memory at all. · Hajek, ECE 313, section 2.5

The one-minute version

  • Repeat a trial until it works. The number of attempts is geometric, with PMF (1−p)k−1p.
  • The tail is cleaner than the PMF: P(L > k) = (1−p)k, because the first k all had to fail.
  • The mean is 1/p, and when p is small the standard deviation is nearly as large as the mean.
  • The single most likely wait is always one trial, whatever p is, even when the mean is 5.
  • Memorylessness: twelve failures in a row change nothing about what happens next.

1. The setup, and the PMF

Run independent Bernoulli(p) trials until one succeeds. Let L be the index of the first success. Walk the first three cases and the pattern is immediate:

  • L = 1 means the first trial succeeded: probability p.
  • L = 2 means fail then succeed: probability (1−p)p.
  • L = 3 means fail, fail, succeed: probability (1−p)2p.
P(L = k) = (1 − p)k−1 × p,   for k = 1, 2, 3, … L: which trial was the first success. p: the success probability, the same on every trial. k − 1: how many failures came first. Note the support starts at 1, not 0, because you always run at least one trial.

Why there is no coefficient here

What is assumed: the trials are independent and share the same p.

  1. Write out what the event is. {L = k} happens exactly when trials 1 to k−1 fail and trial k succeeds.
  2. Notice it is one sequence, not many. There is exactly one outcome pattern that qualifies.
  3. Price it with independence. Multiply: (1−p) taken k−1 times, then p.
The step that does the real work is step 2. Compare the binomial in Lesson 129, where many sequences qualified and a coefficient C(n, k) was needed to count them. Here exactly one does, which is precisely why the geometric formula has no binomial coefficient in it. That difference is the whole structural distinction between the two distributions.

That it sums to 1 is the geometric series from Module 01: summing (1−p)k−1p over all k ≥ 1 gives p ÷ (1 − (1−p)) = p ÷ p = 1.

2. Reach for the tail, not the PMF

P(L > k) = (1 − p)k L exceeds k exactly when the first k trials all failed, which is one multiplication and no sum. Every later step on this page is shorter in this form than in the PMF form, so reach for it first.

3. The mean is 1/p, and the derivation is three lines

The shape: condition on the first trial, notice the problem repeats itself, and solve for the unknown.

  1. Split on the first trial. With probability p it succeeds and L = 1. With probability 1−p it fails.
  2. See what happens after a failure. One trial has been spent, and the number of further trials needed has exactly the same distribution as L did at the start, because the trials are independent and identical.
  3. Write that as an equation. The average of L is p×1 + (1−p)×(1 + the same average).
  4. Expand and collect. mean = p + (1−p) + (1−p)×mean = 1 + (1−p)×mean, so p × mean = 1.
  5. Read it off. mean = 1 ÷ p.
The step that does the real work is step 2, and it is memorylessness being used before it has been named. Notice also what this derivation avoids: it treats the mean as one unknown number and never uses any property of averages, so it stands on its own. Module 08 owns expectation properly and makes arguments like this routine rather than clever.

The variance is (1−p)/p2, so the standard deviation is √(1−p)/p. Hajek's remark on that is operationally important: when p is small the standard deviation is nearly as large as the mean. A mean wait is not a typical wait.

4. The most likely wait is always one

5. Memorylessness

P(L > n + k | L > n) = P(L > k) Read: given you have already waited n trials without success, the chance of waiting k more is the same as it was at the very start. n does not appear on the right-hand side at all, and that absence is the whole property.

Why

  1. Write the conditional out. It is P(L > n+k and L > n) ÷ P(L > n).
  2. Simplify the numerator. If L > n+k then L > n automatically, so the intersection is just {L > n+k}.
  3. Use the tail formula twice. (1−p)n+k ÷ (1−p)n = (1−p)k, which is P(L > k).

The step that does the real work is step 2, the containment that collapses the joint event to one of its parts. After that it is cancelling powers. Hajek adds the converse: any positive-integer random variable with this property is geometric for some p, so memorylessness does not merely describe the geometric, it characterises it.

The gambler's fallacy is the exact denial of this diagram: "I am due a win". You are not. The self-loop has no memory of how many times it has been taken. This is also why a retry that has already failed eleven times is not more likely to succeed on the twelfth - unless the failures are correlated, in which case the trials were never independent and the geometric was the wrong model to begin with.

6. Worked: the retry column

The retries column counts attempts until an upstream call succeeded, generated at p = 0.85.

  1. Formula. P(1) = 0.8500. P(2) = 0.15 × 0.85 = 0.1275. P(3) = 0.0225 × 0.85 = 0.0191.
  2. Observed. 21,200 rows at 1, 3,226 at 2, 497 at 3, so 0.8480, 0.1290, 0.0199.
  3. Recover p from the mean. The observed mean is 1.1786, and 1 ÷ 1.1786 = 0.8485, which recovers the true 0.85 to two decimals.
  4. Check the tail. Formula P(L > 1) = 0.15, observed 0.15200. Formula P(L > 2) = 0.02250, observed 0.02296.
  5. Test memorylessness. P(L > 2 | L > 1) = 0.15105, against P(L > 1) = 0.15200. Those agree to three decimals, on 3,800 rows.

Sanity check. The mean must exceed 1, because the support starts at 1. If you computed 0.1765 you used the other convention, and you are off by exactly one.

What changes if you halve the success rate to p = 0.425. The mean doubles, from 1.176 to 2.353. But P(L > 3) rises from 0.0034 to 0.190, a factor of 56. The mean scales gently and the tail does not, which is why retry budgets and timeouts are set from the tail and never from the mean.

7. Two conventions, and one of them will bite you

The reframing Two definitions of "geometric" are in circulation. Trials until the first success: support starts at 1, mean 1/p. Failures before the first success: support starts at 0, mean (1−p)/p. On this column those predict 1.1765 and 0.1765 for the same data. This course and scipy.stats.geom both use the first. Check before you trust a number.

8. Where this bites

  • Rejection sampling. The expected number of draws until one is accepted is 1 divided by the acceptance rate. At a 12 percent acceptance rate that is 8.33 draws per sample.
  • Speculative decoding. Under an assumption that acceptances are independent and identically distributed, the tokens produced per iteration are, in the authors' own words, "a capped geometric variable, with success probability 1 − alpha and cap gamma + 1".
  • Retry budgets. Exactly what the retries column is, and the tail is what sizes the timeout.
code/0131-the-geometric-distribution.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def geometric_tail(k, p):
    """P(L > k) = (1-p)^k, because the first k trials all had to fail."""
    return (1 - p) ** k


def conditional_tail(values, n, k):
    """P(L > n + k | L > n), computed by filtering, with its sample size."""
    survived = values[values > n]
    return float((survived > n + k).mean()), int(survived.size)

The program reports the sample size behind every memorylessness estimate, and the reason is honesty: the n = 1, k = 1 estimate rests on 3,800 rows and agrees to three decimals, while the n = 2, k = 1 estimate rests on 574 rows and comes out at 0.13415 against 0.15200. That wobble is sampling noise, not a failure of the property, and a program that printed only the numbers would let you conclude otherwise.

The gap between a mode of 1 and a mean of 5 is what the long thin tail is doing.
You have drawn twelve rejections in a row from a sampler with a 12 percent acceptance rate. What is the expected number of further draws?
Memorylessness: the twelve rejections carry no information about what comes next, so the expected further wait is 1 over 0.12 = 8.33, unchanged. The second option is the gambler's fallacy. The third is a genuinely reasonable instinct and it answers a different question - if you doubted the rate itself, twelve failures would be evidence about it, but that is inference about p rather than prediction given p, and it is Module 09.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A flaky endpoint succeeds on 60 percent of attempts, independently each time.

Find the probability the first success comes on attempt 3, the probability it takes more than 4 attempts, and the mean number of attempts. Then work out how many retries you would budget to cover 99 percent of calls.

Hint

The tail form is shorter than the PMF for the second and fourth parts. For the budget, solve (1−p)k ≤ 0.01 and remember that k retries means k+1 attempts.

Show the worked solution
  1. First success on attempt 3. Two failures then a success: 0.42 × 0.6 = 0.096.
  2. More than 4 attempts. The first four all fail: 0.44 = 0.0256.
  3. The mean. 1 ÷ 0.6 = 1.667 attempts.
  4. Set up the budget. Solve 0.4k ≤ 0.01 for the number of attempts k.
  5. Take logs. k ≥ ln(0.01) ÷ ln(0.4) = −4.6052 ÷ −0.9163 = 5.026, so 6 attempts, which is 5 retries.

Sanity check. The mean must fall between 1 and 2, because p is above 0.5, and it does. P(L > 4) must be smaller than P(L = 3), because the whole tail past 4 is thinner than a single early term, and 0.0256 is indeed below 0.096. Note too how far the budget sits above the mean: the mean is 1.7 attempts and covering 99 percent needs 6, which is the tail-not-the-mean point from section 6.

Your teacher. Two habits. First, when you meet a waiting time, ask whether the trials are genuinely independent - a retry against a cold cache or a backing-off client is not, and the geometric will lie to you. Second, size any retry or timeout policy from the tail, never from the mean; the mean of a geometric is dragged around by a tail most calls never reach. Ask an agent to tabulate P(L > k) for p = 0.6 and k up to 8 and find where it crosses one percent.
