Shape is the part the summary throws away

foundation 10 min Every number on this page is arithmetic on a column that already exists.

The one-minute version

  • Four datasets can agree on every summary so far and look nothing alike. That is the whole page, delivered before any vocabulary.
  • Skewness says which tail is longer, and its sign is readable straight off the gap between the mean and the median.
  • Kurtosis is tail extremity, not peakedness. That is taught wrongly almost everywhere, and this page measures the difference rather than asserting it.
  • A bound that holds for every shape is loose. A rule that is tight is assuming a shape. You get one or the other.

1. Four datasets, one summary

Here are four small datasets. They agree on the mean of x, the mean of y, both variances, the correlation, and the fitted line. Every one of those numbers matches to two decimal places, and Anscombe chose the values so.

2. Skewness: which tail is longer

Say it in words first. A column is right-skewed when its long tail runs to the right: most values sit low and a few run far high. Latencies do this, and so do incomes, session lengths and basket sizes. Left-skew is the mirror.

skew = (1/n) ∑i ( (xi − x-bar) / s )3 n: how many values are in the column. xi: the i-th value. x-bar: the mean of the column. s: its standard deviation. (xi − x-bar) / s: the value's distance from the mean measured in standard deviations, so the whole expression is unit-free. The cube: this is the part that does the work, because cubing keeps the sign. Values above the mean contribute positively and values below contribute negatively, and a long right tail wins because its distances are large before they are cubed.
The sign convention is the half people get backwards. Positive skew means the tail runs right and the mean sits above the median. It is easy to reason yourself into the opposite by thinking about where the bulk of the data is. Use the mean-median gap as your check: it has the same sign as the skewness and needs no cubing.

3. Kurtosis is about tails, not peaks

The reframing Kurtosis measures tail extremity: how much of the statistic comes from the handful of points furthest from the mean. It does not measure how sharp the peak is, and the widespread teaching that it does is simply wrong.

The formula is the skewness formula with a fourth power instead of a third, and the fourth power is what makes it a tail statistic: raising a distance of 10 standard deviations to the fourth gives 10,000, while a distance of 0.5 gives 0.0625. The far points dominate by construction.

The program measures exactly that. For session_seconds, 91.25 per cent of rows sit within one standard deviation of the mean, and those rows supply 0.09 per cent of the kurtosis. For pages_viewed it is starker: 99.41 per cent of rows contribute 0.0008 per cent. The peak is where the statistic is least sensitive.

Excess kurtosis subtracts 3, so a Normal-shaped column reads zero and the number becomes a comparison rather than an absolute. That is why the table above shows screen_brightness at −1.179: it is uniform, it has no tail at all, and negative excess kurtosis is what "no tail" looks like as a number.

4. Modality, and the columns that should have been two

A column with two humps is usually two populations that were mixed and should not have been: two device types, two pricing tiers, two versions of an instrument. A mean sits in the valley between them and describes nobody. Before reaching for a shape statistic, count the humps.

5. The trade: a bound for any shape, or a rule that assumes one

Chebyshev's inequality says at least 1 − 1/k2 of any column lies within k standard deviations of its mean. It assumes nothing about shape. The empirical rule says about 68, 95 and 99.7 per cent lie within one, two and three, and it assumes a Normal shape. Meanwhile the two real columns miss the Normal figures in opposite directions: the heavy-tailed one has more than 68 per cent inside one standard deviation because its spread is inflated by the tail, and the uniform one has less because it fills its range evenly and then stops.

6. Worked: identical summaries, different data

Two eight-value columns, built to share a mean and a standard deviation exactly.

A = (2, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 8)     B = (3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 12) Read as: two columns of eight values each. A is symmetric about its centre. B has seven values clustered low and one far high. Nothing about either is random; they are two lists of numbers.
  1. Sum each. A: 2+4+4+5+5+6+6+8 = 40. B: 3+3+3+4+4+5+6+12 = 40.
  2. Divide by 8. Both means are 5.0.
  3. Squared deviations for A. 9, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 9, summing to 22.
  4. Squared deviations for B. 4, 4, 4, 1, 1, 0, 1, 49, summing to 64.
  5. Note they differ, so rescale B about its mean by √(22/64) = 0.5863 to force the variances to match. B becomes (3.83, 3.83, 3.83, 4.41, 4.41, 5.00, 5.59, 9.10), same mean, same variance.
  6. Now the medians. A: the middle pair is 5 and 5, so 5.0, equal to the mean. B: sorted, it is 3.83, 3.83, 3.83, 4.41, 4.41, 5.00, 5.59, 9.10, so the middle pair is 4.41 and 4.41 and the median is 4.41, which is below the mean.
  7. Read the sign before computing anything. B's mean sits above its median, so B is right-skewed and A is not.
  8. Confirm with the third moment. A's skewness is 0.000 by symmetry; B's is +1.757. Note it is the same for raw and rescaled B, because skewness is scale-invariant by construction.

Sanity check. A is symmetric about 5, so every positive cubed deviation is matched by an equal negative one and the skewness has to be exactly zero. If your A came out non-zero, the arithmetic slipped.

What changes if you drop B's largest value? The whole thing. B without the 9.10 is nearly symmetric, and one row in eight was carrying the entire shape. That fragility is a property of the statistic worth knowing before you quote one.

What it means. Two columns agreed on both summaries the course had taught you, and a reader shown only those two numbers would have modelled them the same way. The shape is what separates them, and it took a third number and a picture to see it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A column has high kurtosis. What does that tell you about it?
Kurtosis is a fourth-power statistic, so the points furthest from the mean dominate it: on session_seconds the 91 per cent of rows nearest the middle supply 0.09 per cent of it. The first is the textbook-wrong answer this page exists to correct, and the measured experiment shows two columns with visibly different peaks scoring the same. The third has it backwards. The fourth describes skewness.
A column's mean sits well above its median. Which way is it skewed?
The mean follows the tail and the median does not, so a mean above the median means the tail runs right. The first and third are the sign confusion this page's warning callout is about, and the first reasons from where the bulk sits rather than from where the tail goes. The second is false: the gap between them is a shape measurement, which is exactly why the table above prints it.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Two columns with the same mean and, after the rescale in step 5 of the worked example, the same standard deviation:

A = (2, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 8)     B = (3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 12) Read as: eight values each. Nothing is random and nothing is a sample of anything. These are two lists, and every question below is arithmetic on them.

Find the median of each, say which is skewed and in which direction, and then say which you would be willing to model with a symmetric assumption.

Hint

Compute the mean-median gap for both before you touch any third moment. The gap has the same sign as the skewness and takes about ten seconds, so it tells you the answer and then the moment confirms it.

Hint for the modelling call

Ask what a symmetric assumption would predict about how far the largest value should sit from the mean, and then check that against B's largest value.

Show the worked solution
  1. Sort A. It is already sorted: 2, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 8. Eight values, so the median is the average of the fourth and fifth: (5 + 5) / 2 = 5.0.
  2. Mean of A. 40 ÷ 8 = 5.0. Gap zero, so no skew.
  3. Sort B. 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 12. The fourth and fifth values are both 4, so the median is 4.0.
  4. Mean of B. 40 ÷ 8 = 5.0. Gap +1.0, mean above median, so B is right-skewed.
  5. Confirm with the third moment. A is symmetric about 5, so its cubed deviations cancel exactly and its skewness is 0.000. B's works out to +1.757, positive as the gap predicted.
  6. The modelling call. A, and only A. B's standard deviation is 2.83, so its largest value sits 2.48 standard deviations above the mean while its next largest sits at only 0.35. One value in eight carrying that much of the shape is what a symmetric assumption is least able to represent.
  7. Say what a symmetric fit to B would get wrong. It would place as much mass below 2.48 standard deviations as above, and B has nothing down there at all: its smallest value sits only 0.71 standard deviations below the mean.

Sanity check. A right-skewed column has its mean above its median and a positive skewness. Those are two independent calculations of the same fact, so if your two signals disagree, one of the two is wrong and the gap is the one to trust, because it involves no cubing. Check also that A's skewness came out exactly zero rather than merely small: symmetry makes it exact, not approximate.

Your teacher. If the kurtosis correction is the part to keep, ask an agent: "generate two columns with the same variance, one with a sharp peak and one flat, both with a few extreme values, and print their kurtosis". Watching the two numbers agree while the pictures differ is what makes the correction stick. If you want the mean-median trick in your hands, run it on any latency column you have at work: it will be right-skewed, and the gap will tell you before any plot does.
