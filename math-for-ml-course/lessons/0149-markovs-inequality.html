You can promise something about the tail knowing only the average

working 10 min It is a weak promise. It is also the strongest one a single number can buy.

The one-minute version

  • For a non-negative variable, P(X ≥ a) is at most E[X] divided by a. No distribution required.
  • The proof is one step function: replace X by something never larger, average both sides, done.
  • It is loose, and the reason is measurable: almost all the slack is the body of the distribution, not the tail.
  • It is also tight, because a two-point distribution attains it exactly. You cannot do better on one number.

1. The mental model: how much mass can sit far out?

You are told a queue has an average depth of 20 and nothing else. No distribution, no percentiles, no variance. Can you say anything at all about how often it reaches 100?

Yes, and the reasoning is almost embarrassingly simple. Every time the queue is at 100 or more, it contributes at least 100 to the average. If that happened more than a fifth of the time, those occasions alone would push the average above 20, and you were told it is 20. So it happens at most a fifth of the time.

P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X] ÷ a    for X ≥ 0 and a > 0 Read as: the probability that X reaches at least a is no more than the mean of X divided by a. X must be non-negative, which is the assumption doing quiet work, and a is any threshold you care to name.

2. The proof, which is one step function

Theorem Let X be a non-negative random variable with finite mean, and let a be any positive number. Then P(X ≥ a) is at most E[X] divided by a.

Proof. Build a second random variable that is never larger than X, and whose mean you can write down exactly.

Define the step S which equals a wherever X is at least a, and 0 everywhere else. Compare it with X pointwise. Where X is below a, the step is 0 and X is non-negative, so the step is not larger. Where X is at least a, the step is exactly a and X is at least a, so again the step is not larger. That covers every outcome, so S ≤ X always.

E[S] ≤ E[X] Read as: if one random variable is never larger than another, its average cannot be larger either. This is monotonicity of expectation and it is the only property of averages the proof uses.

Now the step that does the work. S takes only two values, so its mean is immediate: it is a times the probability of the larger one.

E[S] = a · P(X ≥ a) Read as: the step is a with probability equal to the chance that X reached a, and 0 otherwise, so its average is a times that chance. Nothing is approximated here; this is exact.

Put the two lines together and divide by a, which is positive so the inequality survives. That gives P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X] ÷ a.

3. Where all the slack lives, measured

On the real latency column the bound is loose by a factor of about 40 at a one-second threshold. It is worth knowing exactly which part of the distribution is responsible, because the usual guess is wrong.

Split the mean into the part contributed by rows in the tail and the part contributed by rows below the threshold. Those two pieces produce two independent sources of slack, and they multiply.

Markov needs X ≥ 0, and the assumption is easy to skip past. Apply it to the signed residual column, whose mean is about 0.007, and it would claim P(residual ≥ 1) is at most 0.007. The true value is 0.163. Negative values dragged the mean down while contributing nothing to the upper tail, and the bound was never valid to begin with.

4. Worked example: a latency promise with one number

  1. State what you know. Mean latency 100 ms. Latency cannot be negative, which is the only other thing the bound needs.
  2. Name the threshold. You want a promise about exceeding 1,000 ms.
  3. Apply the bound. P(X ≥ 1,000) ≤ 100 ÷ 1,000 = 0.10.
  4. Read it aloud. At most one request in ten exceeds a second. That is a real guarantee, made with no distributional assumption whatever.
  5. Now tighten the threshold. At 4,000 ms the bound is 100 ÷ 4,000 = 0.025.
  6. Notice the shape. The bound falls like one over the threshold, so pushing ten times further out buys only ten times less probability. A real tail usually falls far faster.
  7. Sanity check. The bound must exceed the truth and it must never exceed 1. At a = 50 it would read 2.0, which is useless but not wrong: every probability is at most 1 anyway.
  8. What changes if you learn one more number. Add a standard deviation of 40 ms and the next lesson turns the same 1,000 ms question into a bound of one in 506, a fiftyfold improvement from one extra piece of information.
code/0149-markovs-inequality.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
x = frame["latency_ms"].to_numpy(float)
a = 1_000.0

# The proof, as arithmetic: build the step and compare it with X.
step = np.where(x >= a, a, 0.0)
assert (step <= x).all()                       # never larger
assert np.isclose(step.mean(), a * (x >= a).mean())   # and its mean is exact

print("Markov bound", x.mean() / a)
print("true tail   ", (x >= a).mean())

The full program checks the bound at five thresholds, decomposes the slack into the two factors above and asserts that they multiply to it, and constructs the two-point distribution that attains the bound with a ratio of exactly 1.

5. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You know only that a non-negative quantity averages 50. Bound the chance it reaches 500.
Markov gives 50 divided by 500, which is 0.10, from the mean alone. The first option is the belief this page exists to remove. The third assumes a normal distribution you were not given, which is the most dangerous move here because it produces a confident number from an assumption nobody made.
Markov's bound is about forty times the true tail on real latency. Is it therefore a bad bound?
There is a distribution that hits the bound with equality, so it cannot be improved using only the mean. The looseness measures how much your particular data differs from that worst case, not a defect in the inequality. The second option is the dangerous one: swapping in a normal replaces a proved guarantee with an unproved assumption.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A build pipeline takes 8 minutes on average. Builds cannot take negative time, and you know nothing else about the distribution.

Find the largest fraction of builds that can take 40 minutes or more, then construct a distribution with mean 8 that actually achieves that fraction, and say what its existence proves.

Hint

For the second part, the worst case puts as much mass as possible exactly at the threshold and the rest as low as it can go. Non-negativity says how low that is.

Show the worked solution
  1. Apply the bound. P(X ≥ 40) ≤ 8 ÷ 40 = 0.2, so at most one build in five.
  2. Set up the worst case. Put probability p at exactly 40 and the remaining 1 - p at 0, which is the lowest a non-negative variable may go.
  3. Match the mean. The mean of that distribution is 40p + 0 = 40p, and it must equal 8.
  4. Solve. p = 8 ÷ 40 = 0.2.
  5. Check the tail. P(X ≥ 40) is exactly 0.2 for this distribution, which is exactly the bound.
  6. Say what it proves. The bound cannot be improved. Any inequality using only the mean must allow 0.2 here, because a real distribution reaches it.
  7. Say what it does not prove. It says nothing about your pipeline, whose builds are certainly not all either instant or 40 minutes.
  8. What changes if builds have a floor. Suppose no build is faster than 3 minutes. Then the worst case puts 1 - p at 3, the mean becomes 40p + 3(1-p) = 8, so p = 5/37 = 0.135. One extra fact tightened the bound by a third.

Sanity check. The answer must be a probability, so between 0 and 1, and it must fall as the threshold rises. If a threshold below the mean gives a number above 1, that is correct and simply uninformative, because every probability is at most 1 without any theorem.

Your teacher. Take one service level objective you currently state as a percentile and ask what you could still promise if you lost the histogram and kept only the mean. The answer is usually far weaker than the objective, and the gap is a measure of how much your percentile depends on the data continuing to look as it does today.
