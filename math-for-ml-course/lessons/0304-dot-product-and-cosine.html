One multiply-and-add answers two different questions

foundation 10 min The dot product carries direction and magnitude together. Dividing by the two lengths is what pulls them apart.

The one-minute version

  • The dot product multiplies two vectors entry by entry and adds the results. It is the only arithmetic in the subject.
  • Its sign says whether two vectors point the same way, at right angles, or apart.
  • Cauchy-Schwarz is what guarantees the ratio of the dot product to the two lengths is always a cosine, so the angle is well defined in any number of dimensions.
  • A raw dot product is not a similarity. On this course's corpus, the nearest neighbour of a 100-word article by dot product is a 7,061-word article about a different subject.

The operation

a · b = aTb = a1b1 + a2b2 + … + anbn Read as: a and b are vectors with the same number of entries, n. Multiply the first entries together, the second entries together, and so on, then add all n products. The answer is a single number, not a vector. The middle form is the same thing written as a matrix product, which lesson 05 explains.

For (10, 6, 4) and (2, 1, 20) that is 20 + 6 + 80 = 106. One number out of six.

This is the arithmetic underneath a single neuron, which computes a dot product of weights and inputs before anything is bent. It is also the arithmetic underneath attention: Vaswani and colleagues compute a dot product between every query and every key, and the whole mechanism is those numbers passed through a softmax.

What the sign means

Two non-zero vectors make an acute angle when their dot product is positive, a right angle when it is zero, and an obtuse angle when it is negative. The right-angle case has its own name: the vectors are orthogonal, written with a perpendicular sign, and by convention the zero vector is orthogonal to everything.

Why the ratio is always a cosine

The angle between two non-zero vectors is defined by dividing the dot product by both lengths and taking the inverse cosine. That definition only makes sense if the ratio never leaves the range from −1 to 1, because the cosine of a real angle never does. The guarantee has a name.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

Claim. For any two vectors a and b with the same number of entries, |aTb| ≤ ||a|| ||b||.

Proof. If either vector is zero, both sides are zero and the claim holds. So assume both are non-zero and write α = ||a|| and β = ||b||, both positive. A norm is never negative, so its square is never negative, and in particular

0 ≤ ||βa − αb||2 = β2||a||2 − 2βα(aTb) + α2||b||2 = 2||a||2||b||2 − 2||a|| ||b||(aTb) Read as: the first step expands the squared norm the way any square of a difference expands, using ||x||2 = xTx. The second step substitutes α = ||a|| and β = ||b||, which makes the first and third terms identical, so they combine.

Dividing through by 2||a|| ||b||, which is positive and therefore does not flip the inequality, leaves aTb ≤ ||a|| ||b||. Running the same argument with −a in place of a gives −aTb ≤ ||a|| ||b||. A number and its negative are both at most M exactly when its size is at most M, so |aTb| ≤ ||a|| ||b||. ■

So the ratio is trapped between −1 and 1, and the definition below is sound in any dimension.

θ = arccos( aTb / (||a|| ||b||) ) Read as: divide the dot product by the length of a and by the length of b, then take the inverse cosine, which returns an angle between 0 and 180 degrees. That ratio is called the cosine similarity of the two vectors. Scaling either vector by a positive number leaves it unchanged.

The triangle inequality follows

Claim. ||a + b|| ≤ ||a|| + ||b||, which is the property lesson 03 required of every norm.

Proof. Expand the square, then apply Cauchy-Schwarz to the middle term: ||a + b||2 = ||a||2 + 2aTb + ||b||2 ≤ ||a||2 + 2||a|| ||b|| + ||b||2 = (||a|| + ||b||)2. Both sides are non-negative, so taking square roots preserves the inequality. ■

Why a raw dot product is not a similarity

Here is the failure, in numbers small enough to check. Three documents over the vocabulary (model, data, loss): d1 = (10, 6, 4), d2 = (100, 60, 40), and d3 = (2, 1, 20). The second is the first written ten times longer.

The dot product of d1 and d2 is 1520, and of d1 and d3 is 106, so a raw score says d2 is fourteen times more like d1. The cosine of the first pair is exactly 1.0000 and of the second 0.4272, which is the honest answer: d2 is identical in content to d1 and merely longer. Manning, Raghavan and Schuetze name exactly this effect: two documents with very similar content can differ a great deal as vectors simply because one is longer.

One useful consequence. If every vector is normalised to length 1 first, the dot product is the cosine, and ranking by cosine and ranking by Euclidean distance give the identical order, because ||u − v||2 = 2 − 2(u · v) when both have length 1. That is why a vector index normalises once at build time and then uses whichever primitive is faster.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. q is a query and p1, p2, p3 are passages, each a vector of term counts over (model, data, loss).
  2. Take the numbers. q = (5, 3, 2), p1 = (10, 6, 4), p2 = (100, 60, 40), p3 = (2, 1, 20).
  3. Score by dot product. q·p1 = 50 + 18 + 8 = 76. q·p2 = 500 + 180 + 80 = 760. q·p3 = 10 + 3 + 40 = 53. The order is p2, p1, p3.
  4. Notice a shortcut. q is exactly 0.5p1, and scaling by a positive number does not change any angle, so the cosine with p1 must be 1 without computing anything.
  5. Get the lengths. ||q|| = √38 = 6.1644, ||p3|| = √405 = 20.1246.
  6. Score by cosine. Both p1 and p2 come to 1.0000. For p3, 53 / (6.1644 × 20.1246) = 0.4272, an angle of 64.71 degrees.
  7. Check against the bound. Cauchy-Schwarz says every one of these ratios must lie in [−1, 1], and 1.0000 sits exactly at the boundary, which the proof said happens only when one vector is a positive multiple of the other. It is.
  8. Say what would change. With term frequencies divided by document length instead of raw counts, the dot product ranking would already agree with the cosine one. Normalising the data and normalising the score are two routes to the same place.
m03-l04-dot-cosine.py · runnable Python, numpy and pandas only
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

docs = pd.read_csv("../datasets/documents.csv")
terms = [c for c in docs.columns if c not in ("doc_id", "topic")]
counts = docs[terms].to_numpy(float)
norms = np.linalg.norm(counts, axis=1)

query = 2570                                  # a 100-word machine-learning article
dot = counts @ counts[query]
cos = dot / (norms * norms[query])

print(docs.topic[np.argsort(-dot)[1]])        # football
print(docs.topic[np.argsort(-cos)[1]])        # machine_learning

The full program checks Cauchy-Schwarz on 20,000 random pairs, confirms the ratio never leaves [−1, 1], and measures both rankings against the topic labels over 500 queries.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

For d1 = (10, 6, 4) and d2 = (100, 60, 40), what is the cosine between them?
The second vector is exactly ten times the first, and a positive multiple leaves the angle at zero, so the cosine is 1. The first mistakes the cosine for the ratio of the lengths. The second is impossible: Cauchy-Schwarz confines every cosine to the range minus one to one. The third would need a dot product of zero, and this one is 1520.
Two document vectors have a dot product of 1060 and a cosine of 0.4272. What follows?
A large dot product beside a middling cosine means the lengths are doing the work, because the cosine has divided them out and dropped to 0.43. The first would require a cosine near 1. The third would require a negative cosine. The fourth is the misunderstanding this page exists to remove: the two quantities are independent, and that is exactly why both are worth having.
Practice

A retrieval index holds three passages as raw term-count vectors over (model, data, loss): p1 = (10, 6, 4), p2 = (100, 60, 40), p3 = (2, 1, 20). A query arrives as q = (5, 3, 2).

  1. Rank the three passages by raw dot product with q.
  2. Rank them by cosine.
  3. Which ranking would you serve from a search box, and what single preprocessing step would make the two rankings agree from then on?
Hint

Before computing any cosine, look hard at q and p1. One of them is a scalar multiple of the other, and that fact settles two of the three cosines with no arithmetic at all.

Solution
  1. q·p1 = 76, q·p2 = 760, q·p3 = 53. Order: p2, p1, p3.
  2. q = 0.5p1 and p2 = 10p1, so both cosines are exactly 1.0 and the two tie at the top. For p3: 53 / (√38 × √405) = 0.4272. Order: p1 and p2 tied, then p3.
  3. Serve the cosine ranking. p2 is only p1 written ten times longer and has no business outranking it. The preprocessing step is to normalise every vector to length 1 when the index is built: after that the dot product equals the cosine, so the two rankings are the same ranking and the cheaper primitive can be used.

Sanity check: every cosine you compute must land between −1 and 1. If one does not, the arithmetic is wrong, because Cauchy-Schwarz forbids it.

Your teacher. If the dot-product-is-not-similarity point has not landed, ask an agent to take any two documents you like, concatenate one with itself five times, and print the dot product and cosine against a fixed query before and after. The cosine will not move. The dot product will multiply by five.
