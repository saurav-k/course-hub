The same slope is worthless on eight rows and decisive on twenty thousand

frontier 12 min Part 3 of 3, and the course's last page. Module 9 owns this machinery. This page runs it once, on the fit Part 2 produced.

The one-minute version

  • Eight rows gave a slope of 0.060141. Twenty thousand gave 0.060111. Almost identical, and one of them means nothing.
  • Their standard errors are 0.093435 and 0.000762. The eight-row interval straddles zero; the full one is 123 times narrower.
  • Hand the same machinery the null column and it says so: t of 0.284, and an interval running from negative to positive.
  • Predicting the average spend at 300 seconds is 126 times more precise than predicting one visitor's, and only one of those is usually what you were asked.

1. Two fits, one slope, two verdicts

Part 2 fitted the same model twice, once on eight hand-workable rows and once on all 20,000. The slopes agree to four significant figures. Everything else about them differs.

SE(b1) = √( σ̂2 / Sxx ),  where σ̂2 = SSE / (n - 2) Read as: the standard error of the estimated slope is the square root of the estimated error variance divided by Sxx, the total squared spread of the session lengths about their own mean. The error variance σ̂2 is the sum of squared residuals divided by n minus 2, losing one degree of freedom for the intercept and one for the slope. It is how far a repeat of this whole experiment would typically move the slope.

The two fits put very different numbers into that formula. Both estimate roughly the same error spread, σ̂ of 26.9 against 27.1, so the residual noise is not what separates them. What separates them is Sxx: 82,959 across eight rows against 1,262,098,140 across twenty thousand.

Dividing the slope by its own standard error gives the statistic Module 9 uses to decide. On eight rows it is 0.060141 ÷ 0.093435 = 0.64, which is well inside the range you would expect from noise alone. On 20,000 rows it is 0.060111 ÷ 0.000762 = 78.85, which is not.

The standard error fell by a factor of 123 while the row count rose by a factor of 2,500. That is not the plain square-root law from Module 8, which would predict 50. The extra gain is Sxx: the eight rows were deliberately drawn from the middle of the range, while the full column includes sessions past 8,000 seconds. Spread in the predictor buys precision in the slope on top of what count alone buys, which is the whole argument for varying an input widely when you get to choose.

2. Hand the same machinery a column that carries nothing

Part 1 showed that screen_brightness correlates with spend at 0.002. Fit it anyway, with the identical procedure, and read what comes back.

  1. Fit the line. The estimated slope on brightness is 0.002161 rupees per unit. It is not zero, and it never would be: no estimate from finite data lands exactly on zero.
  2. Compute its standard error. 0.007620, which is larger than the estimate itself.
  3. Take the ratio. 0.002161 ÷ 0.007620 = 0.284, against 78.85 for session length.
  4. Read the two-sided p-value. 0.777. If brightness truly had no effect, data at least this extreme would turn up 78 per cent of the time.
  5. Write the interval. -0.012774 to 0.017096, which contains zero comfortably and is roughly symmetric about it.

The procedure did not need to be told which column was real. Given a column carrying nothing, it returned an interval that includes nothing, and the machinery that made that judgement is the same three lines of arithmetic in both cases.

3. The average visitor and one visitor are different questions

Ask the fitted model what a 300-second session is worth and it answers 48.21 rupees. Two different intervals surround that one number, and Module 9 derives both.

CI: σ̂2(1/n + (x0 - x̄)2/Sxx)  against  PI: σ̂2(1 + 1/n + (x0 - x̄)2/Sxx) Read as: the squared width of the interval for the mean spend at session length x0, against the squared width of the interval for one new session at the same length. The only difference is the extra 1, which is the variance of a single new error term. Everything else is the uncertainty in where the line itself sits.

That single extra 1 dominates. The interval for the mean at 300 seconds is 0.843 rupees wide. The interval for one new visitor is 106.167 rupees wide, a factor of 126.

The prediction interval at 300 seconds runs from -4.87 to 101.30 rupees, and spend cannot be negative. The error model assumes errors are symmetric and unbounded, which is convenient and false here, and at the low end the arithmetic walks straight off the edge of what the data can be. Nothing in the procedure noticed. A model does not know what you know about your own quantity, and an interval that includes impossible values is the model telling you its assumption is doing more work than it can carry.

4. What this fit does not establish

Two limits, stated plainly, because a capstone that ends on the t-statistic teaches the wrong instinct.

The first is size. R-squared is 0.2372, so session length accounts for under a quarter of the variation in spend. The slope is real and small: one more minute is worth 60 × 0.060111 = 3.61 rupees on average, against a mean spend of 40.49. A very large sample makes a small effect visible; it does not make it big.

The second is direction. Nothing in this arithmetic distinguishes longer sessions causing more spend from more engaged buyers causing longer sessions, and both would produce this table. Module 10's warning applies to a fitted coefficient exactly as it applies to a correlation, because the coefficient is the correlation wearing units.

The reframing You did not learn eleven separate subjects. You learned one procedure with eleven parts: state the question, look at the data's shape, choose a model, fit it, and then find out how much the fit is worth. Every paper that reports a coefficient is running this loop, and you can now read all five stages of it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Going from 8 rows to 20,000 took the t-statistic from 0.64 to 78.85. What changed?
The slope barely moved, from 0.060141 to 0.060111, so nothing about the effect itself changed. The residual spread barely moved either, from 26.9 to 27.1, which is precisely why the noise is not the explanation. And the eight-row estimate was not wrong: it was close to the truth and simply could not be distinguished from zero, which is a different failing and the one the standard error exists to report.
Brightness gave t = 0.284 and p = 0.777. What is the correct way to report it?
Failing to find evidence is not the same as finding evidence of absence, so the honest report is that nothing showed up. Claiming no effect has been shown overstates it: the interval still admits effects up to 0.017. Blaming the sample size gets it backwards, since 20,000 rows made the interval narrow enough to rule out anything sizeable. And asserting the effect is real reads a conclusion into a test that gave none.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A colleague fits the eight-row extract and reports "a slope of 0.0601 rupees per second, matching the full dataset". From the eight-row fit you have: SSE = 4,345.44, Sxx = 82,958.9, and n = 8.

σ̂2 = SSE / (n - 2),  SE(b1) = √( σ̂2 / Sxx ),  t = b1 / SE(b1) Read as: estimate the error variance by dividing the residual sum of squares by n minus 2, take the standard error of the slope from it, then divide the slope by its own standard error to get the test statistic.

Compute the standard error and the t-statistic, build the rough 95 per cent interval using a critical value of 2.45 for 6 degrees of freedom, and say in one sentence what is wrong with the colleague's report.

Hint

Do not compare the two slopes. Compute the interval around the eight-row slope first, then check whether zero is inside it. Whether a number matches another number is not the question the interval answers.

Show the worked solution
  1. Estimate the error variance. 4,345.44 ÷ (8 - 2) = 4,345.44 ÷ 6 = 724.24, so σ̂ = √724.24 = 26.91 rupees.
  2. Divide by the predictor spread. 724.24 ÷ 82,958.9 = 0.008730.
  3. Take the square root. SE(b1) = √0.008730 = 0.093435.
  4. Form the statistic. t = 0.060141 ÷ 0.093435 = 0.644 on 6 degrees of freedom.
  5. Build the interval. 0.060141 ± 2.45 × 0.093435 = 0.060141 ± 0.228916, giving -0.169 to 0.289.
  6. Say what is wrong. The interval contains zero, so these eight rows are equally consistent with session length having no effect at all, and with an effect four times larger. Reporting the point estimate as a match hides that the data cannot tell those apart.

Sanity check. The interval must contain the point estimate, and 0.060141 sits inside -0.169 to 0.289. With t below 2 the interval must also contain zero, and it does. If your interval had excluded zero while t was 0.64, the arithmetic went wrong somewhere in steps two and three.

Your teacher. The habit worth taking out of this course is one question, asked every time you meet a coefficient in a paper, a dashboard or a colleague's slide: what is its standard error? If the answer is not on the page, you have been shown a point estimate and invited to treat it as a finding. That question is most of what eleven modules were for.
