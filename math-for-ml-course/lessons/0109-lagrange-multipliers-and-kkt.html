Constrained optimization: Lagrange multipliers and KKT

working 13 min The conditions and the sufficiency proof are Boyd & Vandenberghe, section 5.5.3. · Convex Optimization

The one-minute version

  • At the best feasible point, the objective's contour just touches the constraint. Touching means the two gradients are parallel, and the multiplier is the ratio between them.
  • An inequality constraint is either active, and has a price, or irrelevant, and has a price of zero. Never both. That is all complementary slackness says.
  • The four KKT conditions are necessary in general and sufficient when the problem is convex. Lesson 101 is what earns that second half.
  • Write PCA as a constrained problem and the multiplier turns out to be the eigenvalue, which is the captured variance. They are one number.

1. The picture, before any algebra

Draw the objective's contours and one constraint curve. Walk along the constraint looking for the lowest contour you can reach. At the best point, the contour you are on just touches the curve rather than crossing it.

Why touching? Because if the contour crossed the constraint, you could slide a little along the constraint to the side where the objective is lower, and you would not have been at the best point. Only tangency leaves nowhere to slide.

2. Tangency written as algebra

Two vectors lying on the same line means one is a multiple of the other. Give that multiple a name and you have invented the multiplier:

∇f(x) = ν ∇h(x) f: the objective being minimised. h: the constraint function, where the constraint is h(x) = 0. ∇f, ∇h: their gradients at the point in question, both vectors. ν: a single number, the multiple. It is positive if the two gradients point the same way and negative if they oppose, and its size says how much longer one arrow is than the other.

The Lagrangian is bookkeeping for that statement: define L(x, ν) = f(x) − νh(x), and setting its gradient with respect to x to zero reproduces the line above exactly.

3. Inequalities add exactly one new fact

With f(x) ≤ 0 rather than h(x) = 0, the optimum is in one of two states and there is no third.

4. The four conditions, and what each forbids

The theorem If the objective and inequality constraints are convex and the equality constraints affine, then any point satisfying the four KKT conditions is optimal. Without convexity they are only necessary, and a point satisfying them may be a saddle.

Why sufficiency holds, in five steps

  1. Feasibility. The first two conditions say the candidate is feasible, so it is a legitimate answer to compare against.
  2. The Lagrangian is convex in x. Every multiplier is non-negative, every constraint function convex, every equality affine, so L is a non-negative combination of convex functions plus affine terms.
  3. Stationarity therefore means minimised. This is the step that does the real work. For a convex differentiable function a vanishing gradient is a global minimum, so the candidate minimises L over all x, not merely locally. Convexity is doing the entire job here, and without it this step is false.
  4. Evaluate the dual function there. It equals f plus the two sums, and every term of both sums vanishes: the inequality terms by complementary slackness, the equality terms by feasibility. So the dual value equals the primal value.
  5. Squeeze. Weak duality says the dual value is never above the optimum, and feasibility says the primal value is never below it. Equal values force both to be the optimum, with zero gap.

5. The multiplier is a price

The multiplier is not only algebraic bookkeeping. It measures how much the optimum improves per unit the constraint is relaxed, to first order. That is measurable, and the practice problem measures it.

6. Worked: PCA is a Lagrange multiplier problem

Module 04 derived principal components through the SVD. Here is the same result by an entirely different route, which is why this page carries it: two independent derivations of one answer is worth more than either alone.

  1. State the problem. Maximise wTSw subject to wTw = 1, where S is the covariance matrix. The constraint is there because otherwise you would just make w enormous.
  2. Write the Lagrangian. L(w, λ) = wTSw − λ(wTw − 1).
  3. Differentiate with respect to w and set to zero. 2Sw − 2λw = 0.
  4. Read what that says. Sw = λw. The stationary points are exactly the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and no eigenvalue was assumed anywhere: it fell out of the multiplier.
  5. Substitute back into the objective. wTSw = wT(λw) = λ(wTw) = λ, using the constraint.
  6. Collect. The objective's value at a stationary point is the multiplier. So the maximiser is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue, and the variance it captures is that eigenvalue.
  7. Check it numerically. On the credit dataset's standardised covariance, the largest eigenvalue from a direct eigensolve is 2.0784985219. Running projected gradient ascent on wTSw with w renormalised each step gives 2.0784985219, agreeing to 8.9 × 10-16.

Sanity check. The constraint fixes the length of w at 1, so the objective cannot be made large by scaling, and the answer has to come from direction alone. That is what makes the problem well posed and it is why the constraint is not optional.

What changes if you drop the constraint? The problem has no maximum: doubling w quadruples the objective forever. The constraint is what turns an unbounded problem into an eigenvalue problem.

What it means. This is also where this module's notation collision resolves. The course uses λ for a Lagrange multiplier and λ for a Hessian eigenvalue, which looks careless until you meet this derivation. Here they are the same number, because the multiplier of the unit-norm constraint is the eigenvalue.

The second payoff, named rather than derived. In a support vector machine, complementary slackness says the multiplier is zero for every training point strictly outside the margin. That is not a fact bolted onto SVMs afterwards, it is the reason only the support vectors matter, and it falls straight out of the third KKT condition.
code/0109-lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
    # Route 2: the constrained optimization this page teaches, run as an
    # algorithm rather than solved in closed form.
    w = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, covariance.shape[0])
    w /= np.linalg.norm(w)
    for step in range(1, 20_001):
        w = w + 0.01 * (2.0 * covariance @ w)     # ascend the objective
        w /= np.linalg.norm(w)                    # project back to the sphere

Two lines: step uphill, then project back onto the constraint set. That is projected gradient ascent, and it is the most direct way to see a constraint enforced as an algorithm rather than as algebra. It reaches the same answer as numpy.linalg.eigh to machine precision.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Solve, by Lagrange multipliers:

minimise x2 + y2   subject to   x + y = 2 Read as: find the point closest to the origin, in squared distance, among all points whose two coordinates add to 2. The objective is the squared distance and the constraint is a straight line.

Then relax the constraint to x + y = 2.1 and check whether the objective changes by about λ times 0.1.

Hint

Write the stationarity conditions for x and for y separately and compare them before using the constraint at all. What they say about the relationship between x and y solves most of the problem on its own.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the Lagrangian. L = x2 + y2 − λ(x + y − 2).
  2. Stationarity in x. 2x − λ = 0, so 2x = λ.
  3. Stationarity in y. 2y − λ = 0, so 2y = λ.
  4. Compare them. Both equal λ, so x = y, and that is the geometry settled before any arithmetic.
  5. Use the constraint. x + x = 2 gives x = y = 1, and λ = 2x = 2. The objective is 1 + 1 = 2.
  6. Relax to 2.1. The same argument gives x = y = 1.05, and the objective is 2 × 1.052 = 2.205.
  7. Compare against the prediction. The actual rise is 0.205. The multiplier predicted λ × 0.1 = 0.200.
  8. Explain the 0.005. The multiplier is a first-order price, exact only in the limit of a vanishing relaxation. The gap is the second-order term, and it grows with the size of the relaxation: at b = 3 the prediction is 1.25 against an actual 1.375.

Sanity check. Your λ must come out positive here, and the objective must rise when the line moves further from the origin. If it falls, you have relaxed in the direction that makes the problem easier and should re-read which side of x + y = b the feasible set lies on. Note also that x = y follows from the two stationarity conditions alone, before the constraint is used: if your solution needed the constraint to get there, you have done more work than necessary.

Your teacher. If the tangency picture is the part to keep, ask an agent: "draw the contours of x squared plus y squared and the line x plus y equals 2, and mark where a contour touches the line". Seeing the touching point sit exactly where the two gradients line up is the whole page in one image. If the sufficiency proof felt slippery, the thing to hold is step 3: convexity is what turns "the gradient is zero" into "this is the global minimum", and every other step is bookkeeping.
