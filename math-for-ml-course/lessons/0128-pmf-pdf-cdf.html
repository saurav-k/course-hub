One object, three descriptions, and a density is not a probability

working 12 min The CDF always exists; the PMF is its jump and the PDF is its slope. · Hajek, ECE 313, sections 3.1 and 3.2

The one-minute version

  • The CDF, F(c) = P{X ≤ c}, exists for every random variable of either kind. Start there and you need no case split.
  • For a discrete variable the CDF is a staircase, and the PMF is the height of each step.
  • For a continuous one the CDF is a ramp, and the PDF is its slope. Probability is the area underneath.
  • A density can exceed 1. A published weight initialiser has a density of 6.53, and nothing is wrong.
  • For a continuous variable P(X = v) = 0 for every single v, so endpoints never matter.
This page needs the definite integral and the derivative, from Module 05. That dependency is the reason Module 05 is taught before Module 07 even though probability feels more elementary. If the integral sign is still unfamiliar, read Module 05's two pages on integration first; nothing here works around it.

1. Start with the CDF, because it always exists

F(c) = P{X ≤ c} F: the cumulative distribution function. c: any real number at all, not only a value X can take. P{X ≤ c}: the probability of the event that X comes out at most c. Defined for every random variable, discrete or continuous, without exception.

Three properties characterise it, and a function with all three is the CDF of some random variable.

  1. Nondecreasing. Raising c can only add outcomes to the event, never remove them.
  2. It runs from 0 to 1. Far to the left the event is empty; far to the right it is everything.
  3. Right continuous. The value at a jump belongs to the top of the step, which is a consequence of writing ≤ rather than <.

2. Discrete: the staircase and its steps

The theorem, and why the third property matters

Claim. For any random variable, P{X < c} is the limit of F approaching c from the left, and therefore P{X = c} = F(c) − F(c−): the mass sitting exactly on a point is the size of the jump there.

What is assumed: only the axioms and the definition above.

The shape: build the event {X < c} out of countably many disjoint pieces creeping up to c, add them with axiom P.2, and recognise the limit.

  1. Choose a sequence creeping up to c. Take c1 < c2 < … with limit c.
  2. Cut the event into disjoint pieces. Let G1 be {X ≤ c1} and Gj be {cj-1 < X ≤ cj}. These do not overlap, and their union over all j is exactly {X < c}.
  3. Add them. Countable additivity gives P{X < c} as the infinite sum of the P(Gj).
  4. Notice the partial sums telescope. The first n terms add up to P{X ≤ cn}, which is F(cn).
  5. Take the limit. So P{X < c} = F(c−), and subtracting from F(c) gives P{X = c}.
The step that does the real work is the telescoping in step 4, which is where countable additivity gets spent. And this is why the CDF is defined with ≤ rather than <: that choice puts the value at a jump on the upper step, which is exactly what makes F right continuous and makes the subtraction come out as the jump rather than as zero.

An honest boundary. The three properties are not only necessary but sufficient: any function with them is some random variable's CDF. The direction proved above is the easy one. The converse needs a construction that is beyond this course, and saying so is better than implying you have seen it.

3. Continuous: the ramp and its slope

F(c) = ∫−∞c f(u) du,   and therefore   f = F′ f: the probability density function. ∫ from minus infinity to c: the area under f to the left of c. F′: the derivative of F, by the fundamental theorem of calculus. A random variable is called continuous-type exactly when such an f exists.

There are no jumps in a continuous CDF, so by the theorem above P{X = v} = 0 for every single value v. That sounds alarming and it is not: probability lives in intervals, and all four ways of writing an interval - with each endpoint in or out - give the same number.

4. A density is not a probability

This is the misconception this page exists to remove. The Deep Learning book states the requirement precisely: a density must be non-negative, and "we do not require p(x) ≤ 1".

Squeeze a fixed area of 1 into a narrow interval and the height has nowhere to go but up.

5. Worked: the round trip on a real column

The retries column counts attempts until an upstream call succeeded.

  1. Build the PMF by counting. P(1) = 0.8480, P(2) = 0.1290, P(3) = 0.0199, P(4) = 0.0027, P(5) = 0.0004.
  2. Accumulate into the CDF. 0.8480, then 0.9770, then 0.9969, then 0.9996, then 1.0000.
  3. Go back the other way. F(2) − F(1) = 0.9770 − 0.8480 = 0.1290, which is the PMF at 2. The round trip closes.
  4. Evaluate between attainable values. F(1.5) = 0.8480, the same as F(1), because nothing sits between 1 and 2. A CDF is flat wherever there is no mass.
  5. Now the continuous column. For latency_ms, F(150) = 0.1360, F(180) = 0.4807, F(200) = 0.7414 and F(300) = 0.9915.
  6. Read a CDF backwards. The value with 99 percent of the mass below it is 283.93 ms. That is what a p99 latency is: a CDF statement, solved for c instead of for F.

Sanity check. A CDF must reach exactly 1 at the largest attainable value and must never decrease. Both hold here, and if the accumulated total had overshot 1 the counting was wrong.

What changes if you ask for P{X < 2} instead of P{X ≤ 2}. For retries the answer drops from 0.9770 to 0.8480, a difference of 0.1290, which is exactly the mass sitting on 2. For latency_ms the same change makes no difference at all, because a continuous variable has no mass on any single point. That is the practical face of the theorem in section 2: with a discrete variable you must be careful about endpoints, and with a continuous one you never have to be.

6. Where this bites

Two places, both routine.

Percentile latency is a CDF. "p99 is 284 ms" says F(284) = 0.99. Reading it off a histogram is reading a density and squinting; reading it off a sorted array is evaluating the CDF exactly.

Inverse-transform sampling is the CDF run backwards. Every sampler starts from Uniform(0,1) and pushes it through F-1, which is why the uniform distribution in Lesson 130 is the seed of everything else.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A published initialiser draws weights uniformly on plus or minus 0.0766. What is the height of its density, and is that a problem?
The density is 1 divided by the width, so 1 over 0.1531 is 6.53, and the area is still exactly 1. The second option is the misconception: nothing caps a density, only the integral. The first and fourth quote the half-width and the width, which are inputs to the calculation rather than its answer, and both are seductive because they are conveniently under 1.
Which of the three CDF properties does a curve that dips downward somewhere violate?
Raising the threshold can only add outcomes to the event, so F can never fall. The second option confuses direction with continuity: a dip can be perfectly smooth and still illegal. The third is wrong because a curve can dip in the middle and still reach 1. The fourth is the trap, and it fails because the properties must hold everywhere, not only at the ends.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

For the retries column, F(1) = 0.8480 and F(3) = 0.9969.

Find P{2 ≤ X ≤ 3} and P{X > 3}. Then say why the first of those would be written differently for a continuous variable.

Hint

Both are differences of CDF values. For the first, be careful about which endpoint the ≤ includes, and remember that F(1) already contains everything at or below 1.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take everything up to 3. F(3) = 0.9969.
  2. Remove everything up to 1. F(1) = 0.8480, and this correctly removes the mass at 1 while keeping the mass at 2 and 3.
  3. Subtract. 0.9969 − 0.8480 = 0.1489.
  4. Take the upper tail. P{X > 3} = 1 − F(3) = 1 − 0.9969 = 0.0031.
  5. Say what changes for a continuous variable. There would be no mass sitting on 2, so P{2 ≤ X ≤ 3} and P{2 < X < 3} would be the same number, and the careful choice of F(1) rather than F(2) in step 2 would be unnecessary.

Sanity check. The three pieces must partition the whole: F(1) + P{2 ≤ X ≤ 3} + P{X > 3} = 0.8480 + 0.1489 + 0.0031 = 1.0000. If they do not sum to 1, an endpoint was counted twice or dropped, which is the only error this calculation really admits.

Your teacher. When a question asks "at most" or "at least", reach for the CDF. When it asks "what shape" or "where is the mass", reach for the PMF or PDF. Almost every confusion between the three is really a question asked in the wrong one. And whenever you see a density above 1, do not correct it - multiply it by the width and check you get 1. Ask an agent to sketch the CDF of a fair die and to mark on it where each of the six probabilities lives.
