Mutual information sees dependence that correlation cannot

working 11 min Shannon (1948), page 21, where the three identities below appear together as the rate of transmission.

The one-minute version

  • Compare the joint distribution with what it would be if the two columns were unrelated. That gap is mutual information.
  • It is a KL divergence, so lesson 182 already proved it is non-negative, and zero only under exact independence.
  • Three formulas, one number: a divergence, a drop in uncertainty, and a sum of three entropies.
  • Zero correlation does not mean independent. Zero mutual information does. This is the whole reason the quantity exists.
  • Estimating it from counts is biased upward, badly, and the bias grows with the number of distinct values.

1. The comparison

Two columns, a feature X and a label Y. If they were unrelated, the chance of a particular pair would be the product of the two separate probabilities, p(x)p(y). That is what independence means and it is the only thing it means. You have the real joint distribution, so compare the two, and you already own a tool for comparing distributions.

I(X ; Y) = DKL( p(x, y) ‖ p(x) p(y) ) Read as: the mutual information between X and Y is the KL divergence from the true joint distribution to the joint distribution you would have if the two were independent. The semicolon is conventional and separates the two variables; unlike the double bar in a KL, this quantity is symmetric in its two arguments.

Two things follow immediately from lesson 182 and need no new work. It is never negative, because every KL is. And it is zero if and only if p(x, y) = p(x)p(y) everywhere, because that is the equality case of Gibbs' inequality. That second point is the sentence to carry out of this page: zero mutual information is exactly independence, with no gap between the two statements.

2. Three formulas, one number

The divergence form says what the quantity is. Two other forms say what it is for, and all three are the same number.

I(X ; Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) = H(X) − H(X | Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X, Y) Read as: mutual information is how much the uncertainty about Y falls once you are told X; equally, how much the uncertainty about X falls once you are told Y; equally, the two separate uncertainties added together minus the uncertainty of the pair. H(Y | X) is the conditional entropy, the entropy of Y averaged over the branches of X.

Here is why they agree. Split the logarithm in the divergence form and marginalise.

  1. Expand the ratio. log2( p(x,y) / (p(x)p(y)) ) = log2 p(x,y) − log2 p(x) − log2 p(y).
  2. Take the first piece. ∑ p(x,y) log2 p(x,y) is −H(X, Y).
  3. Take the second. log2 p(x) does not depend on y, so summing p(x,y) over y gives p(x), and the double sum collapses to ∑ p(x) log2 p(x) = −H(X). This is the step that does the real work: three apparently different quantities become one number because a term that ignores an index lets you sum that index away.
  4. The third is the same with the roles swapped, giving −H(Y).
  5. Collect. I = −H(X,Y) + H(X) + H(Y), which is the third form.
  6. Substitute the chain rule. H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X), which lesson 180 met as Shannon's property 5. Then H(X) + H(Y) − H(X) − H(Y|X) = H(Y) − H(Y|X), the first form.

Shannon writes these three identities together on page 21 of the 1948 paper, for the quantity he calls the rate of transmission over a noisy channel. They are his, not a modern repackaging, and the noisy-channel example on lesson 180 was computing one of them without naming it.

The reframing The second form has a consequence worth stating on its own: since I is never negative, H(Y | X) is never larger than H(Y). Conditioning never increases entropy on average. Shannon puts it as "the uncertainty of y is never increased by knowledge of x". Being told something cannot, on average, leave you more confused, though it certainly can on any single occasion.

3. What correlation cannot see

Correlation measures how well a straight line explains a scatter plot. Mutual information asks whether knowing one column changes what you expect of the other in any way at all. The gap between those questions is not a technicality.

Take X uniform on −2, −1, 1, 2 and let Y be X squared. Then Y is 1 or 4 equally often, so H(Y) = 1 bit, and Y is a function of X, so H(Y | X) = 0. By the second form, I = 1 bit exactly. The correlation is zero in the population, not approximately: the symmetry of X about the origin makes it so. One column determines the other completely and the standard diagnostic reports nothing.

4. Pointwise mutual information

Mutual information is an average over all pairs. Drop the average and you get a per-pair quantity, which is what lesson 186 needs.

PMI(x, y) = log2 ( p(x, y) / (p(x) p(y)) ) Read as: the pointwise mutual information of one specific pair is the logarithm of how much more often that pair occurs than it would if the two were unrelated. Positive means the pair co-occurs more than chance, negative means less, and zero means exactly as often as chance.

PMI can be negative; mutual information cannot. That is not a contradiction. Mutual information is the expectation of PMI under the joint distribution, and an average of signed numbers weighted by how often each occurs can be non-negative even when individual terms are not. Gibbs' inequality is precisely the guarantee that it always is.

5. Worked: a two-by-two table

  1. Take a hundred rows. A binary feature F and a binary label Y: 40 rows at (0,0), 10 at (0,1), 20 at (1,0), 30 at (1,1).
  2. Read the margins. F is 0 half the time and 1 half the time. Y is 0 for 60 rows and 1 for 40.
  3. Entropy of the label. H(Y) = −0.6 log2 0.6 − 0.4 log2 0.4 = 0.9710 bits.
  4. Entropy inside each branch. When F = 0 the split is 40 to 10, so H = H(0.2) = 0.7219. When F = 1 it is 20 to 30, so H = H(0.6) = 0.9710.
  5. Average them by branch size. H(Y | F) = 0.5(0.7219) + 0.5(0.9710) = 0.8464 bits.
  6. Subtract. I(F ; Y) = 0.9710 − 0.8464 = 0.1245 bits. Knowing the feature removes 12.8 per cent of the label uncertainty.
  7. Sanity check. Mutual information cannot exceed either marginal entropy, because you cannot learn more about Y than Y contains. Here the cap is min(1.0000, 0.9710) and 0.1245 is far under it. A result above either would mean a cell probability is wrong.
  8. What changes if you square the feature first, or apply any other one-to-one relabelling? Nothing at all. Mutual information depends only on which rows share a value, not on what the values are, so it is invariant to any invertible transform of either column. Correlation is not: it changes the moment you bend the axis.

One PMI cell from the same table, for lesson 186: the pair (F = 1, Y = 1) occurs 30 times in 100, against 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 expected under independence. So PMI = log2(0.30 / 0.20) = log2 1.5 = +0.585 bits, a pair that co-occurs half again as often as chance.

6. The estimate is biased, and badly

Everything above is about distributions. In practice you have counts, and plugging counts into the formula does not give you the population value.

for cardinality in (2, 4, 16, 64, 256, 2048, 12000):
    fake = rng.integers(0, cardinality, size=n)
    table = joint_table(pd.Series(fake), pd.Series(label))
    print(cardinality, mi_as_kl(table))

On real columns of the same table the honest answers are small: plan scores 0.0568 bits, support_tier 0.0055, and theme, which was generated independently of the label, scores 0.000011 rather than exactly zero. A measured mutual information of zero never happens, and knowing roughly how big the noise floor is for your number of cells is the difference between a feature-selection step that works and one that ranks identifiers at the top. Lesson 189 shows that failure happening to a decision tree.

The problem is worse than a finite-sample nuisance. McAllester and Stratos prove that any distribution-free high-confidence lower bound on mutual information computed from N samples is at most 2 ln N + 5, whatever estimator produced it. With N = 128 that ceiling is about 14.7 nats no matter how much information is really there. Neural estimators do not escape it, because the result is about the sample size and not about the method.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You measure a Pearson correlation of exactly zero between two columns. Which of these follows?
Correlation measures linear association and nothing else. The first option is the misconception stated plainly. The second and third are the same misconception dressed in information-theory notation, and they are worth recognising in that costume, because they sound more rigorous and are equally wrong. Y equal to X squared has zero correlation and a full bit of mutual information.
If X determines Y exactly, what does I(X ; Y) come out to?
Use the form I = H(Y) − H(Y | X). A deterministic map makes H(Y | X) zero, so the whole of H(Y) remains. The first option fixes on the one bit from this page's example, which was H(Y) for that particular Y and is not general. The third confuses which variable is being explained: I is symmetric, but H(X) can exceed H(Y) when several values of X map to the same Y. The fourth inverts the meaning entirely.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A hundred rows. Feature F is 1 for fifty of them. Label Y is 1 for forty. Of the fifty rows with F = 1, thirty have Y = 1.

Find the two-by-two table, then H(Y), H(Y | F) and I(F ; Y) in bits. Then a second feature G is a random hundred-value identifier: say what I(G ; Y) computes to on this sample, what it is in the population, and why they differ.

Hint

For the last part, work out how many rows share any given value of G before you compute anything.

Show the worked solution
  1. Fill the table. (1,1) = 30, so (1,0) = 20. Y = 1 for forty rows in total, so (0,1) = 10 and (0,0) = 40.
  2. Label entropy. H(Y) = −0.6 log2 0.6 − 0.4 log2 0.4 = 0.9710 bits.
  3. Inside each branch. F = 0 splits 40 to 10, giving H(0.2) = 0.7219. F = 1 splits 20 to 30, giving H(0.6) = 0.9710.
  4. Average. H(Y | F) = 0.5(0.7219) + 0.5(0.9710) = 0.8464 bits.
  5. Subtract. I(F ; Y) = 0.1245 bits.
  6. Now the identifier. Each value of G appears exactly once, so knowing G identifies the row and therefore Y. H(Y | G) = 0 and I(G ; Y) = H(Y) = 0.9710 bits, the largest value any feature could score.
  7. The population value. Exactly zero. G was generated at random and has no relationship to Y at all.
  8. Why they differ. The plug-in estimator has no way to tell "this value predicts Y" from "this value appears once". With one row per cell every cell is pure by construction, so the estimate saturates. It is the same failure the chart in section 6 measures, and lesson 189 shows a decision tree walking straight into it.

Sanity check. I(F ; Y) must be at most min(H(F), H(Y)) = min(1.0000, 0.9710). 0.1245 clears that easily. If your answer came out above 0.9710 you have either mis-added a cell or subtracted in the wrong order, since H(Y | F) can never exceed H(Y).

Your teacher. If the difference between correlation and mutual information still feels abstract, ask an agent to generate a thousand points on a circle, compute the Pearson correlation between the two coordinates, and then bin both coordinates into ten buckets and compute the mutual information. The correlation is near zero and the mutual information is large, for the same reason as the parabola in section 3.

Primary source to go deeper

McAllester and Stratos, Formal Limitations on the Measurement of Mutual Information. Eleven pages. It is the paper that stops you trusting a large estimate, and it also identifies an error in a well-known convergence claim, which is worth seeing done carefully.
