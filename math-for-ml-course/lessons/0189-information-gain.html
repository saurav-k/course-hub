Module 10 · Information, similarity, and dimension · Lesson 189

A decision tree is a greedy mutual-information maximiser

working 11 min Quinlan, Induction of Decision Trees (Machine Learning 1, 1986), sections 4 and 7.

The one-minute version

  • Entropy before the split, weighted entropy after, and the difference is the gain.
  • That gain is the mutual information between the attribute and the label. Quinlan says so in a footnote and the code confirms it to twelve decimal places.
  • Gain rises whenever an attribute is split more finely. That is a theorem, so it is a bias and not a bug.
  • An account number scores ten times the best honest column on real data, while knowing nothing.
  • Gain ratio narrows that from ten times to 1.13, and still picks the identifier. It is a mitigation, not a cure.

1. Twenty questions

A decision tree asks a sequence of questions and narrows down the answer. At the root it may ask any question it likes about the features. The good question is the one that, on average, leaves you least uncertain about the label afterwards.

"Uncertain about the label" is entropy, which lesson 180 built. "On average, afterwards" means: split the rows into branches, measure the entropy inside each branch, and average those weighted by how many rows land in each.

E(A) = ∑v ( nv / n ) · I(branch v)     gain(A) = I(before) − E(A) Read as: E(A) is the expected label entropy after splitting on attribute A: for each value v of that attribute, take the fraction of rows falling into that branch, nv over n, and multiply it by the label entropy inside that branch. The gain is how much the entropy fell. Both are in bits.

2. It is mutual information, and Quinlan knew

Look again at E(A). It is the label entropy inside each branch, averaged by branch size. The branch sizes are the empirical probabilities of each attribute value. So E(A) is precisely the conditional entropy H(Y | A) from lesson 183.

  1. Rewrite the weights. nv / n is the empirical P(A = v). This is the step that does the real work: once you see that, nothing about E(A) is tree-specific.
  2. Recognise the sum. ∑v P(A = v) H(Y | A = v) is the definition of H(Y | A).
  3. Substitute into the gain. gain(A) = H(Y) − H(Y | A).
  4. Name it. Lesson 183 proved that expression equals I(A ; Y).
The reframing Quinlan's own footnote 3, from 1986: "Since I(p,n) is constant for all attributes, maximizing the gain is equivalent to minimizing E(A), which is the mutual information of the attribute A and the class." You have now met this number three times: as a divergence in lesson 183, as a drop in uncertainty on the same page, and as the splitting rule of the most-deployed model family in the field.

3. Worked: fourteen Saturday mornings

Quinlan's table 1. Fourteen days described by outlook, temperature, humidity and windiness, nine of which were suitable for play and five of which were not.

  1. Entropy before any split. −(9/14) log2(9/14) − (5/14) log2(5/14) = 0.9403, which the paper prints as 0.940 bits.
  2. Split on outlook. Sunny holds 2 and 3, so I = 0.971. Overcast holds 4 and 0, so I = 0. Rain holds 3 and 2, so I = 0.971.
  3. Weight and add. (5/14)(0.971) + (4/14)(0) + (5/14)(0.971) = 0.6935, printed as 0.694.
  4. The gain. 0.9403 − 0.6935 = 0.2467 bits.
  5. The other three, quoted. humidity 0.151, windy 0.048, temperature 0.029. ID3 roots the tree on outlook.
  6. Sanity check. Every gain must be non-negative, because conditioning cannot increase entropy on average, and none may exceed 0.9403, because a split cannot remove more uncertainty than existed. All four clear both bounds.
  7. What changes if the overcast branch were 2 and 2 instead of 4 and 0? Its entropy becomes 1 bit rather than 0, E(outlook) rises to 0.9792, and the gain collapses to 0.0286, below humidity's. One impure branch out of three is enough to lose the root.
  8. Read it back. 0.2467 bits out of 0.9403 available means outlook removes about a quarter of what there was to know. That is a large effect for a single column, and it is why the tree is worth building at all.
Two places where this page and the paper disagree in the third decimal, and both are worth seeing once. The gain computes to 0.2467 where the paper prints 0.246, because the paper subtracts its own rounded intermediates: 0.940 − 0.694 = 0.246. And the split information in section 4 computes to 1.5774 where the paper prints 1.578, which rounds the wrong way. Neither changes any decision the algorithm makes. A number you derived and a number you quoted are different objects, and this course marks them differently for exactly this reason.

4. The bias, and it is a theorem

Split one value of an attribute into two, and the gain cannot go down. Not usually: ever.

The claim. Let A′ be formed from A by splitting one of its values into two. Then gain(A′) ≥ gain(A), with equality only when the class proportions are identical in both halves.

  1. Reduce it. Both gains subtract from the same H(Y), so the claim is H(Y|A′) ≤ H(Y|A).
  2. Isolate the difference. Every branch of A is also a branch of A′ except the value v that was split, so the two conditional entropies differ in exactly one term.
  3. Recognise a mixture. The distribution of Y given A = v is the mixture of the two child distributions, weighted by P(v1)/P(v) and P(v2)/P(v).
  4. Apply concavity. Entropy is concave, so the entropy of a mixture is at least the mixture of the entropies. This is the step that does the real work, and in words it says: splitting a group can never make you more uncertain on average.
  5. Multiply through by P(v) and the right-hand side is exactly the contribution A′ makes. So H(Y|A′) ≤ H(Y|A), and the gain went up.

Now take it to the limit. An attribute whose value is unique to each row puts one row in every branch, every branch is pure, H(Y|A) = 0, and the gain equals H(Y): the largest value any attribute can possibly attain. That attribute is an account number. It knows nothing.

5. Does the standard fix work?

Quinlan's own answer is split information: the entropy of the split itself, ignoring the label, which measures how finely the attribute chops the data. Divide the gain by it and you have the gain ratio.

IV(A) = −∑v (nv/n) log2 (nv/n)     gain ratio = gain(A) / IV(A) Read as: IV(A) is the entropy of the attribute's own value distribution, with no reference to the label at all. It is large for an attribute with many roughly equal-sized branches. Dividing by it penalises exactly the fineness that section 4 showed inflates the gain.

On Quinlan's fourteen rows it works nicely: IV(outlook) = 1.5774 and IV(humidity) = 1, so the gain ratios are 0.1564 and 0.1518. Outlook still wins, and its margin has collapsed from 63 per cent to 3 per cent, which is the criterion doing its job.

On twelve thousand real rows it does not finish the job.

The honest conclusion, and it is not the one usually taught. Gain ratio is a mitigation. The cure is not to hand a tree a column with one value per row, and no splitting criterion will rescue you from doing so.

6. And what about Gini?

The default splitting criterion in scikit-learn is not entropy. It is Gini impurity, 1 − ∑ p2, which needs no logarithm and is therefore cheaper at every candidate split, of which a tree evaluates a great many.

On the three honest columns of the committed table the two criteria rank the columns identically and report different units: plan scores 0.056819 by entropy and 0.030575 by Gini. That is three columns, not a literature review, and this page will not tell you the two criteria rarely disagree in general, because it has not measured that. What it can say is that they measure the same shape of thing and disagree about how to price it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You add a customer-ID column to a training set and information gain picks it as the root. Why?
Section 4 proves it: refining a split can never raise the conditional entropy, so gain never falls, and a unique-per-row column drives it to the maximum. The first option describes a real and different problem, target leakage, which would also need the IDs to correlate with the label and here they do not. The third names the symptom rather than the cause: the tree overfits because the criterion chose that column. The fourth blames the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is exactly right.
On Quinlan's table, gain(outlook) is 0.246 bits and gain(temperature) is 0.029. What does that mean?
Gain is measured in bits of entropy removed, averaged over branches, and that is all it is. The first converts a difference of bits into a ratio of accuracies, which no step in the derivation licenses. The second reads bits as a percentage; the share removed here is 0.246 of 0.940, which is 26 per cent, not 24.6. The third overreaches from a small number: a column with low gain at the root can still be the best split three levels down, inside a branch where the label balance has changed.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Twelve rows, six of each class. Attribute A is binary and splits them into (4 of class P, 2 of class N) and (2 P, 4 N). Attribute B has six values and splits them into six pairs, each holding one P and one N.

Find I(6,6), gain(A) and gain(B); then IV(A), IV(B) and both gain ratios. Then say which attribute the tree should use, and why raw gain gets this case wrong in the opposite direction to the usual warning.

Hint

Work out the entropy inside one of B's six branches before computing anything else. It settles the whole second half of the question.

Show the worked solution
  1. Before any split. Six and six is balanced, so I(6,6) = 1 bit.
  2. Attribute A's branches. Each holds four of one class and two of the other, so each has entropy −(4/6)log2(4/6) − (2/6)log2(2/6) = 0.9183.
  3. Average them. Both branches hold six rows, so E(A) = 0.9183 and gain(A) = 0.0817 bits.
  4. Attribute B's branches. Every one holds one P and one N, so every one has entropy exactly 1.
  5. Average. E(B) = 1, so gain(B) = 0 bits. Six branches and it learned nothing.
  6. Split information. IV(A) = 1 bit, two equal branches. IV(B) = log2 6 = 2.5850 bits, six equal branches.
  7. Gain ratios. A: 0.0817 / 1 = 0.0817. B: 0 / 2.5850 = 0.
  8. Use A, and note the shape of this case. The many-valued bias is a tendency, not a law. A many-valued attribute that genuinely carries no signal scores zero on both criteria, because its branches are exactly as impure as the parent. Anyone who has memorised "gain always prefers many values" predicts B wins here, and B scores zero.

Sanity check. gain(B) must be exactly 0, not merely small. If you got a positive number, check that each of B's six branches really is one P and one N: a single branch of two P and no N would make it positive, and would be a different problem with a different answer.

Your teacher. If section 4's proof felt abstract, ask an agent to take any binary attribute in your own data, split one of its two values at random into two sub-values, and recompute the gain. It will go up, every time, however meaningless the split you invented. That is the theorem happening in front of you.
