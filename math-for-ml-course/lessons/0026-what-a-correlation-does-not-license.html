Anscombe, and what a correlation does not license

foundation 10 min The warning label for the previous page, and it is not optional reading.

The one-minute version

  • A coefficient is a summary of a picture you have not looked at. Four datasets agree on twelve statistics and look nothing alike.
  • Always plot before trusting a correlation. That is the easy habit. The rest of this page is the harder failure.
  • An association can be produced by a third column, by the arrow pointing the other way, or by which rows you kept. The coefficient cannot tell these apart.
  • Measured here: a treatment that wins inside every group and loses overall. No arithmetic error is involved.

1. Four datasets, twelve statistics, one picture each

You met these on Lesson 24. Here they are again, deliberately, because the point is different this time: there it was about shape, here it is about what a coefficient compressed away.

The habit Plot before you trust. It costs one line of code and it is the only defence against the entire class of failure this figure represents.

2. The harder failure: three ways an association appears

Plotting catches a wrong shape. It does not catch a real, linear, correctly-measured association that means something other than what you think. There are three standard ways that happens.

Confounding. A third column drives both. Ice cream sales and drownings rise together and neither causes the other; the season causes both. In a dataset the third column is often one you did not record.

Reverse causation. The arrow points the other way. Note that the coefficient is symmetric: r(x, y) equals r(y, x) exactly, by construction, because the formula multiplies the two deviations and multiplication does not care about order. A symmetric number cannot possibly tell you about an asymmetric relationship.

Selection. The association exists only inside the rows you kept. This is the one that bites in machine learning, because a training set is always a selected set: it is the rows that were logged, that passed validation, that came from customers who stayed.

3. Measured: an association that survives, and one that reverses

First the honest case. On sessions.csv, session_seconds against spend correlates at 0.48681 overall. Split by device:

Every group sits close to the pooled value, so device is not confounding this association. That is worth seeing, because the interesting case is not the only case, and a check that always fires is not a check.

Which table should you act on? That is not a question the numbers can answer. It depends on why the arms ended up with different device mixes. If the assignment was randomised and the mix differed by chance, the grouped tables are the honest ones. If the treatment itself changed who showed up, the pooled table may be measuring a real effect of deploying it. The arithmetic is identical in both cases and the correct action is opposite. Module 09 is where randomisation earns its keep.

4. The one thing a correlation does license

Having said what it cannot do, here is what it can. A correlation licenses a prediction, within the range of the data and for the population that was sampled. If session_seconds and spend correlate at 0.487 on your logged sessions, then for a future logged session in the same range, knowing one does reduce your uncertainty about the other.

That is worth something and it is worth less than people assume. It says nothing about what happens if you intervene to make sessions longer, and it stops applying the moment the population changes.

5. Worked: finding the confounder

  1. Start with the pooled number. Treatment converts at 0.06250, control at 0.12325. Treatment looks half as good.
  2. List the columns you could group by. Here there is one: device, with two values.
  3. Compute the rate inside mobile. Control 128/3,000 = 0.04267. Treatment 490/10,400 = 0.04712. Treatment is ahead by 0.00445.
  4. Compute the rate inside desktop. Control 1,351/9,000 = 0.15011. Treatment 260/1,600 = 0.16250. Treatment is ahead by 0.01239.
  5. Notice the contradiction. Treatment wins in both groups and loses pooled. Check the arithmetic; it is correct.
  6. Find the mechanism in the group sizes. Control is 75 per cent desktop (9,000 of 12,000); treatment is 13 per cent desktop (1,600 of 12,000).
  7. Read why that matters. Desktop converts at about 15 per cent and mobile at about 4.5 per cent. Control's pooled rate is mostly a desktop rate; treatment's is mostly a mobile rate.
  8. Name it. device is the confounder, and it is confounding because it is associated with the arm and with the outcome.

Sanity check. A confounder has to be linked to both. If a grouping column is unrelated to the outcome, splitting by it changes nothing; if it is unrelated to the arm, the mixes match and pooling is safe. Device fails both tests here, which is exactly why it bites.

What changes if the mixes had matched? Everything. Give both arms 75 per cent desktop and the pooled rates become weighted averages with the same weights, so the pooled comparison agrees with both grouped ones. That is what randomised assignment buys, and it is why the design of the experiment is doing more work than the analysis.

What it means. The sessions.csv check in section 3 came back clean and this one did not, on the same arithmetic. Running the check is the habit; expecting it to fire is not.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two columns in your data correlate at 0.9. Which claim is licensed?
The modest claim is the true one: an association, in this sample, in this range. The first is the trap the page is built around, and the coefficient is symmetric so it cannot even say which direction an arrow would point. The third is the same error phrased as an intervention, which is strictly stronger and needs an experiment. The fourth over-corrects: confounding is one explanation among several and the number does not establish it either.
Which failure does a machine learning training set always risk?
Every training set is the rows that were logged, passed validation and survived cleaning, so selection is present by construction rather than by bad luck. The first and second are real risks that depend on the specific problem, and correlated features are ordinary rather than a defect. The fourth is a genuine issue and a different one, and it is not what makes the kept rows unrepresentative of the rows you will predict on.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A hiring dataset shows that candidates who took an optional coding test are hired at a far higher rate than those who did not. Splitting by the recruiter who screened them:

recruiter A: 62% against 60%    recruiter B: 31% against 29%    pooled: 55% against 33% Read as: hire rates for candidates who took the test against those who did not, computed inside each recruiter's pool and then over everyone. The two recruiters screen different numbers of candidates.

Name the phenomenon, say which column is responsible, and say what you would need to know before recommending that the test be made compulsory.

Hint

Compare the size of the gap inside each group with the size of the gap pooled. If the pooled gap is far larger than any group's gap, the grouping column is doing work that the pooled number is attributing to the test.

Hint for the last part

Ask what decides which recruiter a candidate reaches, and whether that could also be related to how likely they were to be hired anyway.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the group gaps. Recruiter A: 62 − 60 = 2 points. Recruiter B: 31 − 29 = 2 points. Both small and both the same.
  2. Read the pooled gap. 55 − 33 = 22 points, eleven times larger than either group's.
  3. Notice the base rates differ. Recruiter A hires around 60 per cent and recruiter B around 30 per cent. That is a bigger difference than the test makes anywhere.
  4. Infer the mix. For the pooled gap to be so much larger, test-takers must be concentrated with recruiter A and non-takers with recruiter B.
  5. Name it. Confounding, and recruiter is the confounding column. It is associated with taking the test and with being hired, which is what a confounder is.
  6. Give the honest estimate of the test's effect. About 2 percentage points, which is what both groups agree on, not 22.
  7. Say what you would need before recommending it. Why test-takers reach recruiter A. If candidates are routed by seniority or role and those also drive hiring, the 2 points may still be confounded by something you have not split on. If assignment to recruiters were random, the 2 points would be credible.

Sanity check. If the association had survived inside every subgroup at roughly its original strength, that grouping column would not be the confounder and you would be looking at the wrong one. Here it collapsed from 22 points to 2, which is the signature. Note also that the effect did not vanish or reverse, so the correct conclusion is "much smaller than it looked", not "nothing there".

Your teacher. If you want the reversal in your hands rather than on a page, ask an agent: "build me a two-by-two table where treatment beats control in both groups and loses overall, and show the arithmetic". Constructing one yourself is the fastest way to stop finding it surprising. And if a stakeholder ever shows you a single pooled rate comparison, the useful question is not about significance, it is "what happens if we split this by anything".

Primary source to go deeper

Anscombe, "Graphs in Statistical Analysis", The American Statistician 27(1), 1973, 17-21, for the quartet and the argument that computation is not a substitute for looking. For the reversal, Simpson, "The Interpretation of Interaction in Contingency Tables", JRSS-B 13(2), 1951. Causal inference as a field is deliberately out of scope here; it needs a course of its own.
