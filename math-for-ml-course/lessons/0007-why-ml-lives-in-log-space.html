Log space is where the arithmetic still works

foundation 12 min Two independent reasons, and merging them is why people cannot say what breaks.

The one-minute version

  • A product of a few hundred probabilities underflows to exactly zero in float64, and a classifier whose classes both score zero cannot decide anything.
  • Taking logarithms turns that product into a sum, and a sum of a few hundred numbers near −9 is unremarkable.
  • The swap is legal because ln is strictly increasing, which is lesson 04's theorem. It is necessary because of floating point. Those are different reasons from different places.
  • The dangerous failure is not the one that returns zero for both classes. It is the one that returns zero for exactly one, because that looks like a confident answer.

1. The failure, before the fix

A naive Bayes classifier scores a support ticket by multiplying together one probability per token. On a short ticket that is fine. Here is what happens on a long one.

P(class) × Πi=1n P(tokeni | class) Read as: the prior probability of the class, multiplied by the probability of each of the n tokens under that class. i indexes the tokens in this one ticket, and every factor is a number between 0 and 1.

Every factor is below 1, so the running product only ever shrinks. With a typical token probability around 1e-4, two hundred tokens gives 10−800. The smallest positive number float64 can represent is about 5e-324. So the answer is not small. It is 0.0.

The exact thresholds are worth carrying, because they are the numbers that decide when this bites. In float64, ex overflows to infinity above x = 709.78 and underflows to exactly zero below about x = −746. Everything on this page lives between those two walls.

2. The fix is one rule from lesson 06

ln(Πi pi) = Σi ln(pi) Read as: the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms. This is the product rule from lesson 06, applied n − 1 times in a row. pi is the i-th probability and the index runs over every token in the ticket.

Two hundred tokens at 1e-4 becomes 200 × ln(1e-4) = −1842.07. That is an ordinary double-precision number with room to spare in both directions. Nothing was approximated and nothing was thrown away.

3. Why it is allowed, which is a different question

Everything so far says log space is useful. It does not say the answer is the same, and that has to be argued separately.

The corollary For a likelihood L(θ) > 0,  argmaxθ L(θ) = argmaxθ ln L(θ). The parameters that maximise the likelihood are the parameters that maximise its logarithm.

Proof. By lesson 04, it is enough to show ln is strictly increasing on the positive reals. Take 0 < a < b. Then ln b − ln a = ln(b/a) by lesson 06's quotient rule, and b/a > 1, so ln(b/a) > 0 and ln a < ln b. So ln satisfies the hypothesis of the monotone-transform theorem with g = ln, and the conclusion is exactly the corollary.

The step that does the work It is not in this proof. It is lesson 04's backwards direction, which is the only place strictness was needed. All this page contributes is checking the hypothesis. That is worth noticing in itself: a two-line corollary standing on a page-old theorem is what mathematics being assembled actually looks like.

And what the corollary does not say. It preserves the location of the maximum. It says nothing about the value, so a likelihood ratio is not a log-likelihood ratio. And it says nothing about whether either quantity can be computed at all, which is the entirely separate problem section 1 opened with.

Test yourself on the merge. If you swapped in a transform that was monotone but the numbers still underflowed, you would be legal and stuck. If you rescaled by something that fixed the underflow but was not monotone - clipping, say - you would be computing happily and answering a different question. You need both, and they are not the same claim.

4. The failure that does not look like one

Underflow sounds like an error you would notice. Run the arithmetic over a real held-out split and the picture is worse than that.

The program fits the classifier on 3,545 training tickets and scores 721 held-out ones both ways. Of those:

  • 8 tickets, 1.11%, have both class scores underflow to zero. The classifier cannot decide, and a tie at least looks like a problem.
  • 26 tickets, 3.61%, have exactly one underflow. These get an answer, the answer looks confident, and it was chosen by floating point rather than by evidence.
The model is the same model in both columns. Only the arithmetic differs, and it costs 1.11 percentage points of accuracy: 0.9889 naively against 1.0000 in log space. Nothing raised an exception, nothing appeared in a log, and a smaller test set would have hidden it entirely.

5. The library does exactly this

This is not a trick a course invented. Open scikit-learn and the method is called _joint_log_likelihood, documented as returning "log P(c) + log P(x | c)". Its Gaussian implementation is worth reading once as a sentence: every + in it is a × that a logarithm turned into a +.

code/0007-why-ml-lives-in-log-space.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download · data: tickets.csv
naive = {c: priors[c] * float(np.prod(probabilities[c][ids])) for c in CLASSES}
logs  = {c: float(np.log(priors[c]) + np.log(probabilities[c][ids]).sum()) for c in CLASSES}

zeros = sum(1 for c in CLASSES if naive[c] == 0.0)
if   zeros == 2: both_zero += 1     # undecidable, and it looks undecidable
elif zeros == 1: one_zero  += 1     # decided by an artefact, and it looks confident

assert np.isfinite(logs[CLASSES[0]]) and np.isfinite(logs[CLASSES[1]])

It closes with the check that matters most: on the 381 tickets where the naive product still worked, the two paths pick the same class 381 times out of 381. That is section 3's corollary holding in practice rather than on paper.

One place a naive logarithm still fails, and it is worth knowing before M10. Computing log(softmax(x)) by running softmax first and taking the log of the result can produce -inf, because a tiny probability underflows before the log ever sees it. That is why PyTorch ships a separate LogSoftmax, and why CrossEntropyLoss takes raw logits and is documented as "equivalent to applying LogSoftmax on an input, followed by NLLLoss". Calling softmax yourself first is a bug, not an optimisation.

6. Worked, on numbers you can still see

Three tokens, so that the product and the log agree in front of you before the product stops existing.

  1. Take the priors. P(urgent) = 0.3 and P(normal) = 0.7, so ln 0.3 = −1.204 and ln 0.7 = −0.357.
  2. Add the urgent token logs. ln 0.01 = −4.605, ln 0.02 = −3.912, ln 0.005 = −5.298. Total with the prior: −15.019.
  3. Add the normal token logs. −6.908, −5.521, −6.215, plus the prior: −19.001.
  4. Compare. Urgent wins by 3.981 on the log scale, which is a factor of e3.981 = 53.6 in probability.
  5. Check against the product. The naive numbers are 3.0e-7 and 5.6e-9, whose ratio is also 53.6. Same answer, and here both ways can still be computed.
Sanity check. Every log score must be negative, since every probability is below 1, and −15 is about the right size for four factors averaging e−4. What changes if the ticket is 200 tokens rather than 3: the product becomes 0.0 for both classes and the ratio becomes nan, while the log scores stay finite and keep the same winner.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is maximising the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood legal?
Legality is a statement about orderings and it comes from lesson 04. The first and third options are both true, and both are reasons the swap is useful rather than reasons it is allowed - keeping those apart is what this page is for. The fourth is false: the values change enormously, and only the location of the maximum does not.
You call softmax, then take log of the result. What can go wrong?
A small probability can underflow to zero inside softmax, and the log of zero is minus infinity - which is why PyTorch ships a separate LogSoftmax. The first is false, log and softmax are not inverses. The second says "always", and a stabilised softmax overflows never. The third is false: softmax sums to one by construction.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Priors P(urgent) = 0.3 and P(normal) = 0.7. A three-token ticket, with P(token | urgent) = 0.01, 0.02, 0.005 and P(token | normal) = 0.001, 0.004, 0.002.

(a) Compute the joint log likelihood of each class.
(b) Which wins, and by how much on the log scale?
(c) Turn the two log scores into posterior probabilities.
(d) The naive products here are 3.0e-7 and 5.6e-9, both perfectly representable. So why bother with logs at all?

Hint

For (c), do not exponentiate the two scores and divide - that throws away the whole point. Subtract the larger of the two log scores from both first, then exponentiate, then normalise.

Show the worked solution
  1. Score urgent. ln 0.3 + ln 0.01 + ln 0.02 + ln 0.005 = −1.204 − 4.605 − 3.912 − 5.298 = −15.019.
  2. Score normal. ln 0.7 + ln 0.001 + ln 0.004 + ln 0.002 = −0.357 − 6.908 − 5.521 − 6.215 = −19.001.
  3. Compare for (b). Urgent is larger, by 19.001 − 15.019 = 3.981 on the log scale.
  4. Shift before exponentiating. Subtract the larger score, −15.019, from both: urgent becomes 0 and normal becomes −3.981. This is softmax's shift invariance from lesson 05, used on two numbers.
  5. Exponentiate and total. e0 = 1 and e−3.981 = 0.01867, so the total is 1.01867.
  6. Normalise. 1 / 1.01867 = 0.982 for urgent and 0.018 for normal.
  7. Answer (d) honestly. Three tokens is not a ticket. The same arithmetic on a real 200-token ticket gives 0.0 for both classes, and this small case exists precisely so you can check the log answer against a product you can still see.

Sanity check. The two posteriors must sum to 1, and the winner on the log scale must still be the winner after exponentiating - if it flipped, you subtracted the smaller score instead of the larger. And a 53-fold advantage in probability should land near 0.98, not near 0.53: those are different quantities.

Your teacher. The one thing to carry off this page is that "legal" and "necessary" are two arguments, not one. If it still feels like a single idea, ask an agent to "give me a transform that is strictly increasing but does not fix the underflow, and one that fixes the underflow but is not monotone, and say what goes wrong with each" - the two failure modes are quite different and meeting both is what separates the arguments.
