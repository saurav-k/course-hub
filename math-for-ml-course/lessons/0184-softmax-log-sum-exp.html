The exponential you write is never the one you compute

working 12 min Blanchard, D. J. Higham and N. J. Higham, Accurately computing the log-sum-exp and softmax functions (IMA J. Numer. Anal., 2021). · the paper

The one-minute version

  • Softmax exponentiates and normalises. It is a soft argmax, not a soft max, and the difference explains everything else about it.
  • It sees only the differences between its inputs. Add any constant to all of them and nothing changes.
  • Written as it stands it overflows: in float16 a logit above 11.0 is already infinity, and 28.7 per cent of a real batch is above it.
  • Subtract the largest logit first. The same identity that makes it safe also proves the gradient of log-sum-exp is the softmax.
  • Which hands you p − y, the most reused derivative in machine learning, in one line.

1. Two steps, no magic

You have k real numbers, called logits, and you need a probability vector: everything positive, everything adding to one. Exponentiating makes them positive. Dividing by the total makes them sum to one. That is the whole construction.

softmax(z)i = ezi / ∑j ezj Read as: the i-th output is e raised to the i-th input, divided by the sum of e raised to every input. The index j in the denominator runs over all k classes, so every output depends on every input, not only on its own.

It is not the only function that could do this. Squaring and normalising would also work and is much cheaper. The exponential earns its place for a reason that only becomes visible in section 4: the logarithm in the loss undoes it exactly, and nothing else has that property.

2. It only sees differences

Add the same constant c to every logit. In the numerator that multiplies by ec. In the denominator every term also multiplies by ec, so it factors out of the sum. The two cancel.

softmax(z + c1) = softmax(z)   and   LSE(z + c1) = LSE(z) + c Read as: adding the same number c to every entry of the logit vector leaves the softmax output completely unchanged, and shifts the log-sum-exp by exactly c. The bold 1 is the vector of ones, so c1 is "add c to everything". A shift applied to only some entries is not free and does change the answer.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

The comfortable one: you may subtract anything you like, provided you subtract it everywhere. Section 3 uses that to make the computation safe.

The uncomfortable one: a single logit means nothing on its own. The vector has k entries and only k − 1 degrees of freedom, so a logit of 12 is not "confident" and a logit of −3 is not "doubtful". Only the gaps carry information. This is also why softmax saturates when the gaps grow large rather than when any one value does.

The reframing Deep Learning, section 6.2.2.3, on the name: "The function is more closely related to the arg max function than the max function... It would perhaps be better to call the softmax function 'softargmax,' but the current name is an entrenched convention." The soft version of max is softmax(z) · z, a scalar. Once you hold that, temperature stops being a knob to memorise: a smoothed argmax obviously becomes the hard argmax as the smoothing goes away.

3. Why it cannot be computed as written

Exponentials get large very quickly, and floating point has a ceiling. Blanchard, Higham and Higham tabulate where each format gives up.

What happens next is worth following step by step, because the failure is silent until the very end.

4. The shift, and what it hands you for free

Section 2 says you may subtract any constant from every logit. Choose the largest one.

LSE(z) = log ∑j ezj = a + log ∑j ezj − a,   a = maxj zj Read as: the log-sum-exp of a vector equals the largest entry plus the log-sum-exp of the vector with that largest entry subtracted from everything. After the subtraction the biggest exponent is exactly zero, so e to it is exactly 1: nothing can overflow, and at least one term in the sum is 1, so the sum cannot underflow to zero either.

Now the loss. Lesson 181 showed that with a one-hot label the cross-entropy is −log of the true class's probability. Write that out with the definition of softmax and the logarithm splits.

−log softmax(z)y = −zy + LSE(z) Read as: the loss for a single example is minus the logit of the true class, plus the log-sum-exp of the whole logit vector. No probability is ever formed, so nothing can underflow to zero and no logarithm is ever handed a zero. y is the index of the true class.

Now differentiate, and watch what happens.

  1. Differentiate the log-sum-exp. The outer function is a logarithm, so the chain rule gives one over the sum, times the derivative of the sum.
  2. Differentiate the sum. Only the j-th term contains zj, and its derivative is ezj.
  3. Put them together. ∂LSE/∂zj = ezj / ∑m ezm, which is softmax(z)j. This is the step that does the real work, and it does nothing clever: the denominator of the softmax is the very sum the logarithm was taken of, so the softmax falls out of "one over u, times du" and nothing else happens.
  4. Add the other term. −zy differentiates to −1 when j = y and 0 otherwise.
∂L / ∂zj = softmax(z)j − 1[j = y]    which is   p − y Read as: the derivative of the loss with respect to the j-th logit is the predicted probability of class j, minus 1 if j is the true class and minus 0 if it is not. Written as vectors that is the prediction minus the one-hot label. The bracket 1[j = y] is an indicator: it is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

Two checks make this trustworthy rather than memorable. The gradient sums to zero over j, because the probabilities sum to one and so does the one-hot label. It must: section 2 says the loss cannot see a constant added to the whole vector, so it can have no slope in that direction. And the program checks both derivatives against central differences, with a largest disagreement of 1.6 × 10−9 over twelve rows.

One rewrite that looks equivalent and is not. Computing softmax as exp(zj − LSE(z)), which removes the division, is used by SciPy 1.4.1 and by the code accompanying a well-known textbook. Blanchard, Higham and Higham conclude that this division-free variant "can suffer from loss of accuracy". The shifted forms are fine and they measure them to be so. That one specific rearrangement is not.

5. Worked: the same loss twice, then the cliff

  1. Take three logits. z = 2.0, 1.0, 0.1, and the true class is the first.
  2. The naive route. exp gives 7.3891, 2.7183, 1.1052; the sum is 11.2126; the probabilities are 0.6590, 0.2424, 0.0986; the loss is −ln 0.6590 = 0.4170 nats.
  3. The stable route, shift. a = 2.0, so the shifted logits are 0, −1.0, −1.9.
  4. Exponentiate and sum. 1 + 0.3679 + 0.1496 = 1.5175.
  5. Finish. LSE = 2.0 + ln 1.5175 = 2.4170, and the loss is −zy + LSE = −2.0 + 2.4170 = 0.4170 nats. The same number, and no probability was formed.
  6. Sanity check. LSE always lies between the largest logit and the largest logit plus ln k. Here that is 2.0 to 2.0 + ln 3 = 3.0986, and 2.4170 sits inside. If your LSE came out below the largest logit you subtracted the shift and forgot to add it back, which is the single most common bug in a hand-written implementation.
  7. What changes if the logits are 800, 801, 802? The naive route overflows even in float64 and returns not-a-number. The stable route gives LSE = 802.4170 and, for the third class, a loss of 0.4170 again. Identical, because only the gaps ever mattered.
  8. And the gradient, from the same numbers. p − y = 0.6590 − 1, 0.2424, 0.0986 = −0.3410, 0.2424, 0.0986, which sums to zero as it must. Reading it: push the true class's logit up, push the other two down, each in proportion to how much probability it wrongly held.

6. Temperature, and what it cannot do

Divide the logits by a positive number T before the softmax. Large T shrinks the gaps and flattens the distribution; small T stretches them and sharpens it. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean introduce it in exactly this form for distilling one model into another, and note that gradients scale as 1/T2, so a mixed objective has to multiply by T2 to keep its two halves in balance.

The argmax never moves. Dividing every logit by the same positive number preserves their order, so no temperature can change which class wins. Temperature changes how confident the model sounds and never what it decides, which is worth knowing before you reach for it to fix a calibration problem: it can only rescale a curve like the one on lesson 181, not bend it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Softmax is best described as a smooth version of which function?
Argmax returns which entry wins, as a one-hot vector, and softmax is a differentiable version of that. The first option is what the name suggests and it is wrong: the soft version of max is softmax(z) dotted with z, a scalar. The second is a true statement about the relationship between the two functions but it does not answer which function is being softened. The fourth describes what the code does and skips what the function means.
Why does every implementation subtract max(z) before exponentiating?
After the shift the biggest exponent is exactly 0, so its exponential is 1 and nothing can overflow, while at least one term equals 1 so the sum cannot underflow to zero. The second option describes something the division already guarantees with or without the shift. The third borrows an idea from feature preprocessing that does not apply, because section 2 shows the gradient is unchanged by any shift. The fourth is not true of any implementation.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A model produces logits 12.0, 9.0 and 3.0, and the computation is running in float16, where the exponential overflows above about 11.09. The true class is the first.

Find what the naive route returns and at which step it first fails; then the loss computed the stable way; then the gradient with respect to all three logits, checking that it sums to zero.

Hint

Work out e12 before you look at anything else, and compare it with float16's largest finite value of 65,504.

Show the worked solution
  1. The first exponential. e12 = 162,754, above float16's largest finite value of 65,504, so it becomes infinity at the very first step, before any summation.
  2. What follows. The denominator becomes infinity, the division gives not-a-number, and the loss is not-a-number. Nothing raised an error along the way.
  3. Now the shift. a = 12.0, so the shifted logits are 0, −3, −9.
  4. Exponentiate. 1, then e−3 = 0.049787, then e−9 = 0.00012341.
  5. Sum and take the log. 1.049910, and ln 1.049910 = 0.048703.
  6. The loss. LSE = 12.0 + 0.048703 = 12.048703, so the loss is −12.0 + 12.048703 = 0.048703 nats.
  7. The probabilities. Divide each shifted exponential by 1.049910: p = 0.952465, 0.047420, 0.000118.
  8. The gradient. p − y = −0.047535, 0.047420, 0.000118. The sum is 0.000003, zero to rounding.

Sanity check. The loss must be small and positive, because the model put 95 per cent on the right class. If it came out above ln 3 = 1.0986 the model would be doing worse than a uniform guess over three classes, which these logits cannot produce. And the gradient must sum to zero: if yours does not, you have almost certainly forgotten to subtract the 1 from the true class's entry.

Your teacher. If section 4 did not land, ask an agent to compute the loss for the logits in section 5 by finite differences: nudge one logit by 0.000001, recompute the loss, divide by the nudge, and compare against p minus y. Watching the two agree to nine decimal places is worth more than reading the derivation twice.
