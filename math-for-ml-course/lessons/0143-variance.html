Module 08 · Expectation, Limits and Simulation · Lesson 143

Every square root of n you have ever seen comes from one line

working 12 min Variance divides by n. Spread divides by the square root of n. That gap sets the price of certainty.

The one-minute version

  • Variance is the expected squared deviation from the mean, and squaring is what stops the positive and negative deviations cancelling.
  • The computational form Var(X) = E[X2] - (E[X])2 is one pass instead of two, and it is numerically dangerous.
  • Variances add across independent pieces. In general there is a covariance term, and forgetting it is how ensembles get oversold.
  • Var of a sample mean is σ2/n, so the standard error is σ/√n. Four times the data halves the error.

1. The mental model: why squared, and not just far

You want one number for "how spread out is this". The obvious try, the average deviation from the mean, is exactly zero for every distribution there is: the mean is the balance point, so the deviations cancel by construction.

So the signs have to go before averaging. Absolute values work and give the mean absolute deviation. Squaring also works, and squaring won for two reasons: it is differentiable everywhere, and squared deviations add across independent pieces while absolute ones do not.

Var(X) = E[(X - μ)2]     where μ = E[X] Read as: the variance of X is the expected value of the squared gap between X and its own mean. The Greek letter mu is the standard name for that mean. Because the gap is squared, variance is in squared units: square metres, or squared milliseconds.

Nobody has intuition for squared milliseconds, which is why the standard deviation, its square root, is what gets reported: back in the original units and directly comparable to the mean.

2. The computational form, proved

Theorem For any random variable X with finite second moment, Var(X) = E[X2] - (E[X])2.

Proof. Expand the square inside the expectation, then use linearity to break it apart.

E[(X - μ)2] = E[X2 - 2μX + μ2] Read as: multiply out the bracket exactly as you would in ordinary algebra. Nothing probabilistic has happened yet, this is just expanding a square.
= E[X2] - 2μE[X] + μ2 Read as: linearity lets the expectation pass through the sum term by term. The constant 2 mu comes out of the middle term, and the expectation of the constant mu squared is just mu squared.

The step that finishes it: E[X] is itself μ, so the middle term is 2μ2.

= E[X2] - 2μ2 + μ2 = E[X2] - μ2 Read as: two copies of mu squared are subtracted and one is added back, leaving one to subtract. Since mu is E[X], that final term is (E[X]) squared.

The point of it is one pass instead of two: accumulate a running sum and a running sum of squares in a single sweep, and never store the data.

That convenience has a sharp edge. The computational form subtracts two large nearly equal numbers, and floating point cannot do that without losing digits. On the serving log, shifting latency by 108 leaves the true variance unchanged at 26,718.980 and makes the one-pass formula return 26,718.000. Three digits gone from an algebraically exact identity. Production code centres first, or uses Welford's method.

3. How variances combine, and the term everyone drops

Two rules do most of the work. Shifting a variable leaves the spread alone; scaling it multiplies the spread by the same factor, so the variance changes by the square.

Var(aX + b) = a2 Var(X) Read as: multiplying a variable by a multiplies its variance by a squared, and adding a constant b does nothing at all. The b vanishes because it shifts every value and the mean by the same amount, so no deviation changes.

Adding two variables needs more care, because the general law has three terms and only the first two get remembered.

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y) Read as: the variance of a sum is the two separate variances plus twice a cross term called the covariance, which measures how the two variables move together. Covariance is the whole of the next lesson. For now, treat it as the correction that makes this line true in general.

When the two are independent that cross term is zero and variances simply add. That special case is true, useful, and carried into places it does not belong.

4. Where the square root comes from

Put the two rules together on a sample mean. The sum of n independent draws has variance nσ2 by adding, and dividing by n divides that by n2 by scaling.

Var(X̄) = nσ2 ÷ n2 = σ2 ÷ n   ⇒   SE = σ ÷ √n Read as: the variance of the sample mean is the population variance divided by the sample size, so its standard deviation, which everyone calls the standard error, is the population standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size.

5. Worked example: the batch size decision

Each training example gives a gradient estimate with per-example variance σ2. A minibatch averages B of them. Your team proposes raising the batch size from 32 to 512.

  1. Write the variance of the batch gradient. It is a sample mean of B independent estimates, so Var(gB) = σ2 ÷ B.
  2. Take the square root for the noise scale. The gradient's standard deviation is σ ÷ √B.
  3. Find the factor on B. 512 ÷ 32 = 16.
  4. Find the factor on the noise. √16 = 4, so gradient noise falls by a factor of four.
  5. Find the factor on the cost. Compute per step is linear in the batch size, so it rises by 16.
  6. State the trade. Sixteen times the work per step for four times the precision per step.
  7. Ask the question that matters. Whether that is worth it depends on whether the larger step you can now take recovers the other factor of four, which is what the linear scaling rule for learning rates is trying to do.
  8. Note what did not change. The gradient's expectation. Batch size buys precision, never a different answer.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
x = frame["prompt_tokens"].to_numpy(float)
y = frame["output_tokens"].to_numpy(float)

total = np.var(x + y)
naive = np.var(x) + np.var(y)
cov   = ((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())).mean()

print("Var(X+Y) measured        ", round(total, 3))
print("Var(X) + Var(Y)          ", round(naive, 3))
print("plus 2Cov(X, Y)          ", round(naive + 2 * cov, 3))
print("dropping it is off by    ", f"{abs(total - naive) / total:.2%}")

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You double your sample size. What happens to the standard error of the mean?
The standard error is σ/√n, so doubling n divides it by √2. The first option is the linear intuition the square root exists to correct, and it is what makes people over-buy data. The fourth confuses the variance, which does divide by n, with the standard error, which divides by its root.
You average 100 models, each of variance 1, with pairwise correlation 0.5. Variance of the average?
With correlation ρ the variance of an M-model average is ρσ2 + (1-ρ)σ2/M, which is 0.5 + 0.005. The second option is the independent answer, and it is the trap: it is what you get by dropping the covariance term, and it is fifty times too optimistic. Adding models drives only the second piece to zero, and the floor ρσ2 is why random forests randomise features rather than just growing more trees.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You are sizing an experiment. A metric has a population standard deviation of 40 units, and you need a 95 percent interval no wider than plus or minus 2 units, which needs a standard error of about 1. Work out the sample size. Then your colleague says they can only get a quarter of that. What precision do they actually get, and is the experiment worth running?

Hint

Set σ/√n equal to the standard error you need and solve for n. For the second part, do not recompute from scratch: use the fact that n falling by a factor of four moves the standard error by a factor of √4.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the requirement. SE = σ/√n, and you need SE = 1 with σ = 40.
  2. Solve for the root. √n = 40 ÷ 1 = 40.
  3. Square it. n = 402 = 1,600.
  4. Check the interval. 1.96 × 1 = 1.96, so plus or minus about 2 units. That is the requirement.
  5. Take a quarter of the sample. n = 400.
  6. Move the standard error. n fell by 4, so SE rises by √4 = 2, giving SE = 2.
  7. Read the new interval. 1.96 × 2 = plus or minus 3.92 units, nearly double the target width.
  8. Answer the real question. It depends entirely on the effect size you care about. If you are hunting a 5-unit effect, an interval of plus or minus 4 still resolves it and the experiment is worth running. If you are hunting a 2-unit effect, the interval is wider than the effect and the experiment cannot answer the question at any confidence level, so running it wastes the traffic.

Sanity check. n must grow as the square of the precision you demand, so ask for twice the precision and n must go up by four. If your answer changed n by the same factor as the precision, you used σ/n rather than σ/√n. And the second answer must be exactly 2, not 1.5 or 4, because a quarter of the data is exactly double the error.

Your teacher. Invert the last problem on something you are actually about to measure. Do not start from how much data you can get. Start from how small the interval has to be before you would change a decision, then solve for n. If that n is unaffordable, you have learned something useful before spending anything.
