Module 04 · Eigenvalues, SVD and PCA · Lesson 65

A matrix written in its own coordinates

working 12 min The last page before the SVD, and the one that explains why the SVD is needed.

The one-minute version

  • Put the eigenvectors in the columns of P and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of D, and A = PDP-1.
  • Read it right to left as change coordinates, scale each axis, change back. Nothing is approximated.
  • Then Ak = PDkP-1, so one matrix power becomes n scalar powers.
  • Apply a matrix repeatedly and the largest eigenvalue takes over, which is why power iteration works.
  • It needs n independent eigenvectors, and a shear does not have them. That gap is what lesson 66 closes.

1. Stacking the eigenvectors

Suppose A has eigenpairs (λ1, v1) through (λn, vn). Put the eigenvectors in the columns of a matrix P and the eigenvalues down the diagonal of D. Then column i of AP is Avi, and column i of PD is λivi. Those are equal for every i, so AP = PD.

A = P D P-1 Read as: P holds the eigenvectors as its columns, D is diagonal and holds the matching eigenvalues, and P-1 undoes the change of basis. The formula only exists when P can be inverted, which is exactly when the eigenvectors are linearly independent.

2. Powers, and the proof

The powers result If A = PDP-1 then Ak = PDkP-1 for every positive integer k, and Dk is diagonal with entries λik.

Proof, by telescoping. Write the product out with k copies:

Ak = (PDP-1)(PDP-1) … (PDP-1) Read as: k identical brackets multiplied together. Between each neighbouring pair sits a P-1 immediately followed by a P, and that pair is the identity, so it disappears.

Every interior pair cancels, leaving PDkP-1. And a product of diagonal matrices multiplies corresponding entries, so Dk holds λik.

The step that does the real work is the interior cancellation P-1P = I. It is the entire content of the theorem, and it is available only because P is invertible. A matrix without enough eigenvectors has no such P and gets no such shortcut.

3. Why the largest eigenvalue takes over

Since powers only ever touch D, applying A repeatedly raises each eigenvalue to the power k. If one is larger in magnitude than the rest, its power outruns them, and any starting vector with a component along that eigendirection ends up pointing there.

4. When it fails

The standard defective matrix is the shear [[1, 1], [0, 1]]. Its only eigenvalue is 1, twice, and solving (A - I)v = 0 gives [[0, 1], [0, 0]]v = 0, so v2 = 0 and every eigenvector is a multiple of (1, 0). One direction, not two, so P cannot be inverted.

Yet the powers plainly exist: A5 = [[1, 5], [0, 1]]. The matrix has powers, it simply has no diagonalisation. That gap is exactly what lesson 66 fills, with a factorisation that every matrix has.

5. Worked in full

  1. Take the matrix. A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]], third appearance, so the eigenwork is trusted.
  2. Collect. From lesson 60, eigenvalues 5 and 2 with eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -2). So P = [[1, 1], [1, -2]] and D = diag(5, 2).
  3. Check P is invertible. det(P) = (1)(-2) - (1)(1) = -3, non-zero, so the two eigenvectors are independent and the method applies.
  4. Invert it. P-1 = [[2/3, 1/3], [1/3, -1/3]].
  5. Power the diagonal. D5 = diag(3125, 32). Two scalar powers, not five matrix products.
  6. Reassemble. A5 = PD5P-1 = [[2094, 1031], [2062, 1063]].
  7. Sanity check. The trace of A5 must be 55 + 25 = 3157, and 2094 + 1063 = 3157. The determinant must be 105 = 100000, since the determinant of a power is the power of the determinant. Both hold, and multiplying A by itself five times gives the same matrix entry for entry.
  8. What changes if. Use the shear from section 4. There is one eigendirection rather than two, so step 3 fails and the method stops. Nothing is wrong with the matrix, and its fifth power is still [[1, 5], [0, 1]]. Only the shortcut is unavailable.

6. The cost, measured

code/0065-diagonalisation-and-powers.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
def power_by_sandwich(matrix, k):
    eigenvalues, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(matrix)     # symmetric, so eigh
    return vectors @ np.diag(eigenvalues**k) @ vectors.T

assert np.allclose(power_by_sandwich(cov, 8), np.linalg.matrix_power(cov, 8))

On the 24-channel covariance the two routes agree, and the timing is worth reading honestly. The sandwich is genuinely flat in k: it pays for one eigendecomposition and then raises 24 numbers to the power k, so going from k = 10 to k = 4000 barely moves it. Repeated multiplication does grow, but only logarithmically, because the library squares repeatedly rather than multiplying k times. The claim worth making is that one route is constant in k and the other is not, which is what the numbers show.

The program also runs the routine on the shear and reports the condition number of its eigenvector matrix as 9.0e+15, rather than crashing. Defectiveness announces itself numerically as a P that is very nearly not invertible.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why can a quarter-turn rotation not be diagonalised over the real numbers?
Diagonalising needs a basis of eigenvectors, and a rotation leaves no real direction fixed. The first states a true fact about rotations that has no bearing. The second is doubly wrong: a rotation is not symmetric, and symmetry guarantees diagonalisation rather than blocking it. The third is a true number and irrelevant.
A three-by-three matrix has three distinct eigenvalues. What follows?
Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are always independent, so three of them span the space and P is invertible. Perpendicularity needs symmetry, which is lesson 62 and is a stronger condition. Invertibility needs every eigenvalue non-zero, and distinct does not mean non-zero: one of them could be zero. The last option reverses an implication that does not hold in either direction.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Diagonalise the matrix below and compute its fourth power two ways.

A = [[3, 0], [1, 2]] Read as: a lower-triangular two-by-two matrix, with 3 and 2 on the diagonal and a single 1 below it.
Hint

It is triangular, so you can read the eigenvalues straight off the diagonal without touching the characteristic equation. Then find one eigenvector for each.

Show the worked solution
  1. Eigenvalues. Triangular, so they are the diagonal entries: 3 and 2.
  2. First eigenvector. A - 3I = [[0, 0], [1, -1]] says v1 = v2, so v = (1, 1).
  3. Second eigenvector. A - 2I = [[1, 0], [1, 0]] says v1 = 0, so v = (0, 1).
  4. Assemble and invert. P = [[1, 0], [1, 1]] with det(P) = 1, so P-1 = [[1, 0], [-1, 1]].
  5. Power and reassemble. D4 = diag(81, 16), and A4 = PD4P-1 = [[81, 0], [65, 16]].

Sanity check. trace(A4) = 81 + 16 = 97, matching 34 + 24. And det(A4) = 81 × 16 = 1296 = 64, matching det(A)4. Both invariants behave as the powers result requires. If your lower-left entry came out wrong, check the order in step 5: P goes on the left.
