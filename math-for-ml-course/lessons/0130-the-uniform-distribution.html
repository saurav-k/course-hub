The flattest distribution there is, and the one that proves a density is not a probability

working 10 min Both forms, the ramp CDF, and a published initialiser with a density of 6.53. · Glorot and Bengio (2010), eq 16

The one-minute version

  • Discrete uniform: k outcomes, each with probability 1/k. The simplest distribution there is.
  • Continuous uniform: density 1/(b−a) on the interval [a, b], and zero outside it.
  • Its CDF is a straight ramp, because integrating a constant is multiplying by a width.
  • Narrow the interval and the density goes above 1. The area is fixed at 1, so the height has nowhere else to go.
  • Glorot and Bengio's initialiser is uniform, not Gaussian, and for a 512-to-512 layer its density is 6.53.

1. Discrete first, because it needs no calculus

P(X = xi) = 1 ÷ k,   for each of the k possible values k: how many outcomes there are. Every one gets the same share. It satisfies the axioms trivially: 1/k is positive because k is a positive whole number, and the k of them sum to k/k = 1.

Its CDF is a staircase with k steps, all the same height. Its mean sits at the midpoint of the values and, in the special case of the integers 1 to k, its variance is (k2 − 1) ÷ 12 - which is Module 08's to derive and is a fact to use here.

2. Continuous, and the ramp

f(u) = 1 ÷ (b − a)   for a ≤ u ≤ b,   and 0 elsewhere u: a value the variable might take. a and b: the two endpoints of the interval, with a less than b. f(u): the density at u, which is the same everywhere inside the interval - that flatness is the whole distribution. Mean (a+b)/2, variance (b−a)2/12.

Why the CDF is a straight line

What is assumed: a < b, and the density is the constant 1/(b−a) on the interval.

The shape: the CDF is the integral of the density, and integrating a constant is multiplying by a width.

  1. Below the interval. For c < a the density is zero everywhere up to c, so the area is 0 and F(c) = 0.
  2. Inside it. For c between a and b, the area from a up to c is the constant 1/(b−a) times the width c − a, so F(c) = (c − a) ÷ (b − a).
  3. Above it. For c > b the whole rectangle is included, and (b − a) ÷ (b − a) = 1.
The step that does the real work is that the density is constant, which turns an integral into a multiplication. That is the entire reason this is the distribution every course starts with. There is also a corollary worth noticing in step 3: the height had to be 1/(b−a) and could not have been anything else, because the total area is forced to be 1. A narrow interval must have a tall density; it is not a choice.

3. The density that is 6.53

Here is the payoff. The area under a density is always exactly 1. So if you squeeze the interval, the height must rise to compensate, and nothing stops it passing 1.

4. Where this bites: the initialiser everyone misremembers

The reframing Glorot and Bengio's initialiser is uniform. Their equation 1 is U[−1/√n, +1/√n] and their proposed normalized initialisation, equation 16, is U over ±√6/√(nin + nout). The paper says so in words: "where U[−a, a] is the uniform distribution". The Gaussian initialiser people are usually thinking of is He et al. 2015, and Lesson 133 covers it.

Two other places the uniform is doing quiet work. Inverse-transform sampling starts from Uniform(0,1) and pushes it through an inverse CDF, which is how a sampler produces any other distribution at all. And a random train/test split is a discrete uniform over row orderings.

5. Worked: a real initialiser, by hand

  1. Add the fan-in and fan-out. 512 + 512 = 1,024.
  2. Take the square root. √1,024 = 32, exactly.
  3. Divide √6 by it. √6 = 2.4495, so the bound is 2.4495 ÷ 32 = 0.07655.
  4. Get the width. The interval runs from −0.07655 to +0.07655, so it is 0.15309 wide.
  5. Get the density. 1 ÷ 0.15309 = 6.532 per unit.
  6. Get a probability, which is a width over a width. P(|W| < 0.01) covers a width of 0.02, so 0.02 ÷ 0.15309 = 0.1306.

Sanity check. Density times width must be 1: 6.532 × 0.15309 = 1.0000. And a probability must be under 1, which 0.1306 is, even though the density that produced it is 6.5.

What changes for a 2,048-to-2,048 layer. The sum is 4,096, its root is 64, so the bound halves to 0.03827 and the density doubles to 13.06. Wider layers get smaller weights and a taller density, and the area is 1 in both cases. That shrinking is the entire point of the initialiser: it keeps the variance of the activations stable as layers get wider, which is what stops signals exploding or vanishing through depth.

6. Both halves, at scale

code/0130-the-uniform-distribution.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def glorot_bound(fan_in, fan_out):
    """W ~ U[-sqrt(6)/sqrt(fan_in + fan_out), +...]. Note this is UNIFORM."""
    return float(np.sqrt(6.0) / np.sqrt(fan_in + fan_out))


def probability_of_interval(a, b, low, high):
    """For a uniform, probability is width over width. No integral needed."""
    overlap = max(0.0, min(high, b) - max(low, a))
    return overlap / (b - a)


# P(|W| < 0.01): formula 0.1306, and 200,000 real draws 0.1308

The program also estimates the density empirically, by binning 200,000 draws and dividing each bin's share by the bin's width. That comes out between 6.420 and 6.653 across twenty bins against an exact 6.532, and it is worth doing once: dividing a probability by a width is what "per unit" means, and it is why the number can exceed 1.

The discrete half of the program hashes 25,000 consecutive request ids into 8 shards and gets exactly 3,125 in every shard. That is too good, and the reason is worth knowing: consecutive integers under a modulus are spread perfectly, not randomly. A perfectly balanced split and a uniformly random split are different things, and only the second is a probability model. Real hash keys are not consecutive, and their shard counts wobble.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A distribution has a density of 6.532 over an interval 0.153 wide. What is wrong with that?
6.532 times 0.153 is 1, so this is a perfectly ordinary density, and it is Glorot's published initialiser. The first option is the misconception the page exists to remove: the cap of 1 applies to the integral, never to the height. The fourth inverts the actual requirement, which is that they multiply to exactly 1. The third invents a constraint that does not exist.
Glorot and Bengio's published initialiser draws each weight from which distribution?
The paper states it directly, and both of its initialisers are uniform. The second option is He et al. 2015, which really is a zero-mean Gaussian with standard deviation the square root of 2 over n, and mixing the two up is the specific error this page names. The first is the vaguer version of the same slip. The fourth would put all the mass on exactly two values, which no initialiser does.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A layer has 256 inputs and 1,024 outputs.

Find Glorot's normalized bound, the density, and the probability that a weight lands in the middle tenth of the range.

Hint

The bound uses the sum of fan-in and fan-out, not either one alone. For the last part, remember that a uniform gives every equal-width slice the same probability.

Show the worked solution
  1. Add them. 256 + 1,024 = 1,280.
  2. Take the root. √1,280 = 35.777.
  3. Divide. 2.4495 ÷ 35.777 = 0.06847, so the interval runs from −0.06847 to +0.06847.
  4. Width and density. The width is 0.13693, so the density is 1 ÷ 0.13693 = 7.303.
  5. The middle tenth. It has width 0.013693, so the probability is 0.013693 ÷ 0.13693 = 0.1 exactly.

Sanity check. The last answer must be exactly 0.1 and you should have been able to write it down without any arithmetic at all, because a uniform gives every equal-width slice equal probability and a tenth of the range is a tenth of the probability. If you needed the density to get there, you did more work than the distribution required - and noticing that is the point of the question.

Your teacher. Two habits from this page. First, whenever you see a density, multiply it by the width of its support and check you get 1; that reflex removes the fear of a big number. Second, whenever you read "uniform" in a paper, check whether it is uniform over an interval or uniform over a finite set, because the two have different formulas and people write both the same way. Ask an agent for the density of Uniform(0, 0.001) and check that the answer, 1000, does not alarm you.

Primary source to go deeper

Glorot and Bengio, "Understanding the difficulty of training deep feedforward neural networks" (AISTATS 2010) is where the initialiser comes from. Equation 1 is the heuristic they started with, equation 16 is the one they propose, and both are uniform. Section 3.4 of Hajek's course notes gives the distribution itself in a page.
