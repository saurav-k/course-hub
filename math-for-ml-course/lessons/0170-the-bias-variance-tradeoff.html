Module 09 · Estimation, testing and inference · Lesson 170

Three things add up to your test error, and you control two

frontier 16 min The decomposition every model-selection decision is arguing about.

The one-minute version

  • Err = irreducible + bias2 + variance, and the three terms are separate things.
  • Complexity moves the last two in opposite directions, which is why total error is a U.
  • The first term is the noise in the outcome itself, and no model can remove any of it.
  • At the optimum measured here, 95.5 per cent of the error was irreducible and never the model's fault.
  • Regularization walks the same curve from the other end: it buys variance with bias.

1. The mental model

Estimating a parameter, you can in principle be exactly right. Predicting an outcome, you cannot: the outcome carries its own noise, and even a model that recovered the true function perfectly would still miss by that noise. So prediction error has one term that has nothing to do with your model, and two that do.

2. The decomposition, and its proof

Theorem Suppose Y = f(X) + e with E[e] = 0 and Var(e) = σe2, and let f̂ be fitted on a random training set. At a fixed input x0, under squared-error loss, the expected test error splits into three terms.
Err(x0) = σe2 + (E[f̂(x0)] - f(x0))2 + E[(f̂(x0) - E[f̂(x0)])2] Read as: the first term is the noise variance in the outcome, which no model touches. The second is bias squared: how far the average fitted prediction sits from the truth, averaged over training sets. The third is variance: how much the prediction jumps around as the training set is redrawn.

What is assumed: the noise has mean zero, constant variance, and is independent of the training set. The shape of the argument is to split the error into a noise part and a model part, show the cross term vanishes, then apply lesson 161's identity to what is left.

Step 1. Write Y - f̂ = (Y - f(x0)) + (f(x0) - f̂(x0)), which is e + (f - f̂). Squaring gives three pieces: e2, the squared model gap, and twice their product.

Step 2, and this is the step that does the real work. The cross term vanishes in expectation. The noise e attaches to the test point and is independent of the training set that produced f̂, so the expectation of the product factorises into E[e] times something, and E[e] = 0. That independence is the whole hinge of the proof: if the test point's noise had leaked into the training set, the term would not vanish and the three-way split would not exist.

Step 3. E[e2] = σe2 splits off as the irreducible term, and what remains is E[(f(x0) - f̂(x0))2]. That is the mean squared error of f̂(x0) as an estimator of the fixed number f(x0), and lesson 161 already split that into bias squared plus variance.

The honest boundary. This is a decomposition of expected error under squared loss at one input, and it is clean because squared loss is. Under other losses there is no equally tidy split: the 0-1 loss of a classifier does not decompose this way, and the several proposals that exist do not agree with each other. Quote this result for regression and be careful quoting it for classification.

3. Watching it happen

The programme fits on fresh 80-row training sets, three thousand times at each complexity, and measures all three terms at one fixed test point where the true noiseless response is -8.5479 and σe2 = 9.0.

The optimum is at five predictors with total error 9.4261. Of that, 9.0 is the irreducible term, so 95.5 per cent of the error at the best possible setting was never the model's fault. Past the optimum the damage is fast: at 30 predictors variance is 5.7701, and at 70 predictors on 80 rows it reaches 32.4935 and total error is 41.4937, more than four times the floor.

4. The same shape, from the other direction

Complexity is not only a count of columns. Keep all thirty predictors and vary a ridge penalty instead, and the same trade appears: at λ = 0 the bias squared is 0.0100 with variance 5.6990, and at λ = 1 the bias has risen to 0.0305 while the variance has fallen to 5.4215, taking total error from 14.7090 down to 14.4520.

The reframing Regularization buys a little bias to sell a lot of variance. Module 06 describes the same knob as a constraint region, and lesson 171 describes it a third time as a prior. Three descriptions, one knob, and noticing that they are the same thing is most of what this module is for.

5. The cleanest instance in the subject

Err(x0) = σ2 + [ f(x0) - (1/k)∑l f(xl) ]2 + σ2/k Read as: the k-nearest-neighbour form. σ2 is the irreducible noise. The middle bracket is the bias: the gap between the truth at x0 and the average of the truth at its k neighbours. The last term is the variance, and it is exactly σ2 divided by k.

Here both moving parts are visible in closed form. Raising k averages more neighbours, so the variance term σ2/k falls like one over k; and it reaches further from x0, so the neighbours' average drifts from the truth and the bias grows. There is no way to have both, and the formula says so in symbols rather than in prose.

6. Worked in full

  1. The setting. A k-NN regressor, noise variance σ2 = 4, and a smooth truth whose value at the query point is f(x0) = 10.
  2. At k = 1. The single neighbour has true value 10.6, so bias is 10 - 10.6 = -0.6.
  3. Assemble at k = 1. 4 + 0.36 + 4/1 = 8.36.
  4. At k = 4. The four neighbours average to 11.2, so bias is -1.2 and bias squared is 1.44.
  5. Assemble at k = 4. 4 + 1.44 + 4/4 = 6.44.
  6. At k = 16. The sixteen neighbours average to 12.5, so bias squared is 6.25, and the total is 4 + 6.25 + 4/16 = 10.50.
  7. Sanity check. Every total must exceed 4, the irreducible floor, and all three do. The variance column must fall exactly as 1/k: 4, 1, 0.25. And the totals form a U with its minimum in the middle, at k = 4, which is the whole shape of the lesson on three numbers.
  8. What changes if. Halve the noise to σ2 = 2. The floor and the variance term both halve but the bias does not move, so the optimum shifts to a smaller k: with less noise to average away, averaging costs more than it saves.
  9. What it means here. The right k is a property of your noise level and your function's curvature, not a number to memorise. And a model that has reached the floor cannot be improved by more capacity, only by better features or cleaner labels.

7. Where the U stops being the whole story

The classical U-curve is not the last word. Very heavily over-parameterised models, well past the point where they interpolate the training data exactly, are observed to have test error fall again rather than continue rising. That behaviour is real, it is reproducible, and the decomposition on this page does not predict it. What this page gives you is exact and it is not universal: it is the classical regime, and the honest statement is that the modern regime is an active area rather than a settled one.

8. The programme

code/0170-the-bias-variance-tradeoff.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
preds = np.array([fit(sample_training_set()).predict(x0) for _ in range(3_000)])
bias2 = (preds.mean() - f_true_x0) ** 2
var   = preds.var()
print(bias2, var, sigma2 + bias2 + var)     # the three columns of the table

Because the truth is generated rather than assumed, f(x0) and σ2 are known exactly, so bias and variance can be measured rather than estimated. That is the only reason this table can exist: on real data you never see the three columns separately.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Total error at the optimum was 9.4261, of which 9.0 was irreducible. What removes that 9.0?
The irreducible term is the variance of the noise in Y itself, and it is present even if the model recovers f exactly. Only better features or cleaner labels move it, and neither is a modelling choice. Options 1, 2 and 3 each correctly name something that reduces one of the other two terms, which is what makes them tempting: they answer a different question.
In the k-NN decomposition, what happens as k rises?
The variance term is exactly sigma squared over k, so it falls like one over k, while averaging over neighbours further away drags the average off the truth at the query point. Option 1 would mean no tradeoff exists. Option 4 inverts the bias direction, which is the most common way this formula is misremembered.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A k-NN regressor has noise variance σ2 = 9. At the query point the truth is 20, and the averages of the true values over the nearest k neighbours are 20.3 at k = 1, 21.0 at k = 9, and 22.4 at k = 36. Find the total error at each and say which k wins.

Hint

Three terms every time. The variance term needs no data at all: it is σ2/k.

Show the worked solution
  1. k = 1. Bias 20 - 20.3 = -0.3, squared 0.09. Variance 9/1 = 9. Total 9 + 0.09 + 9 = 18.09.
  2. k = 9. Bias -1.0, squared 1.00. Variance 9/9 = 1. Total 9 + 1.00 + 1 = 11.00.
  3. k = 36. Bias -2.4, squared 5.76. Variance 9/36 = 0.25. Total 9 + 5.76 + 0.25 = 15.01.
  4. The winner. k = 9, at 11.00.

Sanity check. Every total must exceed the floor of 9, and all three do. The variance column must be exactly 9, 1 and 0.25, falling as one over k. And the totals must form a U rather than fall monotonically, which they do: 18.09, 11.00, 15.01. If your k = 36 total came out lowest, the bias was probably squared before the subtraction rather than after.

Your teacher. If the cross term vanishing in step 2 feels like sleight of hand, ask an agent to simulate it: generate training sets, fit, and compute the average of e × (f - f̂) directly. It comes out near zero and shrinks as the number of simulations grows. Then ask it to reuse the test point inside the training set and watch the same average stop being zero.
