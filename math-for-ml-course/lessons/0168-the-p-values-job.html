A p-value answers one question, and it is not the one you asked

working 15 min The misuse catalogue. Lesson 167 produced the number; this page is about reading it.

The one-minute version

  • It is the probability of data at least this extreme if the whole model, null included, is correct.
  • It is computed under H0, so it can never be a probability about H0.
  • Under a true null it is uniform on the unit interval: below 0.05 one time in twenty, by construction.
  • So twenty independent guardrail metrics give a false positive with probability 0.64.
  • Report an effect size and an interval. A p-value alone tells the reader almost nothing they wanted.

1. The definition, read slowly

The definition The p-value is the probability that the chosen test statistic would be at least as extreme as the value observed, if every model assumption were correct, the test hypothesis included.

Every clause is load-bearing. The chosen test statistic: a different statistic on the same data gives a different p-value. At least as extreme: it includes data you did not see. Every model assumption: the independence, the sampling scheme and the distributional form are all inside the conditional, so a small p-value indicts the whole package and not the null alone. The test hypothesis included: H0 is an assumption of the calculation, which is exactly why the answer cannot be a probability about H0.

2. Under a true null, it is uniform

Theorem If the test statistic has a continuous distribution under H0 with cumulative distribution function F, and the p-value is P = 1 - F(T), then P is uniform on (0, 1) under H0.

What is assumed: only that the statistic's distribution under the null is continuous. The shape of the argument is a single substitution: feed a random quantity into its own cumulative distribution function and what comes out is uniform, whatever the original distribution was.

P(P ≤ u) = P(1 - F(T) ≤ u) = P(F(T) ≥ 1 - u) = 1 - (1 - u) = u Read as: chase the definition through. T is the test statistic and F its cumulative distribution function under the null. The third equality is the one doing the work, and it holds because F(T) is itself uniform. The result, P(P ≤ u) = u for every u, is the definition of a uniform distribution.

The step that does the real work is F(T) ∼ Uniform(0,1), the probability integral transform. Everything else is rearrangement. And the consequence is the one people find hardest to accept: a p-value below 0.05 is not rare when the null is true. It happens one time in twenty, because that is what the threshold was defined to mean.

3. The four misreads

The fourth is different and matters more in practice than it looks. A p-value shrinks as the sample grows, so with enough traffic an effect of no consequence whatsoever will clear any threshold you like. Statistical significance is not practical significance, and the only defence is to report the effect size and its interval next to the verdict.

4. Running more tests manufactures findings

If each of k independent tests has a 5 per cent false positive rate, the probability that at least one fires is 1 - 0.95k.

It happens on real data just as readily. Slicing the sessions into twenty region-by-segment groups and splitting each by a coin flip that changes nothing at all, 1 of 20 slices came in at or below 0.05, the best of them at 0.0176. Reporting that one as a finding is p-hacking, and note that no step in it required dishonesty: every individual test was computed correctly. The error is choosing what to report after looking.

5. Two ways to pay for the extra tests

Bonferroni: reject when p ≤ α/k     Benjamini-Hochberg: reject the i-th smallest when p(i) ≤ (i/k)α Read as: k is how many tests you ran, α is the rate you wanted, and p(i) is the i-th smallest p-value after sorting. Bonferroni holds every test to the same tightened bar. Benjamini-Hochberg sorts first and lets the bar rise as it goes down the list.

They control different things. Bonferroni controls the probability of any false positive, which is what you want when a single wrong call is expensive, and it becomes uselessly strict fast: at twenty tests the bar is 0.0025. Benjamini-Hochberg controls the expected proportion of false positives among the things you rejected, which is what you want when you are screening many candidates and can tolerate some noise in the shortlist. It stays usable at hundreds of tests.

6. Worked in full

  1. The setting. An experiment ships with one primary metric and four guardrails, five tests in all, each at alpha = 0.05. Nothing has actually changed.
  2. Per-test error rate. Each test wrongly fires with probability 0.05, by the definition of alpha.
  3. Probability none fires. 0.955 = 0.7738.
  4. Probability at least one fires. 1 - 0.7738 = 0.2262.
  5. Check against simulation. The programme measured 0.2286 over 40,000 experiments, which agrees to within sampling noise.
  6. Sanity check. The answer must lie between 0.05, the single-test rate, and 5 × 0.05 = 0.25, the union bound. 0.2262 sits just under the union bound, which is right because the bound double-counts the cases where two tests fire together.
  7. What changes if. Go to twenty metrics. 1 - 0.9520 = 0.6415, measured 0.6365. Four times the metrics nearly tripled the error rate.
  8. What it means here. A dashboard with twenty metrics and a 0.05 threshold will show you a red or green cell on most experiments that did nothing. Decide the primary metric before the experiment, and treat the guardrails as monitoring rather than as findings.

7. The programme

code/0168-the-p-values-job.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
# 40,000 experiments in which nothing is going on
p = two_sided_p(simulate_null(n_experiments=40_000))
print((p <= 0.05).mean())                    # -> 0.0510, as uniform predicts

for k in (1, 5, 10, 20, 40):                 # the same p-values, k at a time
    any_fires = (p.reshape(-1, k) <= 0.05).any(axis=1).mean()
    print(k, 1 - 0.95**k, any_fires)         # theory against measurement

It closes by slicing the real sessions data twenty ways on a coin flip that changes nothing, and printing the five smallest p-values so the reader can watch p-hacking happen on data they can download.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A colleague reads p = 0.03 as a 3 per cent chance the null is true. Why is that wrong?
The p-value is computed under the assumption that H0 holds, so it is a statement conditional on H0 and cannot be a probability of H0. Getting that quantity needs a prior, which is lesson 171. Option 2 swaps the conditional in the other direction and is equally wrong. Options 1 and 3 treat a category error as a numerical one.
When the null is exactly true, how often does a p-value fall below 0.05?
Under a true null the p-value is uniform, so it falls below any threshold exactly as often as that threshold says. Option 2 is the intuition this page exists to correct. Option 3 is true of the p-value's behaviour under an alternative but not under the null, where uniformity holds at every sample size. Option 4 confuses the median of a uniform with the chance of being small.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A team runs 12 independent tests at alpha = 0.05 on an experiment where nothing changed. Compute the chance at least one is significant, then find the Bonferroni threshold that would hold the overall rate at 0.05, and say what that threshold costs.

Hint

Start from the probability that a single test does not fire, and remember the tests are independent. Bonferroni divides the level by the number of tests.

Show the worked solution
  1. One test stays quiet. Probability 0.95.
  2. All twelve stay quiet. 0.9512 = 0.5404.
  3. At least one fires. 1 - 0.5404 = 0.4596, so about 46 per cent.
  4. Bonferroni threshold. 0.05 / 12 = 0.004167.
  5. What it costs. A real effect now has to clear a bar twelve times stricter, so the miss rate on every one of the twelve rises. Bonferroni buys control of the false positives by spending power.

Sanity check. The answer must sit between 0.05 and the union bound 12 × 0.05 = 0.60, and 0.4596 does, comfortably under the bound as it should be. Cross-check against the page: 10 tests gave 0.4013 and 20 gave 0.6415, and 12 landing at 0.46 fits between them. If you got 0.6 exactly, you used the union bound rather than the exact calculation.

Your teacher. If the uniformity result still feels wrong, ask an agent to simulate 1,000 tests with no effect and plot the p-values sorted. The line comes out straight from 0 to 1. Then ask for the same plot with a real effect present and watch it bend towards zero. The difference between those two pictures is what a p-value is for.
