Module 04 · Eigenvalues, SVD and PCA · Lesson 66

Every matrix factors, whatever its shape

working 14 min The factorisation with no exceptions, built entirely from the spectral theorem.

The one-minute version

  • A = UΣVT exists for every matrix of every shape, square or not.
  • It gives up on one basis and uses two: one for the input space, one for the output space.
  • The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATA, which lesson 64 showed is semidefinite.
  • They are never negative and always ordered, unlike eigenvalues, which are neither.
  • Rank becomes countable: it is the number of non-zero singular values.

1. Four ways the eigendecomposition has let us down

Three are already on the record. A matrix can have complex eigenvalues, from lesson 61. Its eigenvectors need not be perpendicular, from lesson 62. It may not have enough of them at all, from lesson 65.

The fourth has not been said yet and is the most important. A non-square matrix has no eigenvectors whatsoever, because Av lives in a different space from v and the equation Av = λv cannot even be written down. A data matrix is almost never square, so this is the common case rather than the exotic one.

2. The factorisation

A = U Σ VT Read as: A is m by n. U is m by m and orthogonal, and its columns are the left singular vectors, living in the output space. V is n by n and orthogonal, and its columns are the right singular vectors, living in the input space. Σ is m by n, zero everywhere except the diagonal, which carries the non-negative singular values in decreasing order.

Two differences from eigenvalues are worth fixing now. Singular values are never negative, because they are defined as square roots of non-negative numbers. And they come in a conventional decreasing order, so "the first singular value" is meaningful in a way that "the first eigenvalue" is not.

3. Where they come from, and why it always works

Existence Every real m by n matrix can be written UΣVT with U and V orthogonal and Σ diagonal with non-negative decreasing entries.

Step 1, build V and the singular values. The matrix ATA is n by n and symmetric, since transposing it gives itself back. Lesson 64 showed it is positive semidefinite in one line. So lesson 62 applies: it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors v1, …, vn with real eigenvalues, and lesson 64's test says those eigenvalues are non-negative. Define σi = √λi, which is real precisely because the eigenvalues cannot be negative. Let r be how many are strictly positive.

Step 2, build the first r columns of U. For i ≤ r set ui = Avi / σi, which is legal because σi > 0.

Step 3, check they are orthonormal. This is the step everything turns on.

uiTuj = (Avi)T(Avj) / (σiσj) = viT(ATA)vj / (σiσj) = λj(viTvj) / (σiσj) Read as: the middle equality regroups the product so that the two copies of A meet in the centre as ATA. The last uses that vj is an eigenvector of that matrix. If i and j differ, the dot product of two different v's is zero and the whole thing vanishes. If they are the same, it is λi over σi squared, which is 1.

So the constructed u's are orthonormal, and any orthonormal set can be extended to a full orthonormal basis of the output space.

Step 4, check it rebuilds A. For i ≤ r, Avi = σiui by the definition in step 2. For i > r the singular value is zero, and ‖Avi‖2 = viTATAvi = λi = 0, so Avi is the zero vector. Either way Avi = σiui for every i. Stacking those as columns gives AV = UΣ, and multiplying on the right by VT finishes it.

The step that does the real work Step 3's middle equality. The spectral theorem handed us orthonormality in the input space, and that one regrouping transfers it to the output space. Everything else is bookkeeping. Notice too that step 4's second half identified the directions with zero singular value as exactly the null space, which arrives as a by-product rather than a separate claim.
The honest boundary. The proof assumed A is real; the complex case is identical with conjugate transposes throughout. It also used that an orthonormal set extends to an orthonormal basis, which is module 03's Gram-Schmidt. And the factorisation is not unique when singular values repeat or when r is less than m, because the extension in step 3 involved a free choice. Lesson 68 says exactly when that freedom matters.

4. The rank-one sum, and rank as a number

A = σ1u1v1T + … + σrurvrT Read as: the same layered shape as lesson 62's spectral sum, except that each layer now pairs a vector from the output space with a different vector from the input space. Each term is a rank-one matrix and σi is its weight.

The rank of A is the number of non-zero singular values, which makes rank measurable rather than symbolic. It also makes it a judgement call when the small values are not exactly zero, which the next figure shows and lesson 68 resolves.

5. Eigen against SVD, side by side

6. Worked in full

  1. Take a matrix with no eigenvectors. A = [[3, 2, 2], [2, 3, -2]], which is 2 by 3.
  2. Pick the cheaper Gram matrix. ATA is 3 by 3 and AAT is 2 by 2, so use the small one: AAT = [[17, 8], [8, 17]].
  3. Diagonalise it. trace = 34, det = 289 - 64 = 225, so λ2 - 34λ + 225 = 0 with roots 25 and 9.
  4. Singular values. σ1 = √25 = 5 and σ2 = √9 = 3. Both whole numbers.
  5. Left singular vectors. For 25, [[-8, 8], [8, -8]] gives u1 = (1, 1)/√2. For 9, [[8, 8], [8, 8]] gives u2 = (1, -1)/√2.
  6. Right singular vectors, by the proof's formula run backwards. vi = ATui / σi, giving v1 = (1, 1, 0)/√2 and v2 = (1, -1, 4)/(3√2).
  7. Sanity check, three ways. Av1 should be 5u1, and it is. The squared singular values should sum to the sum of every squared entry: 25 + 9 = 34 and 9 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 9 + 4 = 34. And ATA must have eigenvalues 25, 9 and 0, the extra zero appearing because a 2 by 3 matrix maps three dimensions into two and must collapse one.
  8. What changes if. Transpose A to get a 3 by 2 matrix. The singular values are unchanged at 5 and 3, because ATA and AAT share their non-zero eigenvalues. The roles of U and V simply swap.

7. Rank is a choice, not a number

code/0066-the-svd.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
numerical = np.linalg.matrix_rank(data)          # 24
for cut in (0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0):
    print(cut, int((singular > cut).sum()))      # 24, 24, 4, 3

Every singular value is non-zero because noise is present, so the numerical rank is genuinely full at 24. But the matrix was built from four components, and only a threshold placed inside the cliff recovers that. Rank here is a decision about where signal stops, and lesson 68 is where the decision gets made.

The program also builds the whole factorisation by following the proof literally, then checks it against the library. The constructed U misses orthonormality by 4.1e-14 and the rebuilt matrix differs from the original by 1.3e-15, so step 3 really does deliver what it claims. The singular vectors agree with the library's up to sign, which is the same freedom eigenvectors had on lesson 60.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

How do the singular values of A relate to the eigenvalues of A transpose A?
From A equals U Sigma V transpose we get A transpose A equals V Sigma squared V transpose, so the eigenvalues are the squared singular values. The first option forgets the square root and is the most common form of this error. The second describes the pseudoinverse's singular values, a different question. The third contradicts the construction the page just gave.
What can the SVD do that the eigendecomposition cannot?
Existing for every matrix of every shape is the whole point, and it is what the four disappointments in section 1 were leading to. The other three are true of the eigendecomposition as well whenever it exists, so none of them distinguishes the two.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Find the SVD of the matrix below by hand, then explain why its singular values are not its eigenvalues.

A = [[2, 0], [0, -3]] Read as: a diagonal two-by-two matrix whose second diagonal entry is negative.
Hint

ATA is already diagonal, so no algebra is needed for it. Remember that Σ must come out non-negative and in decreasing order, which forces something to move.

Show the worked solution
  1. Form the Gram matrix. ATA = [[4, 0], [0, 9]], diagonal already.
  2. Read its eigenvalues. 9 and 4, so σ1 = 3 and σ2 = 2, ordered decreasing.
  3. Right singular vectors. The eigenvector for 9 is (0, 1) and for 4 is (1, 0).
  4. Left singular vectors. u1 = Av1/3 = (0, -3)/3 = (0, -1) and u2 = Av2/2 = (2, 0)/2 = (1, 0).
  5. Compare with the eigenvalues. Those are 2 and -3. The singular values are 3 and 2: the magnitudes, reordered. The minus sign has moved out of Σ and into u1, because Σ is required to be non-negative.

Sanity check. σ12 + σ22 = 9 + 4 = 13, and the sum of every squared entry of A is 4 + 9 = 13. Also |det(A)| = 6 = 3 × 2. If you got singular values of 2 and 3 in that order, the decreasing convention was missed rather than the mathematics.

Your teacher. If step 3 of the proof is the piece that will not settle, ask an agent to run it numerically on a small random matrix: build the u's from the formula, then print the full matrix of pairwise dot products. Watching an identity matrix appear out of an expression that never mentioned the output space is what makes the argument stick.
