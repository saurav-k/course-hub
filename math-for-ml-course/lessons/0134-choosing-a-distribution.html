Module 07 · Probability · Lesson 134

You choose a distribution by asking three questions, not by looking at a histogram

working 13 min The decision procedure, the assumption that breaks each family, and what a wrong choice costs. · Hajek, ECE 313, appendix 6.3

The one-minute version

  • Question 1: what values can it take? Binary, one of k, a count, a duration, a bounded range, all the reals. This alone eliminates most of the list.
  • Question 2: what mechanism produces it? One trial, n trials, trials until, events at a rate, gaps between them, a sum of many small effects.
  • Question 3: what do you actually know? Sometimes less than the distribution you were about to pick requires.
  • Every family has one assumption that breaks it, and the table below names all eight.
  • A wrong family can agree on the decision and be wrong by four times on the risk that decision carries.

1. The three questions

Question 1, the support. What values can the quantity take? Not what values you have seen - what values are possible. A count cannot be negative or fractional. A duration cannot be negative. A probability cannot exceed 1. This question is free to answer and it is the one people skip.

Question 2, the mechanism. What process generates the number? One trial, or n of them? Waiting for something, or counting arrivals in a window? A sum of many small independent contributions? The mechanism is what separates two families that share a support.

Question 3, what you know. A rate? A mean? A range and nothing else? Only a variance? The honest answer is sometimes "less than the family I was about to choose requires", and noticing that is worth more than a better fit.

3. The diagnostics, run on real columns

Question 3 has a practical form: each family forces a relationship between its own summary statistics, and checking that relationship is nearly free.

  1. Poisson forces variance to equal mean. The per-second arrival counts have mean 2.2967 and variance 2.3388, a ratio of 1.018. That earns the model.
  2. Exponential forces the standard deviation to equal the mean. The gaps have mean 0.43538 and standard deviation 0.43816, a ratio of 1.0064. Earned.
  3. Geometric forces the mean to be 1/p. The retries have mean 1.1786, so 1 ÷ 1.1786 = 0.8485, recovering the true 0.85.
  4. Normal forces 68.27 percent within one standard deviation. The latency column gives 79.58 percent. Not earned, and Lesson 133 showed why: a heavy tail inflated the standard deviation.

Sanity check. Three diagnostics landed within two percent of what the family demands and one missed by eleven percentage points. If all four had passed perfectly you would be looking at fitted values rather than at data.

4. Worked: pricing one family choice

A decision with a number attached. Bin the 25,000 arrivals into 10,884 one-second windows. If we provision for 6 concurrent requests a second, what overflow rate do we promise the service review?

Two candidate families. A normal, fitted with mean 2.2967 and standard deviation 1.5293. A Poisson, fitted with λ = 2.2967 - note it has one parameter where the normal has two, because its mean and variance are the same number.

  1. Question 1 kills one of them immediately. Counts are non-negative whole numbers. The normal is continuous and unbounded below, and at these parameters it places 6.66 percent of its mass on a negative number of requests. That should end the discussion, and it almost never does, because nobody checks.
  2. Question 2 confirms the survivor. Independent arrivals at a constant rate is the Poisson process of Lesson 132, exactly.
  3. Question 3 agrees. Variance 2.3388 against mean 2.2967 is a ratio of 1.018, which is what a Poisson demands.
  4. Now fit both anyway, and find the capacity. The normal's 99th percentile is 2.2967 + 2.326 × 1.5293 = 5.854, so provision 6. The Poisson's CDF first reaches 0.99 at k = 6, so provision 6. The two families agree on the decision.
  5. Price the risk that decision carries. The normal says P(N > 6) = 0.0030. The Poisson says 0.0093.
  6. Check against the file. 125 of the 10,884 seconds actually exceeded 6, which is 0.0115.
The reframing A model can be wrong in a way that never shows up in the decision it was built for, and shows up badly in the commitment made alongside it. Both families said "provision 6". Only one of them told the truth about what that would cost you.

Sanity check. The two families must agree roughly in the middle and differ in the tail, because that is exactly what a family choice controls. They do, and the observed risk sits above both, which says even the Poisson is slightly optimistic here.

What changes if the objective moves to 0.5 percent. Both families now say provision 7, so the decision is still robust. What is not robust is the belief attached to it, and that belief is what goes in a document someone else reads six months later. The number you commit to is more sensitive to the family than the number you build.

5. Fitting is not choosing

Everything above is about picking a family. Estimating its parameters once picked - maximum likelihood, maximum a posteriori, and how much to trust the result - is Module 09's subject. The two are genuinely separate skills, and doing the second one well does not rescue a wrong answer to the first. A perfectly fitted normal on count data is still a distribution that thinks negative requests happen.

6. When you are unsure

  1. Pick the simplest family the support allows. Question 1 is cheap and it is usually decisive.
  2. Write the assumption in the same paragraph as the answer. Not in an appendix. If the reader has the number, they should have the assumption.
  3. Compute the answer under the next-simplest family too. This costs one extra line of code.
  4. Read the comparison. If the two agree, the choice did not matter and you can stop worrying. If they disagree, that disagreement is the real result: your answer depends on an assumption rather than on the data, and that is what you should be reporting.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You have count data with a mean of 12 and a variance of 47. Is a Poisson a reasonable model for it?
A Poisson has variance equal to its mean, and this data is nearly four times over-dispersed, which says something is clustering the events. The third option names a genuine requirement that is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. The fourth is the most tempting wrong answer, because the window really does set lambda - but changing the window scales mean and variance together and cannot repair a ratio of four.
A quantity is a non-negative duration and the only thing you know about it is its mean. Which family does the procedure reach for, and what does that choice assume?
Support on the non-negative reals plus a single known mean points at the exponential, and the assumption it smuggles in is memorylessness. The first fails question 1: a normal puts mass below zero. The second needs a bounded range you do not have. The third confuses a duration with a count, which is exactly the Poisson-exponential distinction of Lesson 132.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

For each of these, name the family and name the one assumption that would break it:

(a) whether a request is served from cache; (b) how many of 512 rows in a batch are flagged; (c) the seconds between two arrivals; (d) which of three routes a request takes; (e) the number of attempts before an upstream call succeeds.

Hint

Answer question 1 for all five first. Two of them share a support and are told apart only by question 2 - find that pair before you write anything down.

Show the worked solution
  1. (a) Bernoulli. Support is two values. Breaks if "cached" is not really binary - a partial or stale hit is a third state, and forcing it into two loses the distinction that matters.
  2. (b) Binomial. Support is 0 to 512, and the mechanism is a fixed number of trials. Breaks if the rows are not independent, which sampling without replacement guarantees, or if the flag rate varies across the batch.
  3. (c) Exponential. Support is a non-negative duration, and the mechanism is the gap between arrivals. Breaks if arrivals are bursty, because then the rate is not constant.
  4. (d) Categorical. Support is one of three states. Breaks if the routes are not exhaustive and mutually exclusive - a re-routed request counted twice violates both.
  5. (e) Geometric. Support is 1, 2, 3, ..., and the mechanism is trials until a success. Breaks if the attempts are not independent, which a backoff policy or a cold cache makes true.

Sanity check. Items (b) and (e) are both counts of trials and they get different families, which is the check that you used question 2 rather than stopping at question 1. If you gave them the same answer, you stopped at the support. And notice that four of the five break through an independence assumption of some kind - that is not a coincidence, it is the assumption this module leans on hardest.

Your teacher. The habit to leave Module 07 with is the two-line diagnostic. Whenever you fit a distribution, compute the one relationship its family forces - variance against mean for a Poisson, standard deviation against mean for an exponential, the one-sigma band for a normal - and print it next to the fit. It costs nothing and it catches a wrong family before the wrong family reaches a document. Ask an agent to add that check to the next model you fit, whatever it is.
