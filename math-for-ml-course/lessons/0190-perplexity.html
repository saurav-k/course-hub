Perplexity is how many options the model is effectively choosing among

working 12 min Gao et al., The Pile (2021), section 3.1, and Brown et al. (1992), section 2.2.

The one-minute version

  • Exponentiate the cross-entropy and you get a count of options rather than a count of bits.
  • Equivalently, it is one over the geometric mean of the probabilities the model gave the things that actually happened.
  • A uniform model over V options scores exactly V. A perfect model scores exactly 1. Everything sits between.
  • It is a geometric mean, so the smallest probabilities dominate it and accuracy cannot see what it sees.
  • It is per token, so changing the tokenizer changes it without changing the model. Demonstrated here, not asserted.

1. A count instead of a length

Cross-entropy is measured in bits per symbol, and bits are hard to feel. Two and a half bits per token is not a thing anyone has an intuition about.

But you do have an intuition about dice. If a model were choosing uniformly among V options, its cross-entropy would be log2 V bits. So run that backwards: take whatever cross-entropy you measured and ask how many equally likely options would produce it.

PPL = exp( −(1/N) ∑i ln q(xi) ) Read as: perplexity is e raised to the mean negative log-likelihood. The sum runs over the N symbols that actually occurred, and q(xi) is the probability the model assigned to the i-th of them before it saw it. Using e and the natural logarithm together is a convention; base 2 with a 2 outside gives the same number.

A model with perplexity 7 is on average as uncertain as somebody rolling a fair seven-sided die. That is a sentence you can hold, and it is why the metric survived for fifty years.

2. Three readings, one number

The definition above is one of three expressions, and they are equal for a reason worth seeing rather than accepting.

  1. Start from the geometric mean of the assigned probabilities, raised to minus one over N: (∏i qi)−1/N.
  2. Push it through exp of ln, which is the identity on positive numbers: exp( ln (∏ qi)−1/N ).
  3. Bring the exponent down. exp( −(1/N) ln ∏ qi ).
  4. Split the log of the product. ln(ab) = ln a + ln b, so this is exp( −(1/N) ∑ ln qi ), which is the definition. This is the step that does the real work, and it is not merely convenient: the product of twenty thousand numbers below one underflows float64 long before you finish multiplying it, so the geometric mean is the meaning and the log-space sum is the only way to obtain it.
  5. Change base for the third form. exp(x) = 2x/ln 2, so the same number is 2 raised to the mean bits per symbol.
The reframing Perplexity is one over the geometric mean of the probabilities the model gave the words that actually occurred. That reading is what makes the die analogy honest rather than decorative: if the model gave every occurring token a probability of one seventh, the geometric mean is one seventh and the perplexity is seven, exactly as a fair seven-sided die would produce.

The two anchors follow immediately. A uniform model over V options assigns 1/V every time, the geometric mean is 1/V, and the perplexity is V. A perfect model assigns 1 every time and scores 1. So perplexity always lies between 1 and V for a model that never assigns zero, and it reads directly as "how many options, effectively".

3. Where the number comes from

A geometric mean is dominated by its smallest terms, and that is not a defect: it is the whole reason to use one. But it means perplexity and accuracy answer different questions, and the gap between them is measurable.

On the twenty thousand held-out predictions this module ships, the worst 0.1 per cent of symbols carry 1.6 per cent of the total loss, the worst 1 per cent carry 10.4 per cent, and the worst 5 per cent carry 33.5 per cent. Drop the worst 1 per cent and perplexity falls from 2.1293 to 1.9821, while accuracy barely moves, from 0.7924 to 0.8004.

4. The trap, demonstrated rather than asserted

Perplexity is defined per token. Tokens are whatever your tokenizer says they are. So two models that segment text differently are reporting numbers in different units, and comparing them is not a comparison.

Here is the sharpest possible form of that, and it is a theorem rather than a caution. Take a sequence and group it into adjacent pairs, treating each pair as one token. The model's beliefs about the underlying text have not changed at all: the probability of a pair is the product of its two probabilities, so the total log-likelihood is identical to the last decimal place. But perplexity divides by the token count, which has just halved, so the exponent doubles and the new perplexity is the square of the old one.

Brown and colleagues saw this in 1992 and wrote the corresponding warning about a subtler version of it: restricting a model to the test sample's own vocabulary makes the cross-entropy "noticeably lower", but such a value "could not be trumpeted as upper bounds to the entropy of English". Making the units friendlier to yourself is not an improvement.

The modern fix is to normalise to something the tokenizer cannot move: bytes.

BPB = (LT / LB) · ℓ / ln 2 Read as: bits per UTF-8 byte equals the number of tokens per byte, multiplied by the loss in nats per token, divided by the natural logarithm of two to convert nats to bits. LT is the dataset's length in tokens and LB its length in bytes, so their ratio is a property of the tokenizer and the corpus together.

The Pile adopts it and says why: bits per byte is "preferred over bits per character or perplexity ... due to its invariance to different tokenization schemes and the ambiguity of measuring characters in Unicode". Their measured ratio across the whole corpus is 0.29335 GPT-2 tokens per byte.

Two smaller traps in the same family, one line each. Perplexity depends on whether documents are concatenated or scored independently, and The Pile states which it chose because the two give different numbers. And it depends on the context length the model was given, since a model predicting token 2,000 with 1,999 tokens of context is doing an easier job than one with 511. A perplexity quoted without both of those is not reproducible.

5. Worked: four tokens, then a retokenisation

  1. Take four assigned probabilities. 0.40, 0.20, 0.10, 0.05, one for each token that occurred.
  2. Sum the logs. ln 0.40 + ln 0.20 + ln 0.10 + ln 0.05 = −0.9163 − 1.6094 − 2.3026 − 2.9957 = −7.8240.
  3. Take the mean and negate. 7.8240 / 4 = 1.9560 nats per token.
  4. Exponentiate. PPL = e1.9560 = 7.0711.
  5. Check by the other route. The product is 0.40 × 0.20 × 0.10 × 0.05 = 0.0004, and its fourth root is 0.1414. One over that is 7.0711. Same number.
  6. Sanity check. A geometric mean lies between the smallest and largest of the things it averages, so the perplexity must lie between 1/0.40 = 2.5 and 1/0.05 = 20. It does. If your answer fell outside that range you averaged the probabilities rather than their logarithms.
  7. What changes if you pair adjacent tokens? On the committed data, per-token perplexity goes from 2.1293 to 4.5339, exactly the square, and bits per symbol stays at 1.0904 to nine decimal places. The model was not consulted.
  8. Convert to the tokenizer-proof unit. At The Pile's 0.29335 tokens per byte, a loss of 2.0 nats per token becomes 0.29335 × 2.0 / 0.6931 = 0.8464 bits per byte, and that number can be compared with any other model on the same corpus.
code/0190-perplexity.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
a = perplexity_from_nll(nll)                    # exp of the mean
b = perplexity_from_geometric_mean(assigned)    # 1 over the geometric mean
c = float(2.0 ** (nll / np.log(2.0)).mean())    # 2 to the mean bits
assert abs(a - b) < 1e-9 and abs(a - c) < 1e-9  # all three: 2.129295

6. What it does not measure

Perplexity scores one thing: how much probability the model put on text that really occurred. That is a real and useful thing, and it is not the same as being useful.

A model can lower its perplexity by getting better at the frequent, easy, boilerplate tokens that make up most of any corpus, while getting no better, or worse, at the rare tokens a task depends on. Section 3 is the mechanism: the metric is an average, and averages can be moved by the bulk. The scaling-laws lesson in LLM Papers puts perplexity on the y axis and is worth reading with section 4 in mind, and the GPT-2 lesson in How Language Models Happened shows the metric doing its historical job.

The reframing That closes the module. Every quantity in these eleven pages was −log p, averaged in a different way: entropy averages it under the truth, cross-entropy averages the model's version of it under the truth, KL is the difference between those two averages, mutual information is that difference for one specific pair of distributions, information gain is that same difference used greedily, and perplexity is the whole thing exponentiated so a person can picture it.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Model A scores perplexity 18 with a 32,000-token vocabulary. Model B scores 22 with 50,257. Which is better?
Perplexity is per token and the two models do not agree on what a token is, so the numbers are not comparable. Convert both to bits per byte first. The second option is the mistake this page exists to prevent. The third is closer to right in spirit, since the ceiling does differ, but "proportionally" is not the correction: the fix is a change of unit, not a rescaling. The fourth invents a precise margin from two incomparable numbers, which is worse than the second because it looks careful.
A model reports a perplexity of exactly 1.0 on a test set. What does that mean?
A geometric mean of 1 requires every term to be 1, since none can exceed it. The first option is off by a factor: a fair coin gives perplexity 2, not 1. The second is the strongest distractor because it is a strictly weaker condition that sounds identical: a model can be right on every argmax while assigning only 0.6 each time, which gives perplexity 1.67. The fourth would let a trivial model score 1, but it is a claim about the data and the question asked about the model.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A model scores a five-token sentence, assigning the tokens that occurred the probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.125 and 0.5.

Find the mean negative log-likelihood in nats and in bits, then the perplexity by two different routes, checking they agree. The corpus has 0.25 tokens per UTF-8 byte under this tokenizer: convert, and say why you would report that number instead.

Hint

Every probability here is a power of one half, so the bits are whole numbers and you can do the whole problem without a calculator.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read them as powers of two. 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.125, 0.5 are 2−1, 2−2, 2−1, 2−3, 2−1.
  2. So the bits are. 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, totalling 8 over five tokens: exactly 1.6 bits per token.
  3. In nats. 1.6 × ln 2 = 1.1090 nats.
  4. First route to perplexity. 21.6 = 3.0314.
  5. Second route. The product is 2−8, its fifth root is 2−1.6 = 0.32988, and one over that is 3.0314. They agree.
  6. Convert. BPB = 0.25 × 1.6 = 0.400 bits per byte. Here the nats and the ln 2 cancel because you already had bits.
  7. Why report it. Because it is invariant to the tokenizer, which is the reason The Pile adopted it, and because a per-token number cannot be compared with any model that segments text differently.
  8. One thing to notice. 3.03 on a five-token sentence is not comparable with 3.03 on a paragraph either: five samples is far too few to estimate a geometric mean stably, and this problem is arithmetic practice rather than a measurement.

Sanity check. The perplexity must lie between 1/0.5 = 2 and 1/0.125 = 8, because a geometric mean lies between the extremes it averages. 3.03 sits inside. If you got something outside that range, you averaged the probabilities directly instead of averaging their logarithms, which is the one mistake this metric invites.

Your teacher. If you want section 4 to stop being a warning and start being a fact you own, ask an agent to take any short text, tokenize it two ways, and compute the per-token perplexity of a fixed toy model under each. Then have it print bits per byte for both. One pair of numbers will differ and the other will not, and you will never quote a bare perplexity again.
