A logarithm answers one question: what exponent?

foundation 11 min Four rules, one sentence behind all of them, and one rule that does not exist.

The one-minute version

  • logb(y) is the answer to "what power of b gives y". Everything else on this page follows from that.
  • In machine learning, an unlabelled log means the natural logarithm. Reading it as base 10 scales every cross-entropy by 2.303.
  • The three identities and change of base are all one exponent rule, applied four ways. This page proves each one in two lines.
  • There is no rule for a sum, and that absence is not an oversight: it is the entire reason a logsumexp routine has to exist.
  • A straight line on a log-y axis means exponential, which makes the axis a diagnostic rather than a squashing.

1. The exponential comes first

A logarithm is defined from an exponential, so the exponential has to be first. bx multiplies b by itself x times, and the thing to notice is not the formula but the behaviour: the rate of growth is proportional to the current size. Something that doubles every ten epochs is growing slowly at first and alarmingly later, and it never changes its rule.

The base machine learning defaults to is e = 2.71828…, and it earns that place by having a series that is built out of factorials:

ez = 1 + z/1! + z2/2! + z3/3! + … Read as: the exponential of z is an infinite sum whose k-th term is z raised to the k, divided by k factorial. The exclamation mark is a factorial, which lesson 08 counts with; here it is enough to know it grows fast enough to keep the sum finite.

You will not need to compute with that series. It is here because it makes one thing plain that the notation hides: the exponential and the counting on lesson 08 are the same mathematics seen from two ends.

2. The logarithm, and what an unlabelled log means

logb(y) = x   means   bx = y Read as: the logarithm to base b of y is the exponent you have to raise b to in order to get y. b is the base, y is the number you have, and x is the answer.

Two consequences fall straight out. The input must be positive, because no power of a positive base is ever zero or negative - which is why log 0 is minus infinity, and why -inf in a training log usually means a probability reached zero. And log and exp are inverses, in exactly the sense of lesson 03.

The convention that costs people a day In machine learning an unlabelled log is the natural logarithm. The Deep Learning notation table lists it flatly: "log x - Natural logarithm of x". Base 2 appears only where the answer is deliberately in bits, and a paper doing that will say so. Read an unlabelled log as base 10 and every cross-entropy, log-likelihood and perplexity you compute is out by a factor of ln 10 = 2.303, with no error message.

3. Four rules, one sentence, four proofs

Each proof is the same move: name the exponents, use one exponent law, and read the answer back. Write u = ln a and v = ln b, so a = eu and b = ev, with a and b positive.

ln(ab) = ln a + ln b Read as: the log of a product is the sum of the logs. Proof: ab = euev = eu+v, so the exponent that produces ab is u + v, which is ln a + ln b.
ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b Read as: the log of a quotient is the difference of the logs. Proof: a/b = eu/ev = eu−v, so the exponent is u − v.
ln(an) = n ln a Read as: an exponent inside a log comes out as a multiplier. Proof: an = (eu)n = eun, so the exponent is un, which is n ln a.
logb(x) = ln x / ln b Read as: to change base, divide by the log of the new base. Proof: let y = logb(x), so by = x by definition. Take ln of both sides and use the power rule: y ln b = ln x, and ln b is not zero, so divide.
The step that does the work Every one of those four proofs is euev = eu+v, used once. That is why this page teaches one sentence rather than four rules: if you can rebuild the first proof you can rebuild all of them, and there is nothing left to memorise.

An honest boundary. These hold without qualification for positive real numbers, which is every case this course needs. The reference these are checked against, the NIST Digital Library, states them for complex arguments with a restriction on the phase. That restriction is real and it is not this course's problem, and saying so is better than implying the unrestricted version is universal.

4. The rule that does not exist

The counterexample is as small as it gets: ln(1 + 1) = ln 2 = 0.693, while ln 1 + ln 1 = 0 + 0 = 0. Those are not close and there is no correction factor that would rescue the identity.

This matters more than an algebra slip usually does. Lesson 07 turns a product of thousands of probabilities into a sum of logarithms, and the moment it needs to add two of those probabilities back together it can no longer stay in log space by any rule on this page. The way out is called logsumexp, and it belongs to M10.

5. A log axis is a diagnostic

The rule is worth holding as a pair. Straight on a log-y axis means exponential; straight on log-log means a power law. Those two are different claims, and telling them apart is most of reading a scaling plot.

6. Fitting an exponential from its median

Response times in the ticket data are exponential, and one number fixes the whole curve. Getting at it is a two-line application of the rules above.

  1. Write down what "median" means. Half the tickets take longer than the median m, so the survival at m is a half: e−λm = 0.5.
  2. Take logs of both sides. −λm = ln(0.5), and by the quotient rule ln(0.5) = ln 1 − ln 2 = −ln 2.
  3. Cancel the sign. λm = ln 2, so λ = ln(2) / m. One rule from section 3 did all the work.
  4. Put the observed number in. The median of the 5,000 response times is 420.305 seconds, so λ = 0.693 / 420.305 = 0.00164915 per second.
  5. Test the fit rather than trusting it. At t = 800s the fitted survival is 0.2673 and the observed share is 0.2688.
Sanity check. An exponential's mean is 1/λ, which here is 606.4 seconds against an observed mean of 595.3 - the right size, and not suspiciously exact. What changes if the median doubles: λ halves, because the median sits in the denominator. A slower queue has a flatter curve and a fatter tail.
The same relationship, with the same ln 2, is worked from the other direction in this hub's Statistical Foundations course, on a spending dataset rather than a queue. Two datasets, one identity.

7. Checked on the data, not asserted

code/0006-logs-and-exponentials.py · runnable Python, numpy and pandas only · download · data: tickets.csv
assert np.allclose(np.log(a * b),  np.log(a) + np.log(b))     # product
assert np.allclose(np.log(a / b),  np.log(a) - np.log(b))     # quotient
assert np.allclose(np.log(a ** 3), 3 * np.log(a))             # power
assert np.allclose(np.log2(a),     np.log(a) / np.log(2))     # change of base
assert not np.allclose(np.log(a + b), np.log(a) + np.log(b))  # and the one that is not a rule

All four identities hold to within 3.55e-15 on a thousand real pairs, which is floating-point noise rather than disagreement. The last line is the interesting one: it asserts the false identity is false, and the program prints the worst pair it found, where ln(a+b) is 8.50 and ln a + ln b is 15.59.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A paper writes log p(x) with no base given. Which base should you assume?
Machine learning writes log for ln and says so in its notation tables. The second option is the school convention and it scales every result by 2.303 with no error message, which is why this is worth a callout rather than a footnote. The third is right only where a paper is quoting an answer in bits, and such a paper will say so explicitly. The fourth is not a thing: a base is a choice of notation, not a property of data.
Which of these is not a logarithm rule?
There is no identity for the logarithm of a sum, and the counterexample is one line: ln 2 is 0.693 while ln 1 plus ln 1 is 0. The other three are the genuine rules and this page proves all three from the same exponent law. The absence of the third is not an oversight - it is precisely why a logsumexp routine has to be written by hand.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A training schedule halves the learning rate every ten epochs: lr(t) = 0.1 × 0.5t/10.

(a) Is this growth or decay, and what is its half-life?
(b) At which epoch does the rate first reach 0.0125?
(c) Rewrite it in the form lr(t) = 0.1 × e−kt and give k.

Hint

For (c), write 0.5 as eln 0.5 and then use the power rule from section 3. You will not need a calculator until the last step.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the base. It is 0.5, which is below 1, so each step multiplies by less than one: decay. The exponent is t/10, so ten epochs is one halving, and the half-life is 10 epochs by construction.
  2. Set up (b) as a ratio. 0.0125 / 0.1 = 0.125, so you need the rate to have fallen to an eighth of its start.
  3. Recognise the eighth. 0.125 = 0.53, which is three halvings.
  4. Convert halvings to epochs. t/10 = 3, so t = 30 epochs.
  5. Rewrite the base for (c). 0.5t/10 = (eln 0.5)t/10 = e(t/10) ln 0.5, using the power rule.
  6. Fix the sign. ln 0.5 = −ln 2 by the quotient rule, so the exponent is −(t ln 2)/10.
  7. Read off k. k = ln(2)/10 = 0.0693 per epoch. Notice this is the same ln 2 / half-life shape as the worked example in section 6, met from the other direction.

Sanity check. k must be positive for a decay - a negative k in that form would be growth. And k times the half-life must come to ln 2 = 0.693: here 0.0693 × 10, which it does.

Your teacher. The four proofs in section 3 are deliberately identical, and that is the lesson rather than a shortcut. If you want to check you have it, do not re-read them - ask an agent to "give me log base 7 of 2401 and make me derive it from the definition, without a change-of-base formula". If you can answer "what exponent of 7 gives 2401", you have the whole page.

Primary source to go deeper

The NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions, section 4.8 states these identities in their general form, with the phase conditions that the positive-real case makes invisible. Section 4.2 of the same chapter defines ln as the area under 1/t, which is a second and quite different way to see why the product rule is true.
