Rotate, stretch, rotate, and nothing else

working 12 min The picture is a theorem here, not an analogy.

The one-minute version

  • Read UΣVT right to left as three motions: turn, stretch, turn.
  • Only the middle step changes any length, because the other two are rotations.
  • So the unit sphere always becomes an ellipsoid with semi-axes equal to the singular values.
  • σ1 is the most a unit vector can be stretched, and σmin the least.
  • Their ratio is the condition number, and it says how differently the matrix treats its best and worst directions.

1. Three motions, in order

VT acts first, in the input space, and it is a rotation. Then Σ scales each of the turned axes by its singular value, and it may add or remove dimensions. Then U rotates the result into position in the output space.

The order matters, and so does noticing that the two rotations happen in different spaces. When the matrix is not square those spaces do not even have the same dimension, which is the concrete meaning of "two bases" from lesson 66.

2. The theorem behind the picture

The image of the sphere The image of the unit sphere under A is the ellipsoid whose axes point along u1, …, un and whose semi-axis lengths are σ1, …, σn.

Proof. Take any unit vector x. Because V is orthogonal it maps the unit sphere onto itself, so write x = Vy with y also a unit vector, and every unit y arises this way exactly once. Then

Ax = UΣVTVy = UΣy Read as: substituting x = Vy puts a VTV in the middle, which is the identity because V is orthogonal, so it disappears and only UΣy is left.

Write z = Σy, so zi = σiyi. Since U is orthogonal it preserves lengths and angles, so the shape traced by Ax is the shape traced by z, merely re-expressed in the u basis. And for every i with σi > 0 we have yi = zi/σi, so ∑yi2 = 1 becomes

∑i (zi / σi)2 = 1 Read as: the equation of an ellipsoid whose semi-axis along coordinate i is σi. Transporting it by U puts those axes along the left singular vectors.
The step that does the real work The substitution x = Vy at the very start. It is legitimate only because V is orthogonal and therefore maps the unit sphere onto the whole unit sphere, losing nothing and adding nothing. That single move converts a statement about all unit x into a statement in the coordinates where Σ happens to be diagonal. Everything after it is reading off an equation.
The honest boundary. When some singular value is zero the ellipsoid is degenerate: flat in those directions, an ellipse living inside a higher-dimensional space rather than a solid body. The equation simply drops those terms. For a tall matrix the image is likewise a lower-dimensional ellipsoid sitting inside a bigger space, which is not a defect and is exactly what a data matrix does.

3. The largest and smallest stretch

Because ‖Σy‖2 = ∑σi2yi2 is a weighted average of the squared singular values with weights summing to one, it can never exceed the largest of them and never fall below the smallest. So σ1 is the most any unit vector is stretched, attained at v1, and σmin is the least.

That makes σ1 a norm of the matrix, called the spectral norm, and it is what lesson 68 measures approximation error in.

4. The condition number

Module 06 owns what a large ratio does to gradient descent. What this page owns is where the number comes from: it is the shape of the ellipsoid, and nothing more.

5. Volume, and why the determinant is blind

Rotations preserve volume, so only Σ can change it. For a square matrix that gives |det(A)| = σ1σ2…σn. The absolute value is there because the determinant also carries orientation, which Σ cannot express.

This is why the determinant can be a poor summary. The shear in section 1 has determinant exactly 1, and so does the identity. They preserve area equally and distort space completely differently, and only the singular values can tell them apart.

6. Worked in full

  1. Take the matrix lesson 65 could not diagonalise. A = [[1, 0.8], [0, 1]], a shear.
  2. Recall the failure. Its only eigenvalue is 1, twice, with a single eigendirection (1, 0). The eigendecomposition says almost nothing about it.
  3. Form the Gram matrix. ATA = [[1, 0.8], [0.8, 1.64]].
  4. Diagonalise it. trace = 2.64, det = 1.64 - 0.64 = 1, so λ2 - 2.64λ + 1 = 0 with discriminant 6.9696 - 4 = 2.9696 and roots 2.1816 and 0.4584.
  5. Singular values. 1.4770 and 0.6770.
  6. Read the geometry. The unit circle becomes an ellipse with those semi-axes. The shear stretches its best direction by about 48 percent and squeezes its worst by about 32 percent, and those two directions are perpendicular, unlike the single eigendirection.
  7. Sanity check. det(A) = 1 and 1.4770 × 0.6770 = 1.0000, so area is preserved even though the shape is not. And 1.47702 + 0.67702 = 2.64, matching the sum of every squared entry 1 + 0.64 + 0 + 1.
  8. What changes if. Increase the shear to [[1, 5], [0, 1]]. The determinant is still 1 and the eigenvalues are still 1 twice, so both are blind to the change. The singular values move to about 5.1926 and 0.1926, a ratio near 27. The SVD sees a violent distortion where the other two see nothing at all.

7. Measured, not asserted

low, high = sampled_stretch(matrix, 200_000, seed=3)   # random unit vectors
assert low  >= singular.min() - 1e-9      # nothing shrinks more than sigma_min

Two hundred thousand random unit vectors, and not one escaped the bracket the singular values predict. The program then applies the three factors one at a time to a single vector and prints its length after each step: 1.000000000, 1.000000000, then a change, then no change again. Length moves only in the middle step, which is section 1 as an assertion rather than a picture.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A two-by-two matrix has determinant 1. What does that say about how it distorts the unit circle?
The determinant is the product of the singular values, so a value of 1 pins the area and nothing else. The shear on this page has determinant 1 and singular values 1.477 and 0.677. The first two options describe the special case where the singular values happen to be equal, and the third names one matrix among infinitely many with determinant 1.
In A equals U Sigma V transpose applied to a vector, which factor acts first and where?
Matrix products apply right to left to a vector, so V transpose goes first, and it acts on the input. The second option has the right idea about order but the wrong factor and the wrong space. The third states a value judgement rather than an order. The fourth states the false rule that causes this error.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Describe the image of the unit circle under each matrix below, then say what the determinant does and does not distinguish between them.

A = [[3, 0], [0, 1]]    and    B = [[0, -1], [1, 0]] Read as: A is diagonal with entries 3 and 1. B is a quarter-turn rotation, the matrix from lesson 60 that had no real eigenvector.
Hint

Both Gram matrices are easy: one is diagonal already and the other is the identity. Work out the singular values first and the shapes follow.

Show the worked solution
  1. Gram matrix of A. ATA = diag(9, 1), so the singular values are 3 and 1.
  2. Shape of A's image. An ellipse with semi-axes 3 and 1 along the coordinate axes.
  3. Gram matrix of B. BTB = I, so both singular values are 1.
  4. Shape of B's image. The unit circle itself, turned a quarter-turn. Nothing is stretched.
  5. What the determinant sees. det(A) = 3 and det(B) = 1, so here it does separate them, but only by area. It would not separate B from the shear on this page, which also has determinant 1 and is nothing like a rotation.

Sanity check. For each matrix the product of the singular values must equal the absolute determinant: 3 × 1 = 3 and 1 × 1 = 1. Both hold. And B having both singular values equal is exactly what it means for a map to be a rotation, which is the geometric statement of it having no real eigenvector to single out.

Your teacher. If the two rotations still feel like the same rotation, ask an agent to build a 2 by 3 matrix and print the shapes of U, Σ and V. Seeing that V is 3 by 3 and U is 2 by 2 makes it obvious that they cannot possibly be the same object.
