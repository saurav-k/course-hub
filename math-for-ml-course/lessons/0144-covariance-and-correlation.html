One number for how two things move together, and it only sees straight lines

working 13 min Zero correlation is a statement about lines, not about independence.

The one-minute version

  • Covariance is the average of the two centred values multiplied together, so each observation votes on whether the pair moves in step.
  • Centre both columns and covariance is their dot product over n minus one. That is the fact the covariance matrix is built on.
  • Correlation divides covariance by the two standard deviations, which removes the units and forces the answer into minus one to plus one.
  • Independence gives zero covariance. The converse is false, and this page carries a column pair that proves it.

1. The mental model: every observation casts a signed vote

Centre both columns by subtracting their means. Now each observation has two signed numbers: how far above or below average it sits on each variable. Multiply that pair together and you get a vote.

An observation above average on both gives a positive product. Below average on both also gives a positive product, because two negatives multiply. Above on one and below on the other gives a negative. Covariance is the average of those votes.

2. The mechanism, and the form that matters later

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X - μX)(Y - μY)] Read as: the covariance of X and Y is the expected value of the first variable's deviation from its own mean multiplied by the second variable's deviation from its own mean. The two subscripted mus are the two separate means. Setting Y equal to X gives back the variance, so variance is covariance with itself.

Expanding the bracket and using linearity gives the one-pass version, exactly as it did for variance.

Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y] Read as: the average of the product minus the product of the averages. It carries the same numerical warning as the variance version: subtracting two large nearly equal numbers loses digits.

The third form is the one to remember, because the whole of the next lesson is built on it. Write the two centred columns as vectors. Their sample covariance is their dot product, divided by n minus one.

Cov(X, Y) = (x⃗c · y⃗c) ÷ (n - 1) Read as: take the column of centred x values as one vector and the column of centred y values as another, form the ordinary dot product by multiplying element by element and adding, then divide by one less than the number of rows. Covariance is geometry wearing a statistics costume.

3. Correlation, and what the denominator buys

Covariance carries units. On the serving log, prompt and output tokens covary at 14,361.9 squared tokens, and queue depth and GPU utilisation at 27.3 of whatever those units multiply to. Those two numbers cannot be compared, and neither can be called large or small on its own.

r = Cov(X, Y) ÷ (σX σY) Read as: divide the covariance by the standard deviation of each variable in turn. Each division cancels one variable's units, so r is a pure number. It is called Pearson's correlation coefficient.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality forces r into the range minus one to plus one, with the endpoints reached only when one variable is an exact linear function of the other. That bound is what makes two different pairs of columns comparable.

4. Zero correlation is not independence

Independence implies zero covariance, because independence gives E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] and the one-pass form is then exactly zero. The converse fails, and it fails badly enough to matter in practice.

The serving log carries the counterexample as two real columns. log_resid is the standardised residual of latency once tier, work and congestion are removed. resid_energy is that residual squared, which is what a squared-error loss actually sums.

Selecting features by the size of r silently discards every predictor whose relationship is not a straight line. A U-shaped driver of churn, a threshold effect, a seasonal cycle: all of them can score near zero. The cheap defence is to plot the pair, and the cheaper one is to also score the absolute value or a rank correlation, which is what the figure above does in its right-hand column.

5. Worked example: two pairs from 40,000 requests

  1. Centre and multiply. For prompt and output tokens, the average of the centred products over 39,999 degrees of freedom is 14,361.909 squared tokens.
  2. Get the two spreads. The standard deviations are 292.372 and 79.164 tokens.
  3. Divide. 14,361.909 ÷ (292.372 × 79.164) = 14,361.909 ÷ 23,145.3 = 0.6205.
  4. Read it. A strong positive relationship: bigger prompts go with longer answers, which is what you would expect and now do not have to assume.
  5. Now the null pair. Latency against screen density has covariance -4.256.
  6. Is that small? You cannot tell from the number, because it is in millisecond-dots per inch.
  7. Divide again. -4.256 ÷ (163.461 × 45.062) = -0.0006.
  8. Now you can tell. Six ten-thousandths, which on 40,000 rows is indistinguishable from nothing. Only step 7 made steps 4 and 8 comparable.
code/0144-covariance-and-correlation.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
x = frame["prompt_tokens"].to_numpy(float)
y = frame["output_tokens"].to_numpy(float)
n = len(x)

xc, yc = x - x.mean(), y - y.mean()
print("by definition   ", (xc * yc).sum() / (n - 1))
print("as a dot product", np.dot(xc, yc) / (n - 1))
print("correlation     ", np.dot(xc, yc) / (n - 1) / (x.std(ddof=1) * y.std(ddof=1)))

# Exactly dependent, and still uncorrelated.
s = frame["log_resid"].to_numpy(float)
print("r(s, s^2)  ", np.corrcoef(s, s ** 2)[0, 1])
print("r(|s|, s^2)", np.corrcoef(np.abs(s), s ** 2)[0, 1])

The full program adds a table of skewness against each column's correlation with its own square, which shows the mechanism: for a symmetric column that correlation is the third central moment and lands on zero, and for a skewed column it reaches 0.65.

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A feature has correlation 0.00 with your target. What follows?
Zero correlation rules out linear co-movement and nothing else. The first two options are the same misconception in two costumes, and the residual column on this page is a live counterexample: it determines its own square exactly and still scores 0.02. The fourth would force a zero standard deviation, which makes correlation undefined rather than zero.
Two column pairs have covariances of 14,361.9 and 27.3. Which pair moves together more?
Covariance carries the product of the two variables' units, so the raw sizes are not comparable. Here the correlations are 0.62 and 0.56, much closer than the covariances suggest. The first option is the trap and it is the reason correlation exists. The fourth overcorrects: covariance is perfectly interpretable once you fix the units, which is what dividing by the two standard deviations does.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Five users, with session minutes X = 15, 21, 25, 31, 38 and spend Y = 600, 800, 1100, 1250, 2000. Compute the covariance and the correlation by hand. Then say what you would need to check before reporting the correlation to a stakeholder.

Hint

Centre both columns first and confirm each centred column sums to zero, which is a free check on your arithmetic. Use the n minus one divisor throughout so the covariance and the two standard deviations are consistent with each other.

Show the worked solution
  1. The two means. X sums to 130, so x̄ = 26. Y sums to 5,750, so ȳ = 1,150.
  2. Centre X. -11, -5, -1, 5, 12. They sum to 0, so the centring is right.
  3. Centre Y. -550, -350, -50, 100, 850. These also sum to 0.
  4. Multiply pairwise. 6,050, 1,750, 50, 500, 10,200. Every one is positive, so no user disagrees with the trend.
  5. Sum and divide. The products total 18,550, and 18,550 ÷ 4 = 4,637.5, the covariance.
  6. The two spreads. Squared centred X sums to 316, so σX = √(316/4) = 8.888. Squared centred Y sums to 1,160,000, so σY = √(1,160,000/4) = 538.516.
  7. Divide. 4,637.5 ÷ (8.888 × 538.516) = 4,637.5 ÷ 4,786.4 = 0.9689.
  8. What to check before reporting. Plot it. With five points a single outlier can manufacture a correlation this high, and r alone cannot tell a straight line from one leveraged point. Also say out loud that this is association, not evidence that longer sessions cause spending.

Sanity check. both centred columns must sum to exactly zero, and r must land in minus one to plus one. If your r exceeds one, you have mixed divisors, using n for the covariance and n minus one for the standard deviations or the other way round. The divisor cancels in r, so it only has to be the same one everywhere.

Your teacher. Take the correlation matrix your team already computes and pick the pair closest to zero. Plot it. If the cloud has any shape at all, you have just found a feature that automated selection would have thrown away, and you now know why.

Primary source to go deeper

Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning (MIT Press, 2016), chapter 3, section 3.8. It defines covariance, states plainly that independence is a stronger requirement than zero covariance, and gives its own counterexample built from a uniform variable and a random sign.
