Convexity, and the one guarantee it buys

working 11 min The definition is in Boyd & Vandenberghe, sections 2.1.4 and 3.1.1. · Convex Optimization

The one-minute version

  • A set is convex when the segment between any two of its points stays inside. A function is convex when the chord lies above the graph.
  • Convexity buys exactly one thing: every local minimum is also global. That is the whole prize, and it has a half-page proof.
  • It does not buy speed, and it does not buy a unique answer. Lesson 103 is a whole page about a convex problem being slow.
  • Least squares and logistic regression are convex. Anything with a hidden layer is not, and that is the difference between solving and training.

1. A convex set: the segment stays inside

Pick any two points in the set. Draw the straight line between them. If that line never leaves the set, and this holds for every pair you could have picked, the set is convex.

That is the whole definition, and it is worth noticing what it does not mention: no curvature, no smoothness, no formula. A square is convex and has flat sides and sharp corners.

2. A convex function: the chord lies above

Now the same idea one level up. Take two points on the horizontal axis, look at the function's value at each, and draw the straight line between those two heights. If the function itself never rises above that line, anywhere between them, it is convex.

f( t x + (1 − t) y )  ≤  t f(x) + (1 − t) f(y)    for all x, y and all 0 ≤ t ≤ 1 x, y: any two points in the function's domain. t: a mixing weight between 0 and 1, so t x + (1 − t) y is a point somewhere on the segment joining them, at t = 1 it is x and at t = 0 it is y. The left side: the function's actual value at that intermediate point. The right side: the height of the straight chord above that same point. ≤: the function sits at or below its own chord, everywhere, for every pair.

3. The guarantee, and its proof

Here is what all of that buys. It is one sentence, and the proof is short enough to read.

The theorem In a convex optimization problem, every locally optimal point is globally optimal.

The shape of the argument, before the steps: suppose you are at a local minimum and somebody claims there is a strictly better point somewhere far away. Convexity lets you walk a short distance from where you are towards that better point, and the value you find is already lower than where you started. But you were at a local minimum, so nothing nearby can be lower. The far-away point cannot exist.

  1. Set up what "local" means. Say x is locally optimal: it is feasible, and it is the best point within some radius R of itself.
  2. Assume the opposite of what we want. Suppose some feasible y has a strictly lower value, f(y) < f(x).
  3. Note that y must be far away. If it were within R it would contradict local optimality immediately, so the distance from x to y is greater than R.
  4. Step towards it, but only a little. Take z = (1 − t)x + ty with t = R / (2 × distance from x to y), a number strictly between 0 and 1.
  5. Check z is inside the ball. Its distance from x is t × that distance, which is exactly R/2, comfortably less than R.
  6. Check z is feasible. It sits on the segment between two feasible points, and the feasible set is convex, so it is in it. This is the step that does the real work, and it is the only place the convexity of the set is used.
  7. Apply the chord inequality. f(z) ≤ (1 − t) f(x) + t f(y), and since f(y) is strictly smaller and t > 0, the whole right side is strictly below f(x).
  8. Collect the contradiction. z is feasible, within R of x, and strictly better than x. That is impossible if x was locally optimal. So no such y exists, and x is globally optimal.

Sanity check. The proof never differentiates anything, which it should not: the absolute value function is convex and has no derivative at its minimum, and the theorem still applies to it.

4. The ledger: which machine learning problems are convex

ObjectiveConvex?Why
Least squaresyesa quadratic whose Hessian is XTX, always positive semi-definite
Logistic regressionyesBoyd & Vandenberghe 7.1.1 states it directly
Hinge loss plus L2yesa sum of convex functions is convex
Lasso, ridgeyesa convex loss plus a norm, and every norm is convex
k-meansnoa joint minimisation over assignments and centres
Matrix factorisationnothe parameters multiply each other, and the worked example below shows what that costs
Any net with a hidden layernosame reason, composed many times over

You can watch this from a terminal. Fit a logistic regression twice from different starts and get the same coefficients; fit k-means twice with different seeds and get different clusters. That is convexity, or its absence, showing through.

5. What convexity does not buy

Three things it is routinely credited with and does not provide.

Not speed. Lesson 103 measures a convex quadratic that gradient descent crawls across. Not a unique answer. Logistic regression on separable data is convex and has no minimiser at all: the weights run off to infinity, improving forever. Convexity promises any minimum you find is global, not that one exists. Not hopelessness on the other side. Lesson 108 dismantles the fear of non-convex landscapes with a counting argument and a measurement.

6. Worked: two kinds of evidence about one objective

Take the logistic objective from Lesson 100 on the credit dataset and ask whether it is convex, two independent ways.

  1. Route one: sample chords. Draw 5,000 random pairs of parameter vectors, take a random mixing weight for each, and test the chord inequality. Result: 0 violations out of 5,000.
  2. Route two: read the Hessian. Form the second-derivative matrix of the same objective and take its smallest eigenvalue. At θ = 0 it is 1.96 × 10-2; at a random θ it is 1.20 × 10-2. Both positive, so the matrix is positive definite at both points.
  3. Now the counterexample. Run the same 5,000 chords on f(a, b) = (ab − 1)2, the shape of every matrix-factorisation objective. Result: 1,155 violations, worst gap 27.3.
  4. Pin it with one pair by hand. f(1, 1) = 0 and f(−1, −1) = 0, so the chord between them sits at height 0. Their midpoint is (0, 0), and f(0, 0) = 1.
  5. Read the failure. The curve is a full unit above its own chord at the midpoint. One pair settles it.
The two routes are not the same kind of evidence, and this is the transferable lesson. Sampling can only ever fail to find a counterexample, and no number of clean samples upgrades that into a proof. A positive semi-definite Hessian at a point is a proof of convexity at that point. When you check something numerically, know which of the two you are holding.

Sanity check. The clean sampling run and the positive eigenvalues have to agree, and they do. If sampling had found a violation while the Hessian stayed positive definite, one of the two would have a bug, and finding out which would be the next job.

What changes if the data changes? Nothing about convexity. The Hessian of the logistic objective is XT diag(p(1−p)) X, which is positive semi-definite for any data at all, because every weight p(1−p) is non-negative. Convexity here is structural, not a property of this dataset.

What it means. A convex objective is a promise your library can rely on. LogisticRegression can stop when the gradient is small and report that it has finished, because for a convex problem a vanishing gradient is the answer. No training loop for a deep network can honestly say that.

code/0101-convexity-and-the-one-guarantee-it-buys.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def chord_test(evaluate, sampler, trials, rng):
    """Count chord violations. Returns (violations, worst gap seen)."""
    violations = 0
    worst = 0.0
    for _ in range(trials):
        left, right = sampler(rng), sampler(rng)
        weight = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0)
        midpoint = weight * left + (1.0 - weight) * right
        chord = weight * evaluate(left) + (1.0 - weight) * evaluate(right)
        gap = evaluate(midpoint) - chord          # convex means gap <= 0
        if not np.isfinite(gap):
            raise FloatingPointError(
                "non-finite chord gap - the objective overflowed, so this "
                "test would report success without having tested anything")
        if gap > 1e-9:
            violations += 1
            worst = max(worst, gap)
    return violations, worst

The guard on line 12 is not decoration. Written the obvious way, the logistic loss overflows for the large random parameter vectors this test samples, log(0) becomes negative infinity, and the resulting nan compares False against every threshold, so the test reports zero violations without having compared anything. The full program uses a stable form of the loss and keeps the guard anyway.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What exactly does convexity guarantee about a minimisation problem?
Local equals global is the whole prize. The first fails in both directions: least squares is convex and has a closed form, logistic regression is convex and has none. The second is what Lesson 103 disproves on a convex problem. The third fails for logistic regression on separable data, which is convex and has no minimiser at all, so convexity cannot be promising uniqueness.
A set is convex when which of these holds?
The definition is about segments and nothing else. The first fails on a square, which has flat sides and sharp corners and is convex. The third names a special case, since a half-plane is convex and is described by a linear inequality. The fourth is the term collision this page exists to break: a convex set and a convex function are different objects, and you can define a wildly non-convex function on a perfectly convex set.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Decide convexity for each of these four, and justify each with the cheapest correct argument you can find:

f(w) = w4     g(w) = |w|     h(w) = w3     k(a, b) = (ab − 1)2 Read as: three functions of a single real number w, and one function of two real numbers a and b. The last one is the shape of a matrix-factorisation objective, where two sets of parameters multiply each other.
Hint

Two of these have a second derivative everywhere and one does not. For the one that does not, go back to the chord definition rather than reaching for calculus.

Hint for the last one

For a function of two variables you can settle non-convexity with a single pair of points and their midpoint. That is far quicker than any Hessian, and it is a complete argument.

Show the worked solution
  1. f(w) = w4 is convex. f''(w) = 12w2, which is non-negative for every w. A non-negative second derivative on an interval is exactly convexity there.
  2. g(w) = |w| is convex, and the second derivative is no help because it does not exist at 0. Use the chord instead: |t x + (1 − t) y| ≤ t|x| + (1 − t)|y| is the triangle inequality, which holds for every pair and every t.
  3. h(w) = w3 is not convex. h''(w) = 6w, which is negative for every w < 0. One point with negative second derivative is enough.
  4. k(a, b) is not convex. Take (1, 1) and (−1, −1). Both give k = (1 − 1)2 = 0, so the chord between them sits at 0.
  5. Evaluate the midpoint. The midpoint is (0, 0), and k(0, 0) = (0 − 1)2 = 1.
  6. Compare. 1 is above 0, so the function rises above its own chord and the test fails.
  7. Name what you proved. Every matrix-factorisation model has this shape, so you have just shown that class of models is non-convex without computing a single derivative.

Sanity check. Your counterexample for the last one must give a midpoint value strictly greater than the average of the two endpoint values. If it comes out exactly equal you have landed on an affine segment of the function and proved nothing at all, so pick a different pair. Also note that w4 being convex while w3 is not tells you convexity is not about the size of the exponent.

Your teacher. If the chord definition still feels abstract, ask an agent: "draw f(x) = x squared and f(x) = x cubed on the same axes from -2 to 2, and draw the chord from -1 to 1 on each". The picture makes the asymmetry obvious in a way the algebra does not. If the two-routes distinction is the part worth keeping, it generalises far beyond this page: any time you check a mathematical claim by sampling, you are looking for counterexamples rather than establishing truth.
