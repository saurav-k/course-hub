Stochastic gradient descent: paying in noise to buy iterations

working 12 min Bottou, Curtis & Nocedal, Optimization Methods for Large-Scale Machine Learning. · arXiv:1606.04838

The one-minute version

  • A minibatch gradient is unbiased and noisy, which are different things. Its spread falls as 1/√m, so 100 times the work buys 10 times less noise.
  • The right comparison is total work. Full-batch descent costs work proportional to n log(1/ε); SGD costs 1/ε, and neither the cost per step nor that bound depends on n.
  • With a fixed step size SGD does not reach the minimum. It settles into a band whose width is proportional to the step, and stops.
  • Changing the batch size has already changed your learning rate. They are one knob.

1. A sum can be sampled

The objective from Lesson 100 is an average over rows. Its gradient is therefore also an average over rows, one gradient contribution per training example. And an average over twenty thousand things can be estimated from thirty-two of them.

Pick a minibatch at random, average its gradient contributions, and step along that. The estimate is unbiased: averaged over all the minibatches you could have drawn, it equals the full gradient exactly. It is also noisy: any particular draw points somewhere slightly different.

Unbiased and noisy are not the same as wrong. A minibatch gradient is not an approximation that degrades the answer. It is a random variable whose mean is the right answer, and the whole page is about what that costs and what it buys. The proof that the spread of an average falls as 1/√m belongs to Module 08, which owns expectation and variance; this page uses the result and draws it.

2. So compare total work, not accuracy per step

Full-batch gradient descent converges quickly in steps, but each step reads every row. Stochastic gradient descent converges slowly in steps, and each step reads thirty-two. The honest comparison multiplies the two.

The reframing Neither the cost of an SGD step nor its convergence bound depends on how much data you have. That independence from n is why training on ten million rows is possible at all, and it is a stronger claim than "SGD is faster".

3. The bill: a fixed step never arrives

Here is what the noise costs, and it is the most consequential fact on this page.

E[ J(θk) ] − J⋆  →  αLM / (2cμ)    as k grows E[·]: the expected value, averaging over the randomness in which minibatches were drawn. J⋆: the best achievable objective value. α: the fixed step size. L: the smoothness constant from Lesson 102. M: a bound on the gradient noise, and the term that does not vanish as the gradient does. c: the strong-convexity constant. μ: a constant relating the stochastic direction to the true gradient. The whole right side: a fixed positive number that the gap approaches and does not fall below.

The gap does not go to zero. It goes to a number proportional to the step size α. Halve the step and you halve the floor, and also slow the approach to it.

Why, in three moves

  1. One step, with the noise term visible. Smoothness plus the bounds on the stochastic direction give a per-step inequality whose first term is the progress the step buys and whose second is what the noise charges. The noise term contains a constant M that does not shrink as the gradient does. That is the step that does the real work, and everything else follows from it.
  2. Turn the gradient into the gap. Strong convexity gives ‖∇J‖2 ≥ 2c(J − J⋆), so the inequality becomes dk+1 ≤ (1 − αcμ) dk + α2LM/2, an affine recursion in the gap alone.
  3. Solve it. A recursion dk+1 ≤ r dk + q with 0 < r < 1 approaches the fixed point q/(1 − r). Substituting r and q gives αLM/(2cμ), which is the stated limit.

Notice what the fixed point is made of: the step size on top, and the noise level with it. Turn off the noise, M = 0, and the floor is zero and you recover the full-batch behaviour exactly.

4. The fix, and its exact condition

Shrink the step as you go. Not any shrinking will do, and the condition is precise:

∑k αk = ∞    and    ∑k αk2 < ∞ αk: the step size used at iteration k, now a sequence rather than a constant. The first sum diverging: the steps must still add up to an infinite total distance, or you could stop short of the answer and never reach it. The second sum converging: the steps must shrink fast enough that the accumulated noise stays finite. Both at once: shrink, but not too quickly.

The schedule αk = β/(γ + k) satisfies both and gives an O(1/k) rate. A 1/k2 schedule fails the first condition and can stall short of the answer, which is why the two-sided requirement is the useful form to remember rather than "decay the learning rate".

5. Worked: batch size and learning rate are one knob

Every number here is quoted from Goyal et al., who trained ResNet-50 on ImageNet at a minibatch of 8,192 across 256 GPUs in one hour.

  1. The baseline. 8 GPUs, 32 images each, so minibatch 256, at η = 0.1. Top-1 validation error 23.60% ± 0.12.
  2. The scale-up. Minibatch 8,192 is 8192 ÷ 256 = 32 times larger.
  3. Apply the linear scaling rule. Multiply the learning rate by the same 32: 0.1 × 32 = 3.2. That matches the paper's own table.
  4. Run it with no warmup. 24.84% ± 0.37. Scaling the batch alone cost 1.24 points.
  5. Run it with a constant warmup. 25.88% ± 0.56. That is worse than doing nothing at all, by a further point.
  6. Run it with a gradual warmup. 23.74% ± 0.09, which is inside the run-to-run spread of the baseline.

Sanity check. The rule's justification assumes the gradient is roughly unchanged across the steps being merged into one. That assumption is worst exactly when the network is changing fastest, which is the start of training, and that is precisely where warmup is applied. The mechanism and the fix line up.

What changes if you scale further? The paper reports that accuracy holds across a wide range and then degrades, with the turn around 8,192 on this task. The rule is empirical and has a ceiling, and the paper says so rather than extrapolating.

What it means. If you move a recipe from one GPU to sixteen and keep the learning rate, you have quietly divided your effective step size by sixteen. The run will not crash. It will just be worse, in a way that looks like a data problem.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

With a fixed step size, where does SGD end up on a strongly convex objective?
It converges linearly to a neighbourhood whose width is proportional to the step, and then stops improving. The first needs a decaying step, which is the fix rather than the default. The second is impossible here: a strongly convex objective has exactly one minimum, so there is no other one to find. The fourth is false, because the noise is bounded and the contraction still dominates far from the optimum.
Growing the minibatch from 100 to 10,000 costs 100 times the compute. What does it buy?
Standard error falls as 1/√m, and √100 = 10. The first reads the scaling as linear, which is the most common error here and the reason large batches look better value than they are. The third forgets that the full dataset is itself a sample, so even m = n leaves error against the true risk. The fourth is not any power of the batch ratio.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Your working recipe is a minibatch of 256 at a learning rate of 0.1 on one GPU. You move it to sixteen GPUs, each processing 32 images per step.

Find the new total batch size and the learning rate the linear scaling rule prescribes. Then say whether warmup is needed and what you should expect if you skip it.

Hint

Work out the new total batch before touching the learning rate, and express the change as a single multiplier. The rule applies to the total batch across all workers, not to the per-worker batch, and that is the distinction the problem is built around.

Hint for the last part

Ask what the rule assumes about the gradient across the steps it is merging, and then ask at what point in training that assumption is least true.

Show the worked solution
  1. New total batch. 16 workers × 32 images = 512.
  2. The multiplier. 512 ÷ 256 = 2.
  3. New learning rate. 0.1 × 2 = 0.2.
  4. Is warmup needed? Yes. The rule's justification assumes the gradient is roughly constant across the merged steps, and that fails while the network is changing fastest, at the start.
  5. What skipping it costs. At a thirty-two-fold scale-up the measured cost was 24.84% against a 23.60% baseline, closing to 23.74% with gradual warmup. At a two-fold scale-up the effect is smaller but the same in kind.
  6. The trap. A constant warmup measured 25.88%, worse than no warmup at all. "Some warmup" is not the lesson; a gradual ramp is.

Sanity check. The multiplier from the old batch to the new must be exactly the multiplier from the old learning rate to the new: both must be 2. If your two multipliers differ, you have applied the rule to the per-worker batch of 32 rather than the total of 512, which would give a factor of 8 and a learning rate four times too large.

Your teacher. If the noise floor is the part worth keeping, try it: take any training script with a constant learning rate, let it run well past the point where the loss looks flat, and plot the last few hundred steps on their own axis. The jitter you see is the band, and its width is set by a number you chose. If the 1/√m argument feels like it needs a proof rather than a picture, that proof is in Module 08.
