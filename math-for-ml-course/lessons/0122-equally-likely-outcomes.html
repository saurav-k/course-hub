Counting outcomes and dividing is a model, and it is often the wrong one

foundation 9 min Where symmetry is real, where it is assumed, and what assuming it costs. · Hajek, ECE 313, sections 1.3 and 1.4

The one-minute version

  • The formula everyone learns first, count the outcomes and divide, has an assumption buried in it.
  • The assumption is that the outcomes are equally likely, and that is an input to the model, not a fact about the world.
  • It is genuinely true when a mechanism forces symmetry: a shuffle, a fair device, a hash, a random draw.
  • On this module's own data, assuming it is wrong by 79 percent on the most common outcome.
  • "I have no information, so I will assume uniform" is a claim about the world, and it can be false.

1. The formula, and the clause people skip

P(A) = |A| ÷ |Ω|    when every outcome in Ω is equally likely |A|: the number of outcomes in the event A. |Ω|: the number of outcomes in the whole sample space. The vertical bars mean "how many things are in this set". The clause after the comma is a condition, not decoration: without it the formula is simply false.

Read the clause aloud. It is not something you derive and it is not something the data hands you. It is a claim you are making, and the rest of this page is about when you are entitled to make it.

2. When the symmetry is real

Sometimes a mechanism forces the outcomes to be interchangeable, and then the assumption is not an assumption at all, it is a description.

  • A fair die. Six faces, no physical reason for any to be favoured.
  • A shuffled deck. The shuffle is what makes the orders interchangeable.
  • A hash bucket. A good hash function is built so that keys spread evenly.
  • A random draw from a dataset. When you call a seeded random-number generator to pick one row of 25,000, the generator is what makes those 25,000 rows equally likely.

The last one matters most here, because it is the one you write yourself. Notice what the symmetry is a property of: it is a property of the drawing, not of the things drawn. Hold on to that distinction, because the next section is entirely built on it.

3. Where it goes wrong, on this module's data

Every request in the log went to one of three routes. So Ω = {chat, embed, rerank}, three outcomes, and the temptation is immediate: three outcomes, so a third each.

And yet a random draw from that log is perfectly uniform. Both things are true at once, and holding them together is the whole skill this page teaches.

4. Worked: the same file, both answers

Draw one request at random from the 25,000. Here the outcomes really are equally likely, because the draw makes them so.

  1. Count the matching rows. 2,490 of the 25,000 requests went to rerank.
  2. Divide. 2,490 ÷ 25,000 = 0.0996. This is correct, and it is correct because of the draw.
  3. Now make the other assumption. Suppose you had never seen the file and reasoned "three routes, so a third each". You would answer 0.3333.
  4. Compare. 0.3333 ÷ 0.0996 = 3.35. The assumption overstates rerank traffic by a factor of three and a third.
  5. Check where the two models differ. Both satisfy every axiom from Lesson 121. Both sum to 1. Both give non-negative numbers. The axioms cannot tell you which one is right, and that is exactly why choosing a model is a separate skill from doing probability.

Sanity check. Under either model the three route probabilities sum to 1. That is the check people run, it passes, and it catches nothing - which is the point worth remembering.

What changes if you draw two requests without replacement. P(both rerank) becomes (2,490 ÷ 25,000) × (2,489 ÷ 24,999) = 0.009917, rather than 0.0996 squared = 0.009920. The first draw changed what was left, so the second is no longer independent of it. The gap is 0.0000036 here because 25,000 is large, and it is not zero. That gap is the whole difference between the binomial distribution in Lesson 129 and the hypergeometric, and it is why sampling a minibatch without replacement quietly breaks an assumption Lesson 129 will need.

5. The trap: uniform is not the absence of an assumption

The reframing "I have no information, so I will assume every outcome is equally likely" describes the modeller. Whether the outcomes actually are equally likely describes the world. Those are different claims, and only the second one can be wrong in a way that costs you.

This matters more than it looks, because the uniform assumption tends to arrive silently. It is the default in a prior, in a random baseline, in a tie-break, and in the sentence "assume nothing about the input distribution". Each of those is a real modelling choice that someone made on your behalf.

There is a good version of this instinct, and it is a theorem rather than a shrug: of all distributions on a finite set, the uniform one carries the most uncertainty, so it is the honest choice when you genuinely know only the list of outcomes. That is maximum entropy, and Module 10 states it properly. Until then, treat "assume uniform" as a choice you write down and defend, not as a way of avoiding one.

6. Both halves, at scale

The program computes the rerank probability from the definition and from pandas, asserts they agree, then draws 200,000 actual random rows and confirms the empirical frequency lands on the same number. Then it prints the assumed-uniform answer beside the true one for all three routes.

code/0122-equally-likely-outcomes.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def probability_by_counting(frame, route):
    """|A| / |Omega|, written out. Count the matching rows, divide by the total."""
    matching = int((frame["route"] == route).sum())
    return matching / len(frame)


picks = rng.integers(0, len(frame), size=200_000)
empirical = float((frame["route"].to_numpy()[picks] == "rerank").mean())
# counted 0.099600, pandas 0.099600, 200,000 real draws 0.098570

The 200,000 draws land on 0.098570 rather than exactly 0.0996, and that gap is not an error. It is what a finite sample looks like, and measuring how big it should be is what Module 09 does.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A colleague says "we know nothing about which route a request takes, so treat the three as equally likely". What is wrong with that?
Uniform is a statement about the world and the log contradicts it: 0.5972 against 0.0996. The first option is the misconception itself, and it is seductive because uniform feels like refusing to assume. The second is false - a request takes exactly one route, so they are mutually exclusive, and that is what lets their probabilities add. The fourth is invented: the formula has no minimum number of outcomes.
Drawing a minibatch without replacement makes the individual draws what?
Removing a row changes the pool the next draw sees, so the draws are dependent. The third option is true and answers a different question: each individual draw is uniform, and it is the relationship between draws that changed. The second is the mistake, and it is the assumption the binomial needs. The fourth confuses identically distributed with independent, which are the two separate halves of i.i.d.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A hash function spreads the 25,000 requests over 8 shards.

Find the probability a given request lands in shard 3, and the expected number of requests there, assuming the hash spreads them uniformly. Then name one realistic thing that would break the assumption.

Hint

The first two are one division and one multiplication. For the third, do not think about the hash function - think about what it is being computed from.

Show the worked solution
  1. Apply the formula. Eight equally likely shards, so P(shard 3) = 1 ÷ 8 = 0.125.
  2. Scale up. 25,000 × 0.125 = 3,125 requests expected in each shard.
  3. Break it. Hash on route instead of on the request id. There are only three distinct keys, so at most three shards get anything at all, and the chat key alone carries 0.5972 of the traffic into a single shard.

Sanity check. The eight shard probabilities must sum to 1, and 8 × 3,125 must come back to 25,000. Both hold. Note that the uniformity here is a property of the hash, exactly as the uniformity in section 2 was a property of the draw - never of the requests.

Your teacher. The habit from this page is a single question: what makes these outcomes equally likely? If you can name the mechanism - a shuffle, a seeded draw, a good hash - the formula is safe. If the honest answer is "nothing, it just felt neutral", you have found an assumption worth writing down. Ask an agent for five everyday probability claims that quietly assume uniformity, and try to name the mechanism for each.
