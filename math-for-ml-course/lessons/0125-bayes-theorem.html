Bayes' theorem is two lines of algebra, and it reverses the only bar that matters

working 11 min The derivation, the law of total probability underneath it, and the odds form. · Hajek, ECE 313, section 2.10

The one-minute version

  • You can measure how a detector behaves given abuse. You want to know whether this request is abuse. Those are opposite conditionals.
  • Bayes turns one into the other, and it is two lines from the definition in Lesson 123. There is nothing new to believe.
  • Its denominator is not a mystery constant: it is the law of total probability, summing over a partition.
  • The odds form is one multiplication and no denominator: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.
  • Precision is a posterior. That single sentence is why this page sits in a maths-for-machine-learning course.

1. The asymmetry, with numbers

From Lesson 123, on the module's request log:

  • P(flagged | unverified) = 0.010694
  • P(unverified | flagged) = 0.8081

Same two events. A factor of 75 between them. If you can feel why one of those is 1 percent and the other is 81 percent, you already understand the page; the rest is how to get from one to the other without counting.

Swapping these two silently has a name, the confusion of the inverse, and it is not a beginner's error. It appears in court judgments, in medical guidance and in model evaluation reports. The reason it survives is that both numbers are correct, both are about the same two events, and nothing about the notation warns you which one you are holding.

2. The derivation

The joint probability does not care which event you condition on. Write it both ways and set them equal.

  1. Condition on B. From Lesson 123, P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B).
  2. Condition on A instead. The same joint is P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) × P(A).
  3. Set them equal and divide by P(B). P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) ÷ P(B).
P(E | A) = P(A | E) × P(E) ÷ P(A) P(E): the prior, what you believed about E before the evidence. P(A | E): the likelihood, how probable the evidence is when E holds. P(A): the evidence, how probable the evidence is at all. P(E | A): the posterior, what you believe about E after seeing A. Those four names are used in every paper that touches this and are almost never defined.
The step that does the real work is step 2, writing the same joint a second way. That symmetry is the entire content of the theorem, and everything else is division. It also means the theorem cannot be wrong: it is arithmetic. Every disagreement about a Bayesian answer is a disagreement about the prior.

3. The denominator is the law of total probability

P(A) rarely arrives measured. It is assembled from the pieces you do have, by splitting the world into cases.

P(A) = P(A | E1)P(E1) + P(A | E2)P(E2) + … + P(A | Ek)P(Ek) The events E1 to Ek form a partition: mutually exclusive, and between them they cover everything. Each term is "how likely A is in this case, weighted by how likely the case is". For a yes-or-no question there are just two terms.

Why it holds

What is assumed: the cases are mutually exclusive and together they cover the whole sample space.

  1. Cut A along the partition. Every outcome in A lies in exactly one case, so A is the union of the pieces A∩E1, ..., A∩Ek, and those pieces are mutually exclusive.
  2. Add them with axiom P.2. P(A) = P(A∩E1) + … + P(A∩Ek).
  3. Rewrite each piece. The multiplication rule turns P(A∩Ei) into P(A | Ei)P(Ei).

The step that does the real work is that the pieces are disjoint, which is inherited from the partition and is the only reason axiom P.2 applies at all. Substituting this into the theorem gives the form you actually compute with.

4. The two-way slab: the whole theorem in one picture

5. The odds form, which needs no denominator

Divide the theorem for E by the theorem for not-E. The evidence term is the same in both and cancels.

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio odds: a probability rewritten as p ÷ (1 − p). Odds of 4 to 1 mean a probability of 0.8. likelihood ratio: how much more probable the evidence is under E than under not-E. Multiply, then convert back with p = odds ÷ (1 + odds).
  1. Prior odds. 0.29924 ÷ 0.70076 = 0.4270.
  2. Likelihood ratio. 0.010694 ÷ 0.001085 = 9.86. An unverified user is 9.86 times likelier to be flagged.
  3. Multiply. 0.4270 × 9.86 = 4.2105 posterior odds.
  4. Convert back. 4.2105 ÷ (1 + 4.2105) = 0.8081. The same answer, with no evidence term anywhere.

This is the form practitioners use, because independent pieces of evidence simply multiply their ratios: yesterday's posterior is today's prior, and the update is one multiplication.

6. Worked: three routes to one number

  1. Prior. 7,481 of 25,000 requests are unverified, so P(unverified) = 0.29924.
  2. Likelihoods. 80 of those 7,481 are flagged, so 0.010694. And 19 of the 17,519 verified are flagged, so 0.001085.
  3. Evidence, by total probability. 0.010694 × 0.29924 + 0.001085 × 0.70076 = 0.003200 + 0.000760 = 0.003960.
  4. Check it. Counting flagged rows directly gives 99 ÷ 25,000 = 0.003960. The partition closed.
  5. Posterior. 0.003200 ÷ 0.003960 = 0.8081.
  6. Third route. Count: 80 of the 99 flagged requests are unverified, so 80 ÷ 99 = 0.8081.

Sanity check. The evidence assembled from the two branches must equal the flag rate you can count without any theorem. It does, to six decimals. That check is free and it catches almost every arithmetic slip on this page.

What changes if verification is dropped, so everyone is unverified. The prior goes to 1, the posterior goes to 1, and the likelihood ratio becomes undefined because the other branch is empty. The column has stopped carrying information, and Bayes says so by degenerating. A feature with no variation is worth exactly nothing as evidence, and that is visible here as the slab losing one of its two columns.

7. Where this bites: precision is a posterior

Recall is P(flagged | abusive), which you measure on a labelled set. Precision is P(abusive | flagged), which is what an on-call engineer actually needs. Those are the two sides of the bar, so the bridge between them is this page.

code/0125-bayes-theorem.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def posterior_expanded(prior, likelihood, likelihood_other):
    """P(E|A) = P(A|E)P(E) / [ P(A|E)P(E) + P(A|not E)P(not E) ]."""
    evidence = likelihood * prior + likelihood_other * (1.0 - prior)
    return likelihood * prior / evidence


def posterior_from_odds(prior, likelihood, likelihood_other):
    """Posterior odds = prior odds x likelihood ratio. No evidence term at all."""
    posterior_odds = (prior / (1 - prior)) * (likelihood / likelihood_other)
    return posterior_odds / (1 + posterior_odds)


# 1. expanded 0.808081   2. odds 0.808081   3. counted 0.808081

The program runs the same three routes on the abuse detector. At threshold 0.5 it finds recall 0.9192 and a false-positive rate of 0.0116, and precision comes out at 0.2395 from Bayes and 0.2395 from counting: 91 of 380 flags are real. Lesson 126 is about that number.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A detector fires on 92 percent of abusive requests. One has just fired. How likely is it that this request is abusive?
92 percent is the likelihood, P(fires | abusive), and the question asks for P(abusive | fires). Reversing needs two more numbers: how common abuse is, and how often the detector fires on clean traffic. The first option is the confusion of the inverse in its purest form. The third is the same error with a hedge attached, which makes it worse rather than better. The second inverts a number that was never the answer.
In P(E | A) = P(A | E)P(E) / P(A), which term is the evidence?
The evidence is P(A), the probability of seeing what you saw at all, and it is computed by the law of total probability rather than measured. The first option is the likelihood, the third is the prior and the fourth is the posterior: all three are real terms with real names, which is exactly why the four are worth learning together rather than one at a time.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You have already applied the flag evidence, so the odds that a request is unverified now stand at 4.2105 to 1. A second signal fires, conditionally independent of the first, with a likelihood ratio of 3.0.

Find the new posterior odds and the new posterior probability. Then say why you did not need the evidence term at any point.

Hint

In odds form, conditionally independent evidence multiplies. Convert back at the very end, not in the middle.

Show the worked solution
  1. Multiply the ratios in. 4.2105 × 3.0 = 12.632 posterior odds.
  2. Convert to a probability. 12.632 ÷ (1 + 12.632) = 0.9266.
  3. Say why no denominator appeared. The evidence term is the same for E and for not-E, so it cancels when you divide the two forms of the theorem. Odds carry the ratio of the two branches, and that ratio never needed the total.

Sanity check. Evidence pointing the same way must raise the posterior, so the answer has to exceed the 0.8081 you started from, and it must stay below 1. It is 0.9266. As a second check, going back through the whole chain from the original prior gives 0.4270 × 9.86 × 3.0 = 12.632, the same odds, because multiplication does not care about the order.

Your teacher. The habit worth building is saying which conditional you have out loud before you use it. "This is the probability of the alarm given the fire" and "this is the probability of the fire given the alarm" are different sentences, and once you are in the habit of finishing the sentence you stop swapping them. Ask an agent for a confusion matrix and to name all four of prior, likelihood, evidence and posterior in it.
