Cross-entropy is the bill for using the wrong codebook

working 10 min Brown, Della Pietra, Della Pietra, Mercer and Lai, An Estimate of an Upper Bound for the Entropy of English (Computational Linguistics, 1992). · the paper

The one-minute version

  • Cross-entropy is what you pay, entropy is what you must pay, and the gap is your model's fault.
  • With a one-hot label the whole sum collapses to one term, which is why the loss looks so small in code.
  • Module 09 showed this is the negative log-likelihood. This page shows it is also a file size.
  • "Cross-entropy" is not only for classifiers. Mean squared error is one too, against a Gaussian model.
  • It is unbounded above. One confident wrong answer can outweigh a thousand right ones.

1. Two codebooks over one alphabet

Reality emits symbols at frequencies p. You do not know p. You built your code table from your model's beliefs q, giving each symbol a length equal to its surprise under q. Then you have to send real traffic through it.

Every symbol now costs −log q bits instead of the −log p it could have cost, and it arrives at rate p. Average the cost you actually pay, weighted by how often each symbol actually turns up, and that is the whole idea.

H(p, q) = −∑i pi log2 qi Read as: the cross-entropy of q against p is the sum, over every outcome i, of how often that outcome really happens, pi, times how many bits your code spends on it, −log2 qi. The weights come from reality and the lengths come from the model. Both arguments matter and their order is not interchangeable.

Notice which distribution appears where. p weights and q is inside the logarithm. Swap them and you have a different number, which is lesson 202's subject.

2. Why the loss in your code has one term

In supervised classification the label is not a distribution you estimated, it is a fact: this image is a cat. So p is one-hot, a 1 on the true class and 0 everywhere else.

Every term with pi = 0 vanishes. What survives is the single term where pi = 1, so the sum over a thousand classes collapses to the probability your model gave the one right answer.

H(p, q) = −log qy Read as: with a one-hot label, cross-entropy is minus the logarithm of qy, the probability the model assigned to the true class y. Nothing about the other classes enters directly, though they are all inside the normalisation that produced qy.
The reframing Module 09 derived this same expression as a negative log-likelihood, from maximum likelihood and nothing else. It arrived there as a statistical objective. It arrives here as a number of bits. They are the same quantity, and neither derivation needs the other.

3. The floor, and the theorem that puts it there

Cross-entropy has a lower bound, and the bound is the entropy of lesson 200. You cannot compress reality below its own entropy, no matter how clever the codebook.

The decomposition. Add and subtract log pi inside the sum:

  1. Start from the definition. H(p, q) = −∑ pi log2 qi.
  2. Add zero. Subtract and re-add ∑ pi log2 pi. Nothing has changed. This is the step that does the real work, and it does no work at all: the decomposition is bookkeeping, and all the meaning is in what the two pieces turn out to be.
  3. Collect the first piece. −∑ pi log2 pi is H(p), the entropy.
  4. Collect the second. ∑ pi log2(pi / qi) is the KL divergence from p to q, which lesson 202 proves is never negative.
H(p, q) = H(p) + DKL(p ‖ q)  ≥  H(p) Read as: what you pay equals the floor plus a non-negative penalty, so what you pay is at least the floor. The penalty is zero only when the model's distribution q agrees with reality's p exactly.

That inequality is what licenses the number in section 5 as an upper bound on the entropy of English rather than an estimate of it. Any model's measured cross-entropy is an honest ceiling, and a better model lowers the ceiling.

4. The misnomer

Most engineers meet "cross-entropy" as the name of the classification loss and infer that regression uses something else. That is backwards.

Deep Learning, section 5.5, is blunt about it: "Many authors use the term 'cross-entropy' to identify specifically the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli or softmax distribution, but that is a misnomer. Any loss consisting of a negative log-likelihood is a cross-entropy... For example, mean squared error is the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution and a Gaussian model."

So the family is one family. Pick a distribution for your target and the loss is not a choice any more, it is a consequence. Assume Gaussian noise and you get squared error. Assume Bernoulli and you get log loss. Assume a categorical and you get the softmax loss. Module 09 derives the first two; this page names them as one thing.

5. Worked: one prediction, then the entropy of English

Small first, so every step is checkable.

  1. Take a real output. A three-class model produces logits 2.0, 1.0 and 0.1.
  2. Turn them into probabilities. Lesson 204 does this properly; for now, exponentiate and normalise: q = 0.6590, 0.2424, 0.0986.
  3. Read off the label. The true class is the first, so p = 1, 0, 0.
  4. Collapse the sum. Two terms vanish. H(p, q) = −ln 0.6590 = 0.4170 nats, which is 0.6016 bits.
  5. Move the label and re-read. Same prediction, but suppose the truth were the third class: −ln 0.0986 = 2.3170 nats. The identical output now costs 5.6 times as much.
  6. Sanity check. The loss must be positive and it must be below ln 3 = 1.0986 nats if the model beat a blind guess over three classes. 0.4170 is comfortably under, so this prediction was worth something. Had it come out above 1.0986 the model would be worse than knowing nothing.
  7. What changes if the label is soft, say 0.8 and 0.2 and 0? The sum stops collapsing: −(0.8 ln 0.6590 + 0.2 ln 0.2424) = 0.3336 + 0.2836 = 0.6172 nats. Label smoothing is exactly this move, and it costs you something even when you are right, which is the point of it.
  8. Read it back in bits. 0.6016 bits is what it would cost to transmit this one label to someone who shared the model. That is not an analogy. It is what an arithmetic coder would spend.

Now the same quantity at the scale it was invented for. Brown and colleagues built a word-trigram model from 583 million words of training text and measured its cross-entropy against the 5.96-million-character Brown Corpus, over the 95 printable ASCII characters. They obtained 1.75 bits per character. A uniform model over 95 characters costs log2 95 = 6.57 bits, so the model captured about 73 per cent of the redundancy in printed English, and by section 3 that 1.75 is a ceiling on English's true entropy rather than a guess at it.

6. Where the loss actually comes from

Twenty thousand held-out predictions make the shape of the number visible in a way three cannot.

code/0181-cross-entropy.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
# route 1: the definition, all five classes
slow = -(np.where(onehot > 0, onehot * np.log(probs), 0.0)).sum(axis=1)

# route 2: the way a framework does it
fast = -logits[np.arange(n), truth] + log_sum_exp(logits)

assert np.abs(slow - fast).max() < 1e-9    # 3.6e-15 on 20,000 rows

Accuracy on those rows is 0.7924 and the mean loss is 0.7558 nats. Split by whether the prediction was right and the average is 0.2874 on the correct rows against 2.5435 on the wrong ones. The single worst row costs 26.2117 nats, eighty-eight times the median, and the worst one per cent of rows carry 10.4 per cent of the entire loss. That concentration is the practical form of the curve in section 2, and it is why gradient clipping and label smoothing exist.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A three-class model outputs 0.7, 0.2 and 0.1, and the true class is the second. What is the loss in nats?
The loss reads the probability given to the class that actually occurred, so it is −ln 0.2 = 1.609. The first option scores the argmax instead of the label, which is what accuracy does and cross-entropy does not. The third uses 1 minus the probability, which is a plausible error measure but is not a log and is bounded above by 1. The fourth averages all three terms, which is what you would do for a soft label of one third each, not for a one-hot label.
According to Deep Learning section 5.5, mean squared error is which of the following?
Any negative log-likelihood is a cross-entropy, and squared error is the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian, so it is one. The first option confuses the formula you write with the derivation behind it: the logarithm of a Gaussian density is where the square came from. The second invents a restriction that does not exist. The third confuses cross-entropy with the divergence, which is cross-entropy minus the entropy of the data and is a different number.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Two models score the same four-example test set. Model A gives the true class the probabilities 0.9, 0.9, 0.9 and 0.1. Model B gives 0.7, 0.7, 0.7 and 0.6.

Find the accuracy of each at a 0.5 threshold and the mean cross-entropy of each in nats. Then say which you would ship, and why the two metrics disagree about the reason.

Hint

Compute the loss on the fourth example alone, before averaging anything. Then look at what share of A's total it is.

Show the worked solution
  1. Accuracy. A is above 0.5 on three of four; B on four of four. B wins.
  2. Model A's loss. −ln 0.9 = 0.1054, three times, plus −ln 0.1 = 2.3026. Total 2.6188, mean 0.6547 nats.
  3. Model B's loss. −ln 0.7 = 0.3567, three times, plus −ln 0.6 = 0.5108. Total 1.5809, mean 0.3952 nats.
  4. Find where A's loss lives. That single fourth example contributes 2.3026 of A's 2.6188, which is 88 per cent of A's total loss on 25 per cent of the data.
  5. Ship B, and say why. Accuracy charges a flat 1 for A's error. Cross-entropy charges A for having been confident about it. The two metrics agree on the winner and disagree completely about the margin, and the margin is what tells you the failure mode.
  6. The caveat worth stating. If that fourth example is mislabelled in the test set, cross-entropy has just punished A severely for being right. Cross-entropy trusts labels more than accuracy does.

Sanity check. Both means must be positive and below ln 4 = 1.386, the loss of a model that spreads its probability uniformly over four options. 0.6547 and 0.3952 both clear it, so both models beat guessing. A mean above 1.386 would mean the arithmetic went wrong, or the model is genuinely worse than knowing nothing.

Your teacher. If section 4 did not land, ask an agent to write out the log of a Gaussian density with fixed variance, drop every term that does not contain the mean, and show what is left. The squared error falls out in three lines, and seeing it happen is worth more than being told it happens.
