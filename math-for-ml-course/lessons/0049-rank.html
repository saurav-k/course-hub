Rank: the number of directions a matrix really has

working 12 min Not how many rows. Not how many columns. How many independent directions, and the two counts always agree.

The one-minute version

  • Rank counts independent directions, and it can never exceed the smaller of the two dimensions.
  • Column rank equals row rank, always, and the factorisation A = CR is why.
  • Rank plus nullity equals the number of columns. What the matrix does not reach and what it destroys add up exactly.
  • Rank is a budget: an m by n matrix of rank r is r(m + n) numbers, not mn. That gap is what low-rank adaptation spends.

The count, and the smallest honest example

The rank of a matrix is the size of the largest linearly independent set among its columns. It cannot exceed the number of columns, and it cannot exceed the number of rows either, which is the first surprise: a 3 by 4 matrix has rank at most 3.

Take Strang's example, from the appendix of Introduction to Linear Algebra. The third column is 3 times the first plus 4 times the second, and the fourth is 5 times the first plus 6 times the second, so only two columns are doing any work.

Column rank equals row rank

What is assumed. A is any m by n matrix. Its column rank is the number of independent columns and its row rank the number of independent rows. Nothing is assumed about shape.

The shape of the argument. Factor A as CR, where C is m by r and R is r by n. Then read the same factorisation twice: once by columns and once by rows. Each reading bounds one of the two ranks by r, and together they force both to equal it.

Building the factorisation. Walk the columns of A from left to right and keep each one that is not a combination of those already kept. Call the kept columns C, and say there are r of them. Every discarded column was, by construction, a combination of kept ones, so every column of A is. Collect those recipes as the columns of a matrix R, and A = CR by construction.

The step that does the real work is reading that one equation in two directions.

  • By columns. Lesson 44 says each column of CR is a combination of the columns of C. So every column of A lies in the span of r vectors, and the column rank is at most r. It is also at least r, because the r columns of C are columns of A and were chosen independent. So the column rank is exactly r.
  • By rows. The same lesson says each row of CR is a combination of the rows of R. So every row of A lies in the span of the r rows of R, and the row rank is at most r.

That gives row rank ≤ r = column rank. Now run the whole argument again on AT, whose rows are A's columns and whose columns are A's rows. It yields column rank ≤ row rank. Two inequalities in opposite directions force equality. ■

Strang calls this "the first great theorem in linear algebra", and the factorisation is what makes it a short argument rather than a long one.

Rank-nullity: what is reached and what is destroyed

The null space of A is every vector the matrix sends to zero, and its dimension is called the nullity. Lesson 46's collapse is exactly a matrix with a non-trivial null space: a whole line of inputs was flattened onto the origin.

rank(A) + nullity(A) = n Read as: n is the number of columns of A, which is the dimension of the space the inputs live in. The rank counts the directions that survive to the output and the nullity counts the directions that are destroyed. Every input direction does exactly one of the two, so the counts add to n.

For Strang's matrix: four columns, rank 2, so the nullity must be 2. Building an explicit basis for the null space from the echelon form gives exactly two vectors, and applying A to each returns zero to the last bit. The count and the construction agree.

The same bookkeeping in the other direction gives the four fundamental subspaces. The column space and the left null space live where the outputs live and their dimensions add to m. The row space and the null space live where the inputs live and their dimensions add to n. For a 3 by 4 matrix of rank 2 that is 2 + 1 = 3 and 2 + 2 = 4.

Two things rank explains

Collinearity. Add a column to the sensor table that is vibration_x again in different units. The table now has nine feature columns, and its rank is still 8. Nothing was added, and something was broken: the condition number of the design matrix goes from 8.9 × 103 to 1.8 × 1017, and the coefficients stop being unique. Adding 1000 to one area coefficient and subtracting 1 from the other changes the residual by 8.5 × 10−14, which is nothing. Two completely different explanations, one identical fit.

A budget. A rank-r matrix of size m by n can be written as CR and stored as r(m + n) numbers instead of mn. For a 12,288 by 12,288 weight matrix that is 150,994,944 numbers at full rank against 98,304 at rank 4, which is 0.0651 per cent. Hu and colleagues spend exactly that gap: freezing the weights and learning a rank-4 correction to two matrices in all 96 layers of a 175-billion-parameter model gives 18,874,368 trainable parameters, and they report the checkpoint shrinking from 350GB to roughly 35MB.

Rank tells you how many numbers a given matrix needs. It does not tell you which rank-r matrix is the best approximation to one that is not low rank, and that is a different and harder question. Module 04 answers it with the singular value decomposition. Nothing on this page finds a best approximation, and the LoRA result above is about learning a low-rank correction, not about compressing an existing weight.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. A is Strang's 3 by 4 matrix with rows (1, 2, 11, 17), (3, 7, 37, 57) and (4, 9, 48, 74).
  2. Look for dependence. Row 1 plus row 2 equals row 3: 1+3 = 4, 2+7 = 9, 11+37 = 48, 17+57 = 74. So the three rows are dependent and the row rank is at most 2.
  3. Keep the independent columns. Column 1 is (1, 3, 4) and column 2 is (2, 7, 9); neither is a multiple of the other, so both are kept. That is C.
  4. Write the recipes. Column 3 = 3×column 1 + 4×column 2, and column 4 = 5×column 1 + 6×column 2. With the identity for the first two columns, R = [[1, 0, 3, 5], [0, 1, 4, 6]].
  5. Multiply back. CR reproduces A exactly, entry for entry, which confirms the factorisation rather than assuming it.
  6. Read both ranks off. The inner dimension is 2, so the column rank is 2 and the row rank is 2, matching step 2's ceiling.
  7. Count the null space. Four columns and rank 2, so the nullity must be 2. Building the basis from the echelon form gives two vectors, and A times each is the zero vector to the last bit.
  8. Say what would change. Change one entry of the last row so that row 1 plus row 2 no longer equals row 3, and the rank jumps to 3, the nullity falls to 1, and the fourth column is the only one still expressible in terms of the others.

Sanity check. The rank can never exceed 3 here, because the matrix has only three rows, no matter how many columns you add. If a calculation ever returns rank 4 for a 3 by 4 matrix, the calculation is wrong.

What it means. On a feature table the rank is the number of measurements that carry genuinely separate information. Eight sensors with rank 8 means every one is pulling its weight. Nine columns with rank 8 means one of them is decoration that will make the fit unstable, and lesson 51 shows what that instability looks like when you try to interpret the coefficients.

code/0049-rank.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1., 2., 11., 17.], [3., 7., 37., 57.], [4., 9., 48., 74.]])
C = A[:, :2]
R = np.array([[1., 0., 3., 5.], [0., 1., 4., 6.]])

assert np.allclose(C @ R, A)                                   # the factorisation holds
print(np.linalg.matrix_rank(A), np.linalg.matrix_rank(A.T))    # 2 2, and they always agree

The full program builds C and R by elimination rather than by hand, constructs a null-space basis from the echelon form without calling a decomposition routine, checks rank-nullity against that construction, prints all four subspace dimensions, and measures the collinearity failure on the sensor table.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A matrix is 3 by 4 and its third column is the sum of the first two. What is its rank?
Rank cannot exceed the smaller dimension, which is 3 here, and the stated dependency means it may be lower, which has to be checked rather than assumed. The first counts columns instead of independent ones. The third assumes the rows are independent, which was never said. The fourth confuses the number of dependencies with the rank itself.
You add a column holding a feature you already have, in different units. What happens?
A rescaled copy is a linear combination of a column you already had, so the span and the rank are unchanged, and the Gram matrix becomes singular so the coefficients are no longer unique. The first two both claim the rank rises, which a scalar multiple cannot do. The third gets the rank right and the consequence wrong: nothing improves, because no new information arrived.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A language model has d = 12,288 and 96 layers. A low-rank adapter replaces the update to a d by d weight with BA, where B is d by r and A is r by d.

  1. How many parameters are in one full d by d weight?
  2. How many are in BA at r = 4, and what fraction of the full matrix is that?
  3. Applying the adapter to two matrices in every layer at r = 4, how many trainable parameters in total?
  4. How large is that in half precision, and how does it compare with the paper's stated "roughly 35MB"?
Hint

Count B and A separately and add. Notice that both have d as one of their dimensions and r as the other, which is why the total is 2dr rather than anything more complicated.

Show the worked solution
  1. Count the full matrix. 12,288 × 12,288 = 150,994,944 parameters.
  2. Count the two factors. B has 12,288 × 4 = 49,152 entries and A has 4 × 12,288 = 49,152, so BA costs 2 × 12,288 × 4 = 98,304 parameters.
  3. Take the fraction. 98,304 ÷ 150,994,944 = 0.0651%, which is about one 1,536th.
  4. Scale to the model. Two matrices per layer across 96 layers is 192 adapted matrices: 192 × 98,304 = 18,874,368 parameters.
  5. Convert to bytes. Half precision is two bytes each, so 37,748,736 bytes, about 37.7 MB.
  6. Compare with the paper. The paper states an 18M budget and "roughly 35MB". The exact count is 18.87M and 37.7 MB, so the paper is rounding down, and the tenfold-thousand claim about the checkpoint is unaffected.

Sanity check. Step 4 should land near 19 million, which is around a ten-thousandth of a 175-billion-parameter model. If your answer came out in the billions you have multiplied by d somewhere instead of by r, which is the one slip this arithmetic invites.

Your teacher. If "column rank equals row rank" still feels like a coincidence, ask an agent to generate ten random matrices of assorted shapes, print the rank of each and of its transpose, and then print the shapes of C and R for each. Watching the same inner dimension appear on both sides ten times in a row is what turns the theorem from a claim into a fact you expect.

Primary source to go deeper

Strang, Introduction to Linear Algebra, sixth edition, the appendix "Elimination and Factorization A = CR". It is four pages, it is where this page's matrix and its factorisation come from, and it states the theorem in the same sentence used here: "this factorization brings with it a proof of the first great theorem in linear algebra: Column rank equals row rank".
