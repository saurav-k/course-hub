The theorem that makes "just measure it" legitimate

working 11 min Your test-set accuracy is a sample mean, and this is the theorem that says it is going somewhere.

The one-minute version

  • The sample mean converges to the true mean, and the proof is three lines from Chebyshev.
  • It says nothing about self-correction. A run of bad luck is diluted by later data, never repaid.
  • The strong law needs only a finite mean, which is weaker than the finite variance the short proof uses.
  • It fails when there is no mean to converge to. A Cauchy average is as spread out at a hundred thousand samples as at ten.

1. The mental model: the target stops moving because the spread shrinks

Lesson 143 showed that averaging n independent draws divides the variance by n. So the sample mean is a random variable whose spread collapses as n grows, while its centre stays fixed at the true mean. Squeeze the spread to nothing around a fixed centre and there is nowhere left for the average to be.

That is the whole idea, and it is why Chebyshev is enough to prove it: Chebyshev converts a shrinking variance directly into a shrinking probability of being far away.

2. The proof, in three lines

Theorem, the weak law Let X1, X2, ... be independent draws from a distribution with mean μ and finite variance σ2. For any ε > 0, the probability that the sample mean differs from μ by at least ε goes to zero as n grows.

Proof. Line one is a fact you already have.

Var(X̄n) = σ2 ÷ n Read as: the variance of the average of n independent draws is the single-draw variance divided by n. It came from adding variances and then scaling by one over n, in lesson 143.

Line two applies the previous lesson to that random variable. Chebyshev holds for any variable with a finite variance, and the sample mean is one, with mean μ.

P(|X̄n - μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(X̄n) ÷ ε2 = σ2 ÷ (nε2) Read as: Chebyshev in its threshold form, applied to the sample mean. The bound is the variance of the sample mean divided by the squared threshold, and substituting the first line gives sigma squared over n epsilon squared.

Line three is the step that does the work, and it is just reading. Hold ε fixed. Then σ2 and ε2 are constants and n is in the denominator, so the whole expression goes to zero as n grows. A probability squeezed below something going to zero goes to zero.

That proof used a finite variance, because Chebyshev needs one. The strong law is a better theorem: it needs only a finite mean, and it concludes that the sequence of averages itself converges rather than merely that a probability shrinks. Its proof is genuinely beyond this course, and it is worth naming that boundary rather than gesturing at it. What matters here is the direction: the condition is weaker than the one this page's proof assumed, so there are distributions where the average converges and this proof cannot see it.

3. What the law does not say

The most expensive misreading in probability is that a run of bad luck gets repaid. It does not. The draws are independent, so nothing about the past changes what comes next, and the law never claimed otherwise.

The confusion comes from conflating two quantities that move in opposite directions. Suppose ten heads in a row, then ten thousand more fair flips.

4. Where it fails outright

The law needs something to converge to. A Cauchy distribution has no mean, and the consequence is stark rather than subtle: the average of n Cauchy draws has exactly the same distribution as a single draw, for every n. Averaging buys nothing at all.

n = 10 → IQR 2.02    n = 1,000 → IQR 2.09    n = 100,000 → IQR 1.96 Read as: the interquartile range of the sample mean of a Cauchy, measured across two thousand repeats at each sample size. IQR is the width of the middle half of the outcomes. Across four orders of magnitude of n it does not shrink, while for a normal it would fall by a factor of one hundred.
This is not slow convergence, it is no convergence, and the difference matters in practice. Heavy-tailed quantities such as file sizes, revenue per customer and time-to-failure can have such thin margins on their moments that averages behave far more like the Cauchy column than like the normal one. If your running average of a metric is still jumping after a lot of data, the first thing to check is whether the metric has the moments you assumed.

5. Worked example: what your reported accuracy is worth

  1. Recognise the quantity. Test-set accuracy counts correct predictions and divides by n, so it is a sample mean of Bernoulli outcomes.
  2. Take the per-example variance. For a Bernoulli with success rate p it is p(1 - p). At p = 0.90 that is 0.09.
  3. Divide by n. On a 1,000-example test set the variance of the reported number is 0.09 ÷ 1,000 = 0.00009.
  4. Take the square root. The standard error is √0.00009 = 0.00949, so about 0.95 percentage points.
  5. Turn that into an interval. Roughly two standard errors either side gives about ±1.9 points.
  6. Now read a paper. A model reporting 91.2 percent against a baseline's 90.4 percent is claiming a 0.8-point gap with a 1.9-point error bar on each number.
  7. Sanity check. The error bar must shrink with the square root of the test-set size, so a 10,000-example set gives about ±0.6 points and a 100-example set about ±6.0. Those match the √10 steps between them.
  8. What changes if the test examples are not independent. Near-duplicate images or sentences from the same document introduce positive covariance, the variance rises above p(1-p)/n, and the effective test-set size is smaller than the row count. The error bar is then wider than this calculation says, not narrower.
code/0151-the-law-of-large-numbers.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
x = frame["latency_ms"].to_numpy(float)
mu, sd, eps = x.mean(), x.std(), 10.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(20260831)

for n in (10, 100, 1_000, 10_000):
    bound = min(1.0, sd ** 2 / (n * eps ** 2))       # the proof's own quantity
    means = np.array([rng.choice(x, n).mean() for _ in range(4_000)])
    print(n, round(bound, 6), round(float((np.abs(means - mu) >= eps).mean()), 6))

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A fair coin gives ten heads running. What does the law of large numbers say about the next ten flips?
The flips are independent, so nothing about the run changes what follows; the ten-head excess is divided by an ever-larger n rather than repaid. The first option is the gambler's fallacy stated in the law's own vocabulary, which is what makes it the strongest distractor. The fourth confuses a limit with a guarantee about any finite stretch.
You average 100,000 Cauchy draws instead of 10. What happens to the spread of the average?
The average of n Cauchy draws has the same distribution as one draw, so the measured spread is unchanged across four orders of magnitude. The second option is the trap: this is not slow convergence but its complete absence, and calling it slow suggests more data would eventually fix it. Trimming does help, but by changing the estimator to something that is no longer the mean.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You are deciding how large a held-out set needs to be. Your model's accuracy is somewhere near 95 percent, and you want the reported number to be within half a percentage point of the truth with high confidence.

Find the sample size using Chebyshev alone, without assuming any distribution, taking "high confidence" as at most a 5 percent chance of missing. Then say why the number is larger than a normal-based calculation would give, and which you would use.

Hint

Use the proof's own bound directly: the probability of missing by epsilon is at most the variance of the mean over epsilon squared. Set that to 0.05 and solve for n. The per-example variance is p(1-p).

Show the worked solution
  1. Per-example variance. p(1 - p) at p = 0.95 is 0.95 × 0.05 = 0.0475.
  2. Write the bound. P(|X̄ - p| ≥ 0.005) ≤ 0.0475 ÷ (n × 0.0052).
  3. Compute the squared threshold. 0.0052 = 0.000025.
  4. Set the bound to 0.05. 0.0475 ÷ (n × 0.000025) = 0.05.
  5. Solve. n = 0.0475 ÷ (0.05 × 0.000025) = 0.0475 ÷ 0.00000125 = 38,000.
  6. Now the normal-based version. A 95 percent normal interval uses 1.96 standard errors, so it needs 1.96√(0.0475/n) = 0.005, giving n = 0.0475 × (1.96/0.005)2 = 7,299.
  7. Explain the gap. Chebyshev asks for five times as much data because it must hold for every distribution with that variance, including far worse-behaved ones than a sum of Bernoulli trials.
  8. Which to use. The normal one, here, because the next lesson shows a proportion at p = 0.95 with n in the thousands is comfortably in the range where the approximation holds. Chebyshev is the answer when you cannot make that argument, and its value is that it needs no argument at all.

Sanity check. The Chebyshev answer must be larger than the normal one, because it assumes strictly less. Both must scale as one over the squared threshold, so demanding a quarter of a point instead of half should quadruple both. If your Chebyshev number came out smaller, the bound was inverted.

Your teacher. Compute the standard error of the last accuracy number your team reported, then compare it with the gap you claimed over the previous model. If the gap is inside one error bar, the honest sentence is "not established on this test set", which is a different sentence from "no improvement" and worth practising saying.
