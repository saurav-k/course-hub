Newton's method, and why nobody runs it at scale

frontier 12 min Cost and behaviour from Goodfellow et al. 8.6.1 and Boyd & Vandenberghe 9.5. · Deep Learning, ch. 8

The one-minute version

  • Build a quadratic model instead of a straight-line one and minimise it exactly. The step is −H-1∇J.
  • On a quadratic it lands on the optimum in one step, from anywhere. Measured here to within 2 × 10-14.
  • It is affine invariant: rescale a feature by a million and its iteration count does not move. Everything Lesson 103 worried about simply does not apply.
  • And it is unaffordable: k2 entries, k3 work, every iteration. At GPT-3's size the Hessian alone is 61 zettabytes.

1. Build a better model

Lesson 102 built a straight line through the current point. A line has no bottom, which is why the step had to be bounded. So build a parabola instead: it has a bottom, and you can jump straight to it.

J(θ + d) ≈ J(θ) + ∇J(θ)Td + ½ dTHd,    minimised at   d = −H-1∇J(θ) d: the step being chosen. ∇J(θ): the gradient, as before. H: the Hessian, the matrix of second partial derivatives, one entry for every pair of parameters. It says how the surface curves in every direction at once. dTHd: the curvature felt along the direction d. H-1: the inverse of that matrix. The whole step: instead of scaling the gradient by one number, it is transformed by a matrix that undoes the surface's stretching.

2. On a quadratic it is exact, in one step

Let J(x) = ½xTAx − bTx, with A symmetric positive definite. Then ∇J = Ax − b and the Hessian is A everywhere.

  1. Take the Newton step from any x0. x1 = x0 − A-1(Ax0 − b).
  2. Distribute the inverse. A-1Ax0 = x0, so the expression is x0 − x0 + A-1b.
  3. Simplify. x1 = A-1b. Every trace of the starting point has cancelled.
  4. Check that is the answer. Setting ∇J = 0 gives Ax = b, whose unique solution is A-1b because A is invertible.
  5. Conclude. One step, from anywhere, exactly. Measured on a random 6 × 6 problem from starting points of norm about 100, the distance to the optimum after one step is 2 × 10-14.
And there is the catch, hiding in step 1. Computing that step required solving a linear system in A. On a two-parameter problem that is nothing. On a real model it is the entire difficulty, moved rather than removed.

3. Affine invariance: why Lesson 103 does not apply

Change coordinates by any invertible matrix T, so x = Ty, and let g(y) = J(Ty). Then ∇g = TT∇J and the Hessian becomes TTHT. The Newton step in the new coordinates is

−(TTHT)-1 TT∇J = −T-1H-1T-T TT∇J = −T-1H-1∇J T: any invertible change of coordinates, for instance the diagonal matrix that rescales one feature column. T-T: the inverse of the transpose. The middle step: the inverse of a product reverses the order and inverts each factor. The cancellation: T-TTT is the identity, so it disappears. The result: the step in y is exactly the image of the step in x, so mapping back gives the identical move.

So Newton's iterates under any linear rescaling are the images of its original iterates: the method does not notice. Compare with the gradient step, −ηTT∇J, which is not the image of −η∇J unless T is a rotation. That single asymmetry is the whole of Lesson 103.

4. The bill

The Hessian is k by k for k parameters, and the step needs a linear solve, which costs on the order of k3. And because the parameters move, the whole thing is rebuilt and re-solved every iteration.

5. Worked: the Hessian that does not fit on Earth

  1. Take the parameter count. GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters, stated in its abstract.
  2. Count the Hessian's entries. k2 = (1.75 × 1011)2 = 3.06 × 1022.
  3. Convert to bytes. At two bytes an entry, 6.13 × 1022 bytes. Assumption, stated: bfloat16.
  4. Name the unit. That is 61 zettabytes, for one matrix.
  5. Cost the solve. On the order of k3 = 5.4 × 1033 operations. Assumption, stated: the textbook cubic bound, not a measured cost.
  6. Remember the multiplier. Every iteration, because the parameters changed.
  7. Turn the question round. Solve 2k2 = 80 × 109 for the largest model whose Hessian fits one 80 GB accelerator: k = 200,000.

Sanity check. Squaring a parameter count means doubling its exponent, so 1011 becoming 1022 is right, and the jump from 200 MB to 200 TB for a thousandfold increase in k is a factor of a million, as squaring demands.

What changes at four bytes an entry? The storage doubles and nothing about the conclusion moves. The gap here is not a factor of two, it is twenty orders of magnitude, and no precision choice closes it.

What it means. A model small enough for a full Newton step is smaller than a single layer of anything modern. But look at the first bar: your LogisticRegression call from Lesson 100 has k = 13, and its default solver is lbfgs, a quasi-Newton method. You have been running an approximate second-order optimizer this whole time, on a problem where it is simply the right choice.

6. The second reason, which Lesson 108 owns

Cost is not the only obstacle. The Newton step points downhill only if H is positive definite, and in a deep network it is not. What it does instead is the subject of the next page, and it is more interesting than "it goes slightly wrong".

code/0107-newtons-method-and-why-nobody-runs-it.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def newton(design, target, tolerance=1e-8, cap=100):
    theta = np.zeros(design.shape[1])
    for step in range(1, cap + 1):
        grad = gradient(design, target, theta)
        if np.linalg.norm(grad) < tolerance:
            return theta, step
        # solve, never invert: same answer, better conditioned, and it is
        # what any real implementation does.
        theta = theta - np.linalg.solve(hessian(design, theta), grad)
    return theta, cap

Note the comment. Writing inv(H) @ g would be mathematically identical and numerically worse, and no production solver forms an explicit inverse. The program reaches the same optimum as gradient descent to ten decimal places, in 6 iterations against 1,264, and reproduces both theorems directly.

The main obstacle to Newton's method on a large network is that
At 175 billion parameters the matrix alone is 3 × 10²² entries and it is rebuilt every iteration. The second is a real secondary problem on stochastic minibatches, but it is not the blocker. The third is true only on a measure-zero set of points and is not what stops the method. The fourth is backwards: the pure Newton step takes no learning rate at all, which is part of its appeal.
What does Newton's method buy that gradient descent cannot?
Affine invariance is the prize, and it makes the condition number irrelevant. The first is false: pure Newton can diverge from a poor start, which is why real implementations add a trust region. The second gets the cost exactly backwards. The third is precisely backwards and is the most valuable wrong answer here, because Lesson 108 shows Newton is attracted to saddle points rather than escaping them.

Then find the largest k whose Hessian fits in one 80 GB accelerator, and say what fraction of the real cost that number accounts for.

Hint

The last part is the one with teeth. Storage is not the only bill and the other term grows faster, so a model that just fits in memory is not a model you can actually take a step on.

Show the worked solution
  1. The formula. bytes = 2k2.
  2. k = 104. 2 × 108 bytes, which is 200 MB. Comfortable.
  3. k = 107. 2 × 1014 bytes, which is 200 TB. Not comfortable.
  4. k = 1.75 × 1011. 6.13 × 1022 bytes, which is 61 zettabytes.
  5. Largest k for 80 GB. Solve 2k2 = 8 × 1010, so k2 = 4 × 1010 and k = 200,000.
  6. Now the other half. At k = 200,000 the solve is on the order of k3 = 8 × 1015 operations.
  7. Per what? Per iteration. So the model that exactly fills your memory still needs a petaflop-scale computation for every single step, and storage accounted for only the part you could see.

Sanity check. Moving from k = 104 to k = 107 multiplies k by 1,000, so the memory must multiply by 1,000,000. Check: 200 MB to 200 TB is exactly that. If your ratio is 1,000 you have computed 2k rather than 2k2, and the whole argument on this page collapses to something affordable, which should itself be the warning.
