Least squares: the normal equations, and why they are a projection

working 10 min The most-used formula in applied statistics is lesson 50's picture with a data matrix in it.

The one-minute version

  • When no exact solution exists, minimise ||A x − b|| instead, and the answer is the projection of b onto the column space.
  • The normal equations ATA x = ATb are exactly the statement that the residual is orthogonal to every column.
  • ATA is invertible exactly when the columns are independent, which is lesson 49's rank condition doing real work.
  • The formula with the inverse in it is a formula, not an algorithm. Solve, or use QR, and never form the inverse.

The setup, and the honest admission

With more samples than parameters the system is tall, and lesson 47 said that a tall system usually has no solution. So change the question. Instead of demanding Ax = b, ask for the x that makes the two sides as close as possible.

minimise ||Ax − b||2 over all x Read as: A is the design matrix with one row per sample and one column per parameter, b is the vector of observed targets, and x is the vector of coefficients being chosen. The double bars are the Euclidean norm of lesson 42, and squaring it removes the square root without changing which x wins, because squaring is increasing on non-negative numbers.

Squaring is a choice. It is not the only sensible measure of "close" and this module does not claim it is the best one. What it is, is the choice that makes the answer a projection, and module 09 gives the statistical reason it is also the natural one.

The normal equations are the orthogonality condition

Lesson 50 proved that the closest reachable point is the one whose residual is perpendicular to everything reachable. Here "everything reachable" is the column space of A, so the condition is that the residual is orthogonal to every column.

Why ATAx = ATb is exactly that condition

What is assumed. A is any matrix, b any target of matching length, and x a candidate coefficient vector with residual r = b − Ax. No calculus is used, and no assumption is made about the shape of A.

The shape of the argument. Write "the residual is orthogonal to every column" as one matrix equation, then rearrange it. The rearrangement is the normal equations, so the two statements are the same statement.

The step that does the real work is noticing that stacking one dot product per column is a matrix-vector product with AT. Column j of A is row j of AT, so the vector whose j-th entry is (column j) · r is precisely ATr.

ATr = 0  ⇔  AT(b − Ax) = 0  ⇔  ATb − ATAx = 0  ⇔  ATAx = ATb Read as: 0 is the zero vector with one entry per column of A. The first double arrow substitutes the definition of the residual. The second distributes AT over the subtraction, which lesson 44 allows. The third moves one term across. Every step is reversible, which is why these are equivalences and not merely implications.

Every arrow runs both ways, so a coefficient vector solves the normal equations if and only if its residual is orthogonal to the column space, which by lesson 50 holds if and only if it is the minimiser. Existence, uniqueness and optimality all come from that theorem rather than from anything new here. ■

The matrix ATA is called the Gram matrix. It is square with one row per parameter, and it is symmetric because its (i, j) entry is the dot product of columns i and j. It is invertible exactly when the columns of A are linearly independent, which is lesson 49's full-rank condition, and when it is not, lesson 49 already showed what happens: many different coefficient vectors fit equally well.

ATAx = ATb can be rewritten as x = (ATA)−1ATb, and that form is worth recognising because it appears everywhere. It is not worth computing. Forming the Gram matrix squares the condition number, which on this course's own data takes it from 8.9 × 103 to 7.9 × 107, and forming the inverse on top of that adds lesson 47's penalty again. A QR factorisation avoids the squaring altogether.

The two pictures are one picture

A fitted line drawn through a scatter of points and a vector dropping perpendicularly onto a plane look like unrelated diagrams. They are the same fit, drawn in two different spaces, and seeing that is the most useful thing on this page.

What a solver actually does

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. X is the design matrix: a column of ones for the intercept, then the eight sensor columns, so 12,000 rows by 9 columns. y is the target and β the nine coefficients.
  2. Build a target with a known truth. The program forms y = Xβtrue + noise, with βtrue = (12.5, 3.2, −1.8, 0.9, 2.4, −0.6, 1.1, 0.35, 0.07) and noise of standard deviation 2. So there is a right answer to recover, not just a number to report.
  3. Check the shape. 12,000 equations in 9 unknowns. Tall, over-determined, and with noise added there is no exact solution.
  4. Form the two sides. XTX is 9 by 9 and symmetric, and XTy is a 9-vector.
  5. Solve them. The fitted coefficients are (11.5384, 3.2030, −1.8178, 0.8537, 2.4283, −0.5915, 1.0696, 0.3565, 0.0721).
  6. Compare with the truth. Every slope is within 0.05 of the value used to build the data, and the largest slope error is 0.0463. The intercept is out by 0.96, which is much the loosest, and the reason is that no sensor sits near zero so the intercept is extrapolating a long way past the data.
  7. Check the orthogonality. XTr has largest entry 1.06 × 10−7, which relative to the sizes of X and r is 10−13: zero, in floating point. And 50,000 random nearby coefficient vectors were all worse, the smallest gap being positive.
  8. Say what would change. Add a duplicate sensor column in different units and the Gram matrix becomes singular, the coefficients stop being unique, and lesson 49's two-answers-one-fit result appears. Nothing about the fit gets worse; what breaks is the ability to read the coefficients as separate effects.

Sanity check. The root mean square residual comes out at 2.0112 against a noise standard deviation of 2.0. It has to land near 2: much smaller would mean the fit had memorised the noise, which nine parameters cannot do to 12,000 points, and much larger would mean the model shape was wrong.

What it means. Every linear regression anyone runs is this. The library call hides the factorisation, and the thing it returns is the coefficient vector whose residual is perpendicular to every column of the design matrix. The residual that remains is not a failure of the fitting: it is the part of the target that no linear combination of these particular columns can express.

code/0051-least-squares.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/sensors.csv")
SENSORS = ["vibration_x", "vibration_y", "acoustic_db", "current_amp",
           "humidity_pct", "dust_index", "temp_c", "pressure_kpa"]
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(frame))] + [frame[s].to_numpy(float) for s in SENSORS])

truth = np.array([12.5, 3.2, -1.8, 0.9, 2.4, -0.6, 1.1, 0.35, 0.07])
y = X @ truth + np.random.default_rng(20260822).normal(0.0, 2.0, len(frame))

beta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)     # the normal equations, solved not inverted
r = y - X @ beta
print(np.round(beta, 4))
print(np.abs(X.T @ r).max())                 # 1.06e-07, which is zero here

The full program also tests 50,000 nearby coefficient vectors and finds none better, checks that Pythagoras holds between the fitted vector and the residual across all 12,000 dimensions, and compares the normal equations against QR and against NumPy's own routine.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What do the normal equations ATAx = ATb say?
Rearranged they read A transpose times the residual equals zero, which is precisely "the residual is orthogonal to every column". The first is what least squares gives up on and is the case that has no solution. The second is not required and is usually false, since A is tall and has no inverse at all. The third would mean a perfect fit, which is the situation least squares does not assume.
ATA turns out not to be invertible. What does that tell you about A?
The Gram matrix is invertible exactly when the columns of A are linearly independent, so a failure means a dependency, which in a feature table is collinearity. The first is a shape observation that does not settle it: a tall matrix can still have dependent columns. The third is about scaling, which changes the condition number but never the rank. The fourth is about b, which does not appear in A transpose A at all.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Fit y = b0 + b1t to four observations, by hand.

t = (0, 1, 2, 3),   y = (1, 3, 4, 6) Read as: four samples. t is the single predictor and y the observed value, so the design matrix has a column of ones and a column of t values, and there are two coefficients to find.
  1. Write the design matrix X.
  2. Form XTX and XTy.
  3. Solve the normal equations for b0 and b1.
  4. Compute the four residuals and verify XTr = 0, then say in words what the two components of that check mean.
Hint

The entries of XTX are dot products of columns with columns, and the entries of XTy are dot products of columns with y. There are only four numbers and two numbers to compute, and the column of ones makes half of them sums.

Hint for part 4

XTr has one entry per column. Ask what the dot product of the ones column with the residual is, and then what the dot product of the t column with the residual is. Each has a plain-English reading.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the design matrix. X has rows (1, 0), (1, 1), (1, 2) and (1, 3): a ones column for the intercept and the t values beside it.
  2. Form the Gram matrix. Ones dotted with ones is 4. Ones dotted with t is 0+1+2+3 = 6. t dotted with t is 0+1+4+9 = 14. So XTX = [[4, 6], [6, 14]].
  3. Form the right-hand side. Ones dotted with y is 1+3+4+6 = 14. t dotted with y is 0+3+8+18 = 29. So XTy = (14, 29).
  4. Solve. 4b0 + 6b1 = 14 and 6b0 + 14b1 = 29. Multiply the first by 1.5: 6b0 + 9b1 = 21. Subtract from the second: 5b1 = 8, so b1 = 1.6. Then 4b0 = 14 − 9.6 = 4.4, so b0 = 1.1.
  5. Fit the four points. At t = 0, 1, 2, 3 the fitted values are 1.1, 2.7, 4.3 and 5.9.
  6. Take the residuals. r = (1 − 1.1, 3 − 2.7, 4 − 4.3, 6 − 5.9) = (−0.1, 0.3, −0.3, 0.1).
  7. Check both components. Ones dotted with r = −0.1 + 0.3 − 0.3 + 0.1 = 0. And t dotted with r = 0(−0.1) + 1(0.3) + 2(−0.3) + 3(0.1) = 0 + 0.3 − 0.6 + 0.3 = 0.
  8. Read them in words. The first says the residuals sum to zero, so the fit is not biased up or down overall. The second says the residuals are uncorrelated with t, so there is no leftover trend the line could have picked up. Together they are the orthogonality principle in the only two directions this model has.

Sanity check. The residuals must sum to exactly zero whenever the design matrix contains a column of ones, and that is a free check on any fit with an intercept. If yours do not sum to zero, the arithmetic in step 4 is wrong, not the data.

Your teacher. If the two pictures still feel like two topics, ask an agent to fit three points to a line, then to print the residual vector and the two design columns, and to compute the two dot products. The residual having zero dot product with both columns is the right angle in the second picture, and there is nothing else to it.
