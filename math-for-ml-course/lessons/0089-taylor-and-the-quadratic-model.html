Taylor expansion: every loss is a quadratic if you stand close enough

frontier 13 min Lesson 83 gave the direction and Lesson 88 gave the curvature. This page is where they meet.

The one-minute version

  • Keep one term and you have a tangent line. Keep two and you have a bowl that tracks the real loss nearly five times further.
  • Substitute a gradient step into that bowl and three named terms fall out: where you are, what the slope promises, and what the curvature takes back.
  • The third term is why a downhill step can raise the loss. Nothing about the direction was wrong; the step was too long.
  • Minimising the model gives one number, and on the sensor regression that number is five millionths. Standardise the columns and it becomes 0.13.

1. One term, then two

Pick a point, and ask for the best straight line through it. That is the tangent, and it is the first-order Taylor approximation: the value plus the slope times how far you moved.

f(x) ≈ f(x0) + ∇f(x0)T(x − x0) f: a loss, taking a vector of parameters and returning one number. x0: the point you are standing at. x: any nearby point. ∇f(x0): the gradient there, a column with one entry per parameter, from Lesson 83. ∇f(x0)T(x − x0): one number, the slope in the direction you moved times the distance you moved.

Now add the next term, which needs the Hessian from Lesson 88.

f(x) ≈ f(x0) + ∇f(x0)Th + ½ hTHh,   where h = x − x0 h: the displacement from the expansion point, a column of the same shape as the parameters. H: the Hessian at x0, the matrix of second partial derivatives. hTHh: one number, the curvature along h scaled by the squared length of h, which is the quadratic form Module 04 met and Lesson 88 gave a meaning.

2. Taylor's theorem, and the remainder that makes it a model

What is assumed: f is differentiable enough on an interval containing both points. The shape of the argument: build one function that vanishes at both ends of the interval, apply Rolle's theorem once, and read off the constant it forces. The step that does the real work is the telescoping derivative in step 3, because that is what collapses a whole polynomial into a single term.

Theorem (Taylor, with the Lagrange remainder). If f is n+1 times differentiable on an open interval containing a and x, there is a c strictly between them with f(x) = ∑k=0..n f(k)(a)(x−a)k/k! + f(n+1)(c)(x−a)n+1/(n+1)!.

  1. Fix the target. Fix x ≠ a and write P(t) = ∑k=0..n f(k)(t)(x−t)k/k!, the Taylor polynomial about a moving base point t, evaluated at the fixed x.
  2. Build a function that vanishes twice. Let g(t) = f(x) − P(t) − M(x−t)n+1, choosing the constant M so that g(a) = 0. Then g(x) = 0 too, because P(x) = f(x) and the last term dies.
  3. Differentiate P and watch it telescope. Every term of P′(t) cancels against the next except the last, leaving P′(t) = f(n+1)(t)(x−t)n/n!. This is the step that does the work.
  4. Apply Rolle. g vanishes at both a and x, so g′(c) = 0 for some c strictly between. That reads −f(n+1)(c)(x−c)n/n! + M(n+1)(x−c)n = 0.
  5. Cancel and substitute. (x−c)n is non-zero because c ≠ x, so M = f(n+1)(c)/(n+1)!. Putting that M back into g(a) = 0 is the statement of the theorem. QED

The multivariate case this course actually uses is one line from here. For f twice continuously differentiable and any displacement h, apply the theorem with n = 1 to φ(s) = f(x + sh) on [0, 1]. The chain rule of Lesson 81 gives φ′(s) = ∇f(x + sh)Th and φ″(s) = hTH(x + sh)h, and the one-variable statement φ(1) = φ(0) + φ′(0) + φ″(t)/2 becomes f(x+h) = f(x) + ∇f(x)Th + ½hTH(x+th)h for some t in (0, 1).

That last Hessian is evaluated somewhere along the step rather than at its start, and that is the whole content of the remainder: it is what turns "close enough" from a gesture into a bound you could compute if you knew how big the second derivative can get.

3. Substitute a gradient step and read the three terms

Take a step of length ε against the gradient, so h = −εg, and substitute.

f(x0 − εg) ≈ f(x0) − ε gTg + ½ ε² gTHg ε: the learning rate, a positive number saying how far to move. g: the gradient at x0. gTg: the squared length of the gradient, always positive away from a critical point, so the middle term is always a decrease. gTHg: the curvature along the gradient direction, scaled by the squared length of the gradient. When it is positive, the last term is always an increase.
The reframing A gradient step does not fail because the direction is wrong. It fails because the third term is bigger than the second, which is a statement about how long the step was and how curved the surface is, and about nothing else.

4. The step that minimises the model

The right-hand side is a quadratic in ε that opens upward whenever gTHg > 0. Differentiate it in ε, set it to zero, and one number falls out.

ε* = gTg / gTHg,   and in the worst case ε* = 1/λmax ε*: the step that drives the second-order model lowest. λmax: the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, which Lesson 88 showed is the curvature in the steepest-curving direction. The worst case is when g lies along that direction, because then the curvature term is as large as it can be for a gradient of that length.

5. Worked: how big a step will the sensor regression tolerate

Twelve thousand machine readings, six sensor columns and an intercept, squared-error loss, standing at the intercept-only guess that predicts every reading as the overall mean temperature.

  1. Take the squared length of the gradient. gTg = 6.143982 × 105.
  2. Take the curvature along it. With H = (2/n)XTX from Lesson 88, gTHg = 1.199192 × 1011.
  3. Divide. ε* = 6.143982 × 105 ÷ 1.199192 × 1011 = 5.123437 × 10−6.
  4. Compare with the worst case. λmax = 1.984268 × 105, so 1/λmax = 5.039641 × 10−6. The two agree to two per cent.
  5. Ask why they agree. Because g is almost the top eigenvector: |cos ∠(g, vmax)| = 0.991784. Unscaled columns guarantee this, since the column with the largest numbers dominates both the gradient and the curvature.
  6. Scan the true loss along the ray. 54.275914 at ε = 0, 52.701999 at ε*, and 54.275914 again at 2ε*, to six decimals.
  7. Standardise every column and repeat. λmin and λmax move from 5.608 × 10−3 and 1.984 × 105 to 2.505 × 10−2 and 7.621, so κ falls from 35,382,029 to 304.2 and 1/λmax rises from 5.040 × 10−6 to 0.1312, a factor of 26,038.

Sanity check. ε* must be positive, since gTg > 0 and gTHg > 0 for a positive definite H. It must also be at least 1/λmax, because gTHg ≤ λmaxgTg for every g. Both hold, and the near-equality is itself the diagnosis rather than a coincidence.

What changes if you standardise only the worst column? κ falls from 35,382,029 to 748.6, which is most of the distance, but the fully standardised design reaches 304.2, so the largest tolerable step is still short by a factor of 16. Fixing one column moves the ratios between the columns you left alone without equalising them, which is why scaling is applied to the whole design matrix rather than to the column that looks worst.

In words. Nothing about the data changed. The readings are the same readings and the fit is the same fit. Only the units changed, and the largest step the curvature will tolerate rose by four orders of magnitude.

A demonstration that does not work, and why the page says soThe obvious way to show Taylor's error rates is to halve the distance and watch the second-order error fall eightfold. On a squared-error loss it does not, because such a loss is a quadratic: its third derivative is zero, the second-order model is exact, and the measured error sits between 0 and 2.2 × 10−11 at every distance, which is the floating-point floor and not a rate. The program therefore measures the rates on a logistic loss instead, where the error ratios per decade come out at 100.2 for first order and 1004.0 for second, matching the distance squared and the distance cubed. A page that claimed a cubic rate on the sensor regression would have been contradicted by its own numbers.

6. What this buys, and what Module 06 does with it

The condition number is not a curiosity. Boyd and Vandenberghe measure it directly and conclude that convergence "depends greatly on the condition number of the Hessian", that it can be very slow even in the hundreds, and that at 1000 or more "the gradient method is so slow that it is useless in practice". The sensor regression sits at 35 million, and standardising six columns takes it to 304.

So feature scaling is not tidiness and not a preprocessing habit. It is a curvature fix, and this page is where that becomes provable rather than folklore. What to do with ε* and κ once you have them belongs to Module 06.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In f(x0 − εg) ≈ f(x0) − εgTg + ½ε²gTHg, which term can make a downhill step raise the loss?
The third term is positive whenever the curvature along the gradient is, and it grows with the square of the step, so it eventually beats the linear decrease. The first option is the misconception the page exists to kill: downhill is a statement about an infinitesimal step. The first term is a constant that does not depend on ε at all. The second term is −εgTg, which is negative for every positive ε and so is a decrease at any step length, however long.
Standardising the sensor columns drops the Hessian's condition number from 35,382,029 to 304.2. What follows for the step the curvature will tolerate?
The bound is 1/λmax, and standardising takes λmax from 1.98 × 105 down to 7.62, so the tolerable step rises by a factor of 26,038. The second has the right magnitude and the sign backwards, which is what makes it the strongest distractor: shrinking the widest column shrinks the largest curvature, so the bound rises rather than falls. The third is a true statement about the fitted values, which really are unchanged, answering a question about predictions rather than about steps. The fourth ignores the worst-case bound 1/λmax, which needs the Hessian and not the gradient.
