How big, how far, and why the units decide the answer

foundation 9 min A norm is a choice, not a fact, and every distance-based method in machine learning inherits whichever one you made.

The one-minute version

  • A norm answers "how big", and there is more than one sensible answer for the same vector.
  • Distance is the norm of a difference, so every choice about size is a choice about nearness.
  • The three properties a norm must have are a definition you can check, and the popular non-example fails one of them.
  • On raw sensor readings, one of the eight carries 95.98% of all squared distance. Standardising is not tidying, it is deciding what the question means.

Four norms, one vector

The Euclidean norm, written with double bars, is the square root of the sum of the squares of the entries. It is the one that matches the ruler, and it is so common that the subscript is usually dropped.

||x||2 = √(x12 + x22 + … + xn2) = √(xTx) Read as: square every entry of x, add them all up, take the square root. The n is the number of entries. The right-hand form says the same thing using the dot product of the vector with itself, which is the operation the next lesson is about.

Three others earn their place. The L1 norm is the sum of the absolute values, and it grows at the same rate everywhere rather than slowly near the origin, which is why it is reached for whenever the difference between zero and slightly-not-zero matters. The L-infinity norm, or max norm, is the largest magnitude among the entries. The Frobenius norm applies to a matrix and is the Euclidean norm of the matrix read as one long list.

For the vector (3, −4, 0, 12) these give 19, 13 and 12 respectively. Three different answers to "how big is this", all of them correct.

What makes something a norm

A norm is any function from vectors to non-negative numbers with three properties. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville state them as: the value is zero only for the zero vector, the triangle inequality holds, and scaling the vector by a number scales the value by the size of that number.

f(x) = 0 ⇒ x = 0  ·  f(x + y) ≤ f(x) + f(y)  ·  f(αx) = |α| f(x) Read as: f is the candidate size function, x and y are any two vectors of the same length, 0 is the vector of all zeros, and α is any single number. The vertical bars around α mean its size ignoring sign. The middle property says a detour is never shorter than going direct.

These are worth having as a checklist because a very common quantity fails one of them.

The non-zero count is not a norm

Claim. The function that counts a vector's non-zero entries, sometimes written with a subscript zero and called a norm, is not one.

Proof. Let f be that count and take any vector x with at least one non-zero entry, so f(x) = k for some k ≥ 1. Take α = 2. Scaling by 2 multiplies every entry by 2, which turns no zero entry into a non-zero one and no non-zero entry into a zero one, so f(2x) = k. But absolute homogeneity would require f(2x) = |2| f(x) = 2k, and k = 2k only when k = 0, which contradicts k ≥ 1. So the property fails for every vector that is not the zero vector. ■

The count is still a useful quantity, and it is exactly what L1 regularisation is trying to approximate with something a gradient can work with. Calling it a norm is the error, not using it.

Distance, and the units problem

The distance between two vectors is the norm of their difference. Nothing more is needed, and that is precisely the trouble: the entries of a feature vector are quantities in different units, and the norm treats them all alike.

Boyd and Vandenberghe put it plainly: when entries represent different kinds of quantity, the units should be chosen so that typical numerical values are similar in magnitude, or the entries with large numbers will decide every distance on their own.

On this course's sensor table the effect is not subtle. pressure_kpa is recorded in kilopascals and sits in the hundreds, while six of the other seven sensors sit near zero. Standardisation is a norm fact first

Subtracting the mean of a vector's entries from each entry gives the de-meaned vector. Its own mean is zero. The standard deviation is then the root mean square of that de-meaned vector, which is to say its Euclidean norm divided by the square root of the number of entries.

std(x) = ||x − avg(x)1|| / √n Read as: 1 is the vector of all ones, so avg(x)1 is the mean repeated n times, and subtracting it centres the vector. Take the Euclidean norm of what is left and divide by the square root of the number of entries. On the course's vibration_x column both sides come to 5.451393.

Dividing the de-meaned vector by that number gives the standardised vector, whose entries are z-scores. Module 09 owns what a z-score means statistically. Here it is simply the rescaling that makes the previous figure's second bar look the way it does.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. g is a gradient, a vector with one entry per parameter. The threshold τ is the largest norm we are willing to take a step with.
  2. Take the vector. g = (0.4, −1.2, 3.5, 0.9, −2.8, 1.1), with τ = 1.0.
  3. Measure it. The sum of squares is 0.16 + 1.44 + 12.25 + 0.81 + 7.84 + 1.21 = 23.71, so ||g|| = 4.8693.
  4. Decide. 4.8693 is at least 1.0, so the rule fires. This is the norm clipping of Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, their Algorithm 1.
  5. Compute the scale. τ / ||g|| = 1 / 4.8693 = 0.2054.
  6. Apply it. The clipped gradient is (0.0821, −0.2464, 0.7188, 0.1848, −0.5750, 0.2259).
  7. Check twice. Its norm is 1.000000, which absolute homogeneity predicted without any arithmetic: scaling by τ/||g|| must give norm |τ/||g||| × ||g|| = τ. And its cosine with the original is 1.000000, so the direction is untouched.
  8. Say what would change. Clipping each entry separately instead, to the range ±τ, would be an L-infinity rule and would bend the direction. The choice of norm is the choice of what "too big" means.
code/0042-norm-and-distance.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3.0, -4.0, 0.0, 12.0])
print(np.abs(v).sum())            # 19.0   L1
print(np.sqrt(v @ v))             # 13.0   L2
print(np.abs(v).max())            # 12.0   L-infinity
print(np.count_nonzero(v))        # 3      not a norm

g = np.array([0.4, -1.2, 3.5, 0.9, -2.8, 1.1])
clipped = g * (1.0 / np.linalg.norm(g))
print(np.linalg.norm(clipped))    # 1.0, exactly

The full program checks absolute homogeneity and the triangle inequality on 10,000 random pairs drawn from the sensor table, confirms that the non-zero count breaks homogeneity in all 10,000 of them, and produces the two bars in the figure above.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is counting a vector's non-zero entries not a norm?
Absolute homogeneity demands that scaling by two doubles the value, and doubling every entry changes no entry's zero-ness, so the count stays put. The second is not a defect: every norm returns zero, and does so only for the zero vector. The third is false, since the count is exactly what L1 regularisation approximates. The fourth is untrue, because the count does satisfy the triangle inequality.
On raw sensor readings, pressure carried 95.98% of all squared distance. What does that mean?
Distance is dominated by whichever entry has the largest numerical spread, so a nearest-neighbour search on these raw sensors is very nearly a search on pressure with seven sensors along for the ride. The first blames the data, which is fine; the numbers are simply in kilopascals. The second is the opposite conclusion: the others are informative, they are just numerically small. The fourth blames the norm when the fault is the units, and standardising fixes it without changing the norm.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

A training step produces the gradient g = (0.6, −0.8, 2.4, −1.8), and the trainer clips by global norm with threshold 2.0.

  1. Does clipping fire?
  2. What is the scale factor, to four decimal places?
  3. What is the clipped vector, and what is its norm?
  4. A colleague proposes clipping each entry to the range ±2.0 instead. What is the resulting vector, and what has changed that the global rule preserved?
Hint

For part 3 you do not need to recompute the norm from the entries. Absolute homogeneity tells you what it must be. For part 4, compare directions, not lengths.

Show the worked solution
  1. The sum of squares is 0.36 + 0.64 + 5.76 + 3.24 = 10.0, so ||g|| = 3.1623. That is at least 2.0, so yes.
  2. 2.0 / 3.1623 = 0.6325.
  3. (0.3795, −0.5060, 1.5179, −1.1384). Its norm is exactly 2.0, because scaling by 0.6325 scales the norm by 0.6325, and 3.1623 × 0.6325 = 2.0.
  4. Entrywise clipping gives (0.6, −0.8, 2.0, −1.8): only the third entry moved. The vector now points somewhere else, because one coordinate shrank and the others did not. The global rule scaled everything by one positive number, which is why its direction survived and this one's does not.

Sanity check. in part 3 every entry should be smaller in size than the matching entry of g, and the ratio of any entry to its original should be the same 0.6325 in all four positions.
