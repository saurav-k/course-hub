A histogram has a choice baked into it, and the choice can change the answer

foundation 11 min Plot the column before you summarise it. Then plot it again at a different width.

The one-minute version

  • A frequency table counts how often each value occurs; a histogram is that table drawn once the values have been grouped into class intervals.
  • Relative frequency is what lets two differently sized samples be compared, and the cumulative column is where a median can be read off directly.
  • Area is the quantity, not height. With equal bins the two agree, which is exactly why unequal bins mislead.
  • The bin width is your choice, and the modal interval moves with it. "The most common session length" is not a property of the data alone.

1. A frequency table, by hand

When a column has few distinct values, you can count them directly. Take twenty response codes and tally them: that is a frequency table, and it is the whole idea.

Two columns are worth adding immediately. Relative frequency is each count divided by the total, and it is what makes two samples of different sizes comparable - 300 out of 20,000 and 3 out of 200 are the same story. Cumulative frequency is the running total, and it answers "how much of the data is at or below this point", which is where lesson 23 will pick a quantile straight off the column.

2. Class intervals, when there are too many values to tally

A column of session lengths has thousands of distinct values, so tallying is useless. Group them into class intervals of equal width instead, and count how many land in each.

One convention has to be stated out loud, because leaving it implicit is where every off-by-one in a bin count comes from. This course uses half-open intervals: a value at a boundary belongs to the interval it starts, not the one it ends. So [0, 11.2) holds everything from 0 up to but not including 11.2, and 11.2 itself goes into the next interval. Every value lands in exactly one bin, which is lesson 02's word: the intervals are a partition.

3. Two rules for the width, and what they do to a heavy tail

There are standard rules for picking a width. On a real column they can disagree wildly, and watching them disagree teaches more than either rule does.

4. Area, not height

With equal-width bins, a taller bar means more data and everyone reads it correctly. The moment the widths differ, that stops being true: a bin twice as wide collects roughly twice as much data for the same underlying density, so its bar is twice as tall for no reason a reader can see.

The quantity a histogram encodes is area. With equal widths, area is proportional to height and the distinction never comes up, which is exactly why it catches people the first time they meet an unequal-bin chart.

5. The width is a choice, and the mode moves with it

Here is the finding that makes this page worth its place. Take the same column, change nothing but the bin width, and ask which interval is the most common.

6. The cumulative column, and reading a median off it

The cumulative column is the running total of the relative frequencies, and it climbs from 0 to 1. Wherever it crosses 0.5 is where half the data sits below - the median, arriving as a row number rather than as a formula.

7. Bars that touch, and bars that do not

One drawing convention carries real information. A histogram has bars that touch, because the axis underneath is continuous and the intervals are adjacent. A bar chart has gaps, because the categories underneath are separate things with no space between them.

A chart that gets this wrong is making a claim about the column's measurement scale from lesson 20 - and getting it wrong. Touching bars over a nominal column assert an ordering and a spacing that the column does not have.

8. Worked: twenty values, five classes

Twenty response times in seconds, sorted: 3, 5, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 21, 23, 24, 27, 29, 33, 38, 44, 51, 68.

  1. Pick the range and the width. Minimum 3, maximum 68, so a span of 65. Five classes of width 14 covers it from 0: [0,14), [14,28), [28,42), [42,56), [56,70).
  2. Count into half-open intervals. 3, 5, 8, 9, 11, 12 gives 6; then 14, 16, 17, 19, 21, 23, 24, 27 gives 8; then 29, 33, 38 gives 3; then 44, 51 gives 2; then 68 gives 1.
  3. Check the total. 6 + 8 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 20. Every value in exactly one bin, which is the partition doing its job. Note that 14 went up, not down.
  4. Divide for relative frequency. 0.30, 0.40, 0.15, 0.10, 0.05, summing to 1.00.
  5. Run the cumulative column. 0.30, 0.70, 0.85, 0.95, 1.00. It passes 0.5 inside the second class, so the median lies in [14, 28).
  6. Check against the sorted list. With 20 values the median is the average of the 10th and 11th, which are 19 and 21, giving 20 - and 20 does sit in [14, 28). The table and the sort agree.
Sanity check. The relative frequencies must sum to exactly 1 and the last cumulative entry must equal 1. If either misses, a boundary value went into two bins or into none. What changes if you use ten classes of width 7 instead: the counts spread out, the modal class moves, and the median still lands between the same two data values, because a median reads the sorted list rather than the table.

9. Both bin counts, checked against each other

# bin twice: once with an explicit walk over the boundaries, once with the library
counts_by_hand = [((column >= lo) & (column < hi)).sum() for lo, hi in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:])]
counts_by_cut  = pd.cut(column, bins=edges, right=False).value_counts().sort_index().to_numpy()
assert (counts_by_hand == counts_by_cut).all()

The explicit version is the half-open convention written out: greater than or equal to the low edge, strictly less than the high one. Asserting the two agree is how you find out that pd.cut defaults to closing on the right, and that right=False is what makes it match the convention this page uses.

One honest note. The brief for this page asked for three widths, one of which hides a second mode. This column does not have a second mode - it is a single peak with a long right tail - so no width can hide one. What the program actually found is stronger and is what the page teaches instead: the modal interval itself moves with the width. The lesson survives the correction; the invented second mode would not have.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

On a histogram with unequal bin widths, what does a bar's height represent?
Area carries the count, so with unequal widths the height has to be count over width for the areas to stay comparable. The first option is true whenever the bins are equal, which is nearly always, and that is exactly why it is the belief that misleads the one time it matters. The third is the cumulative curve, a different chart. The fourth describes the horizontal axis, not the vertical one.
A colleague reports "the most common session length is 0 to 120 seconds". What is missing?
A modal interval is a joint fact about the data and the width: on this column the answer moves from [25, 30) to [0, 120) as the width goes from 5 s to 120 s. The second and third are good habits and neither rescues the claim, since the mode still moves at any sample size and in any unit. The fourth would be a useful extra number and is not what is missing from this one.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Twenty response times, already sorted: 3, 5, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 21, 23, 24, 27, 29, 33, 38, 44, 51, 68.

(a) Build a frequency table with four classes of equal width, with relative and cumulative columns.
(b) Build it again with eight classes.
(c) Name one thing you would conclude from one table and not the other.

Hint

Fix your boundary convention before you count anything, and use the same one both times. For (c), look at whether the bulk of the data looks like one lump or like something with structure inside it.

Show the worked solution
  1. Four classes. Span 0 to 72 in steps of 18: [0,18), [18,36), [36,54), [54,72).
  2. Count them. 9, 7, 3, 1. Total 20. Relative 0.45, 0.35, 0.15, 0.05; cumulative 0.45, 0.80, 0.95, 1.00.
  3. Eight classes. Steps of 9: [0,9), [9,18), [18,27), [27,36), [36,45), [45,54), [54,63), [63,72).
  4. Count again. 3, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0, 1. Total 20; note 9 went up into the second class and 27 up into the third.
  5. Compare the modal class. At four classes it is the first, [0,18). At eight it is the second, [9,18), and the first class is now visibly thinner than it. The four-class table hides that the very shortest times are relatively rare.
  6. Answer (c). From the eight-class table you can see the distribution rises to a peak and then falls, with an empty class before the last value. From the four-class table it looks like a simple decline from the first bar, and there is an empty class at eight that the coarse table cannot show at all.

Sanity check. Both relative columns must sum to 1 and both cumulative columns must end at 1, and both totals must be 20. If a total comes to 21 you counted a boundary value twice, and if it comes to 19 you dropped one - those are the only two ways the arithmetic fails here.

Your teacher. This page names no theorem. A frequency table is a construction and the area convention is a definition, so there is nothing to prove - what there is, is a habit. Before you summarise any column, plot it, then plot it again at a different width, and only then decide what to report. If a conclusion survives both pictures it is probably about the data.

Primary source to go deeper

Freedman, D. and Diaconis, P., "On the histogram as a density estimator", Zeitschrift für Wahrscheinlichkeitstheorie und Verwandte Gebiete 57 (1981), 453-476. It is the source of the width rule this page contrasts with Sturges, and its framing is the reason the width matters: a histogram is an estimate, and the width is part of the estimator rather than part of the data.
