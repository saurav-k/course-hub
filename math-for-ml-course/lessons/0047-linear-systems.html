Ax = b, and the three answers a system can have

working 9 min No solution, exactly one, or infinitely many. There is no fourth option, and the reason is one line of algebra.

The one-minute version

  • A linear system has no solution, exactly one, or infinitely many. "Exactly two" is impossible.
  • Tall means over-determined, more equations than unknowns, and is the normal case for data. Wide means under-determined.
  • The inverse is a formula, not an algorithm. Solving directly costs about 2n3 flops; forming the inverse costs about 3n3 and is less accurate.
  • On this course's own normal equations, inverting gives a residual 352,315 times worse than solving.

The system, and its three outcomes

A set of linear equations in the same unknowns collapses into one matrix statement. A is called the coefficient matrix and b the right-hand side.

Ax = b Read as: A is an m by n matrix holding the coefficients, one row per equation and one column per unknown. x is the n-vector of unknowns and b is the m-vector of right-hand sides. A solution is any x that makes the equality hold in every one of the m rows.

Two equations in two unknowns are two lines in the plane, and there are exactly three things two lines can do. They cross once, they are parallel and never meet, or they are the same line and meet everywhere.

Why "exactly two solutions" is impossible

What is assumed. A is any matrix, b any right-hand side of matching length, and p and q are two different solutions of Ax = b.

The shape of the argument. Build a whole line of solutions out of the two you were given. If two exist, infinitely many do, so the count can never stop at two.

The step that does the real work is the distributive property of matrix-vector multiplication, from lesson 44. For any real number α, let z = αp + (1 − α)q. Then

Az = A(αp + (1 − α)q) = αAp + (1 − α)Aq = αb + (1 − α)b = b Read as: α is any single real number. The second equality is matrix-vector multiplication distributing over addition and passing through a scalar, which lesson 44 established. The third substitutes Ap = b and Aq = b, which is what being a solution means. The last is α + (1 − α) = 1.

So every z on the line through p and q is a solution. Since p and q are different, that line has infinitely many points, and each α gives a different one. Therefore a system with two distinct solutions has infinitely many, and the possible counts are zero, one, and infinite. ■

Tall, wide, square

The shape of A is the first thing to read, exactly as lesson 40 said, and here it predicts which outcome to expect.

Tall, with more rows than columns, means more equations than unknowns and is called over-determined. More samples than features is the normal case for a dataset, and an over-determined system usually has no exact solution at all. Wide means under-determined: more unknowns than equations, so solutions are typically a whole family. More features than samples is the ordinary situation in text and in genomics, and it is why regularisation is not optional there. Square is the case where a unique answer is plausible, and even then it needs the columns to be independent.

Solve, do not invert

When A is square and its columns are independent it has an inverse, and x = A−1b is a true statement. It is also the wrong way to compute the answer.

Boyd and Vandenberghe count both routes. Solving directly through a factorisation and back substitution costs about 2n3 flops. Forming the inverse costs about 2n3 for the same factorisation plus n3 for n back substitutions, so about 3n3, and then multiplying by b is extra work on top.

Accuracy points the same way. MathWorks' own reference page for inv calls solving a system with it "a frequent misuse", and recommends the backslash operator instead "from the standpoint of both execution time and numerical accuracy".

The bonus. Ten right-hand sides with the same A cost about 2n3 + 3kn2 rather than 2kn3, because the factorisation is done once and reused. At n = 700 and k = 10 that predicts a 9.8-fold saving, and measuring it gives 9.5.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. Three small systems, each written Ax = b, chosen so each lands in a different one of the three outcomes.
  2. Take the first. x1 + x2 = 1, x1 = −1, x1 − x2 = 0. Three equations, two unknowns, so A is 3 by 2 and tall.
  3. Look for the contradiction. The second equation forces x1 = −1, and the third then forces x2 = −1. Substituting both into the first gives −2, not 1. No solution, and the closest any x gets is a residual of 1.2247.
  4. Take the second. 2x1 + x2 = 5 and x1 + 3x2 = 10. Square, and the columns are independent, so exactly one: x = (1, 3), with a residual of 0 to machine precision.
  5. Take the third. x1 + x2 = 1 and x2 + x3 = 2. Two equations, three unknowns, so wide. Both (1, 0, 2) and (0, 1, 1) satisfy it.
  6. Build the line. Taking α from −2 to 3 in eleven steps and forming αp + (1 − α)q gives eleven different vectors, and every one has a residual of exactly zero. At α = 3 that vector is (3, −2, 4), which looks nothing like either of the two you started with.
  7. Count the cost of the wrong route. On a 700 by 700 system, solving takes 686 million flops and 0.0057 seconds; inverting takes 1,029 million flops and 0.0125 seconds, which is 2.2 times longer.
  8. Say what would change. If the third system had a third equation that contradicted the first two, it would move from the "infinitely many" case straight to "none" without ever passing through "exactly one".

Sanity check. In step 6 every residual must be zero to machine precision, not merely small. If one of them is around 10−3 rather than 10−16, the blend was formed with coefficients that do not sum to one, which is the only way the argument fails.

What it means. Fitting a model is asking a computer to solve Ax = b where the rows are samples. With more samples than parameters, the system is tall, and step 3 is the ordinary outcome: no set of parameters reproduces the data exactly. Lesson 51 is what to do about that, and it is not a workaround.

code/0047-linear-systems.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1., 1., 0.], [0., 1., 1.]])
b = np.array([1., 2.])
p, q = np.array([1., 0., 2.]), np.array([0., 1., 1.])

for alpha in np.linspace(-2.0, 3.0, 11):
    z = alpha * p + (1 - alpha) * q
    print(np.round(z, 3), f"{np.linalg.norm(A @ z - b):.3e}")   # every one is zero

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

How many solutions can a system of linear equations have?
If two distinct solutions exist then every blend of them is a solution too, so the count jumps straight from one to infinite. The first forgets the under-determined case, where a whole family satisfies the equations. The second forgets the inconsistent case, which is the usual one for real data. The third is exactly what the blending argument rules out.
You must solve Ax = b once, for a square invertible A. Which is better?
Solving directly is about 2n cubed flops against about 3n cubed to build the inverse, and it is also more accurate: on this course's own normal equations the inverse route's residual is 352,315 times larger. The first is the habit the page exists to break. The third contradicts both flop counts. The fourth would be reasonable if the two were equivalent, and they are measurably not.

Hint

For 1 and 2, count equations against unknowns before doing any algebra. That tells you which outcome to go looking for, and then you only have to confirm it.

Hint for part 3

The two formulas are on this page. The thing to notice is that one of the two terms in the cached formula is much smaller than the other when k is small compared with n.

Show the worked solution
  1. Shape the first. Three equations, two unknowns: 3 by 2, tall, over-determined.
  2. Solve what you can. The second equation gives x1 = −1. Putting that in the third gives −1 − x2 = 0, so x2 = −1.
  3. Test the leftover equation. The first needs x1 + x2 = 1, and we have −1 + −1 = −2. No solution.
  4. Shape the second. Two equations, three unknowns: 2 by 3, wide, under-determined.
  5. Find two solutions. Setting x3 = 2 gives x2 = 0 and x1 = 1. Setting x3 = 1 gives x2 = 1 and x1 = 0. So (1, 0, 2) and (0, 1, 1) both work, and by the blending argument there are infinitely many.
  6. Naive cost. Ten separate solves at about 2n3 each: 2 × 10 × 109 = 2.0 × 1010 flops.
  7. Cached cost. 2n3 + 3kn2 = 2 × 109 + 3 × 10 × 106 = 2.03 × 109 flops, a ratio of about 9.9 times.

Sanity check. The cached figure in step 7 should come out barely above 2n3, because 3kn2 is smaller than 2n3 by a factor of about n/k, which is 100 here. If your two totals were close to each other, the n2 term was written as an n3 term somewhere.

Your teacher. If the "never exactly two" argument feels like a trick, ask an agent to give you an under-determined system and then to test your own blends of two of its solutions, including blends with α outside the range from 0 to 1. Watching a coefficient like α = 7 still give a residual of zero is what makes the line feel infinite rather than merely long.
