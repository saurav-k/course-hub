Module 03 · Vectors, matrices, and linear maps · Lesson 64

A matrix is a stack of vectors, and multiplying by one is a bag of dot products

working 8 min There is no new operation here. There is the dot product from lesson 04, arranged so that many of them happen at once.

The one-minute version

  • A x has two readings that always agree: one dot product per row, and a weighted sum of the columns.
  • You need both. The row reading explains a layer, and the column reading is what lessons 09 and 10 are built on.
  • A B is just A applied to each column of B, so there is still only one operation.
  • Matrix multiplication is associative and not commutative, and for rectangular matrices the two orders often do not even have the same shape.

Two readings of the same product

A matrix is a rectangular array with two axes. Read it as a stack of rows, and each row is a vector. Read it as a set of columns, and each column is a vector. Both readings are correct and the product uses both.

The row reading: entry i of Ax is the dot product of row i of A with x. If A holds one house per row and x holds a price per unit of each feature, then entry i is the predicted price of house i. This is what a linear layer computes.

The column reading: Ax is a linear combination of the columns of A, weighted by the entries of x. The output is built out of the columns, so the set of outputs you can reach is limited by what the columns can build. Lesson 09 turns that sentence into the idea of a span, and lesson 10 counts it.

The shape rule

An m by p matrix times a p by n matrix gives an m by n matrix. The two inner dimensions must agree, because they are the length of the vectors being dotted together, and they vanish from the answer.

Matrix times matrix, and the two rules that matter

Once Ax is understood, AB needs nothing new: the columns of AB are A applied to each column of B, one at a time. That is the whole definition.

Two properties then follow, and they behave differently from ordinary multiplication.

The transpose of a product reverses the order

Claim. For any matrices A and B whose product is defined, (AB)T = BTAT.

Proof. Compare the two sides entry by entry. The (i, j) entry of the left-hand side is, by the definition of the transpose, the (j, i) entry of AB, which by the definition of the product is the sum over k of AjkBki. The (i, j) entry of the right-hand side is the sum over k of (BT)ik(AT)kj, which is the sum over k of BkiAjk. The two sums have the same terms, and multiplication of numbers commutes, so they are equal for every i and j. The shapes agree too: if A is m by p and B is p by n, both sides are n by m. ■

Multiplication does not commute

This is where the analogy with numbers breaks, and it breaks hard enough that the question is often not even well formed.

Take A of shape 2 by 3 and B of shape 3 by 2. Both products exist, because the inner dimensions match in both directions. But AB is 2 by 2 and BA is 3 by 3. They are not equal, and they could not have been: they are not even the same size.

What does still hold. Multiplication is associative, so (AB)C and A(BC) are the same matrix, and it distributes over addition. Associativity is the rule the next lesson turns into money: the two groupings give the same answer at wildly different cost.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. X is the housing data matrix, 20,000 rows by 5 columns. w is a price per unit of each feature, a vector of 5 numbers.
  2. Check the shapes. X is (20000, 5) and w is (5,), so the inner dimensions are 5 and 5. The product exists and has shape (20000,).
  3. Take the first house. Row 0 is (2.830, 4, 2, 35.07, 8210.5) and w = (232000, 43000, 21000, −900, 3.1).
  4. Do the row reading. The dot product is 656,560 + 172,000 + 42,000 − 31,563 + 25,452.55 = 864,449.55, one predicted price.
  5. Do the column reading on a smaller case. Taking six houses, scaling each of the five columns by its entry of w and adding the five scaled columns gives the same six numbers as six separate row dot products.
  6. Check them against each other. The program computes both and asserts they agree, and it agrees with NumPy's own product as well. Three routes, one answer.
  7. Scale it up. The full product is 20,000 dot products of length 5, which is 20,000 predictions from one expression.
  8. Say what would change. Stacking three different weight vectors as the columns of a 5 by 3 matrix W gives XW of shape (20000, 3): three whole models evaluated in one product, at the cost of one loop instead of three.
code/0064-matrix-times-vector.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/housing.csv")
X = frame[["area_k_sqft", "bedrooms", "bathrooms", "age_years", "lot_sqft"]].to_numpy(float)
w = np.array([232_000.0, 43_000.0, 21_000.0, -900.0, 3.1])

by_rows = np.array([X[i] @ w for i in range(6)])          # one dot product per row
by_columns = sum(w[j] * X[:6, j] for j in range(5))       # a weighted sum of columns
assert np.allclose(by_rows, by_columns)
assert np.allclose(by_rows, X[:6] @ w)                    # and numpy agrees

A = np.array([[-1.5, 3.0, 2.0], [1.0, -1.0, 0.0]])
B = np.array([[-1.0, -1.0], [0.0, -2.0], [1.0, 0.0]])
print((A @ B).shape, (B @ A).shape)                       # (2, 2) (3, 3)

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A is 2 by 3 and B is 3 by 2. Which statement is true?
The inner dimensions match in both directions, so both products exist: AB is 2 by 2 and BA is 3 by 3. The first two each forget one of the two directions. The fourth is the commutativity error, and here the two results are not even the same size, so equality is not a question that can be asked.
In Ax, what does the column reading say the product is?
The entries of x are the weights and the columns of A are what they weight, which is the reading lessons 09 and 10 need in order to talk about what a matrix can reach. The second swaps rows for columns and describes nothing. The third is one entry of the answer rather than the whole of it. The fourth is a different product with a different shape.
Practice

Let A = [[−1.5, 3, 2], [1, −1, 0]] and B = [[−1, −1], [0, −2], [1, 0]].

  1. Give the shapes of AB and BA without computing either.
  2. Compute AB in full.
  3. Without computing BA in full, give the entry in its first row and first column.
  4. Use the transpose rule to give (AB)T without transposing your answer to part 2, then check that it matches.
Hint

For part 3, an entry of a product needs exactly one row of the left matrix and one column of the right one. For part 4 the rule is that the transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in the opposite order.

Solution
  1. AB is 2 by 2 and BA is 3 by 3: the outer dimensions survive and the inner 3 and 2 are consumed.
  2. Row 1: (−1.5)(−1) + 3(0) + 2(1) = 3.5, and (−1.5)(−1) + 3(−2) + 2(0) = −4.5. Row 2: 1(−1) + (−1)(0) + 0(1) = −1, and 1(−1) + (−1)(−2) + 0(0) = 1. So AB = [[3.5, −4.5], [−1, 1]].
  3. Row 1 of B is (−1, −1) and column 1 of A is (−1.5, 1), so the entry is 1.5 − 1 = 0.5.
  4. BT is 2 by 3 and AT is 3 by 2, so BTAT is 2 by 2. Its first row is (−1, 0, 1) against the columns of AT: 1.5 + 0 + 2 = 3.5, and 1 + 0 + 0 = −1. Its second row is (−1, −2, 0): 1.5 − 6 + 0 = −4.5, and 1 + 2 + 0 = 1. That gives [[3.5, −1], [−4.5, 1]], which is exactly part 2's answer with rows and columns swapped.

Sanity check: in part 1, if your two shapes were the same you have made an error, because the inner dimensions here are different numbers.

Your teacher. If the column reading has not landed, ask an agent to take a 3 by 3 matrix and compute Ax for x equal to (1, 0, 0), then (0, 1, 0), then (0, 0, 1), printing the answer each time. Each one returns a column of A, and after three of those the reading is hard to un-see. It is also exactly the trick lesson 07 opens with.
