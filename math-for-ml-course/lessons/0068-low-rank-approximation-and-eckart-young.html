The truncation that is provably best

frontier 15 min A theorem with a proof, and a measurement that agrees with it to twelve decimals.

The one-minute version

  • Cut the SVD's rank-one sum after k terms and you get Ak, a rank-k matrix.
  • No rank-k matrix anywhere is closer to A, in either the spectral or the Frobenius norm.
  • The error is exactly what you discarded: σk+1 in one norm, the root of the tail sum in the other.
  • Choosing k by a cliff in the spectrum beats choosing it by a percentage.
  • The guarantee needs a fully observed matrix. Missing entries break it, and filling them in does not repair it.

1. The question

The measurement matrix in this course is 8,000 rows by 24 channels, which is 192,000 numbers. Lesson 66 showed its singular values collapse after the fourth. So: how few numbers can be kept while still having something that is recognisably the same matrix?

Truncate the rank-one sum from lesson 66 after k terms.

Ak = σ1u1v1T + … + σkukvkT Read as: the first k layers of the sum and none of the rest. It has rank at most k by construction, because it is built from k rank-one pieces.

That is a rank-k matrix near A. The question this page answers is whether it is the nearest one, which there is no reason yet to believe.

2. Two ways to measure "near"

The answer depends on what distance means, so name two. The spectral norm ‖M‖2 is the largest singular value of M, which lesson 67 showed is the worst stretch it applies to any unit vector. The Frobenius norm ‖M‖F is the square root of the sum of every squared entry, which treats the matrix as one long vector.

3. The theorem, and its proof

Eckart-Young-Mirsky For every matrix B of rank at most k, ‖A - B‖2 ≥ σk+1 = ‖A - Ak‖2. So Ak is a closest rank-k matrix to A.

Move 1, the value Ak achieves. The difference A - Ak is the leftover sum ∑i>kσiuiviT. That is already an SVD, with orthonormal vectors and decreasing weights, so its largest singular value is σk+1. By lesson 67 the spectral norm is the largest singular value, so ‖A - Ak‖2 = σk+1.

Move 2, suppose something did better. Take B of rank at most k with ‖A - B‖2 < σk+1.

Move 3, count dimensions. Because B has rank at most k, the set of vectors it sends to zero has dimension at least n - k. Separately, let W be the span of v1, …, vk+1, which has dimension k + 1. Two subspaces of an n-dimensional space whose dimensions add to at least n + 1 must share a non-zero vector. Take such an x and scale it to unit length. Now bound ‖Ax‖ twice.

‖Ax‖ = ‖(A - B)x‖ ≤ ‖A - B‖2 < σk+1 Read as: the upper bound. Because x is killed by B, subtracting Bx changes nothing, and then the spectral norm bounds how much A - B can stretch a unit vector.
‖Ax‖2 = ∑i≤k+1 ci2σi2 ≥ σk+12 Read as: the lower bound. Because x lies in W it is a combination of the first k+1 right singular vectors with coefficients ci whose squares sum to one. Every σi appearing is at least σk+1, so the weighted average cannot fall below it.

The two bounds contradict each other, so no such B exists.

The step that does the real work Move 3's dimension count. Everything else is bookkeeping about norms. The entire force of the theorem is that a rank-k matrix must annihilate an (n-k)-dimensional subspace, and such a subspace cannot avoid a (k+1)-dimensional one inside n dimensions. The pigeonhole is the proof.

4. The Frobenius half

The error formula in the other norm is easy, and worth seeing because it is what compression metrics report. The matrices uiviT are orthonormal under the Frobenius inner product, so the squared norm of their weighted sum is the sum of the squared weights.

‖A - Ak‖F = √(σk+12 + … + σr2) Read as: the error in the Frobenius norm is the root of the sum of the squares of every singular value you threw away. The spectral norm keeps only the largest of them; this one keeps all of them.
The honest boundary. That Ak is also optimal in the Frobenius norm is true and is not proved here. The argument needs an inequality relating the singular values of A - B to those of A, which is more machinery than this module carries. The result holds for every unitarily invariant norm, which is why the theorem carries three names. What section 3 proved is the spectral case.

5. Watching theory and measurement agree

6. Choosing k, and what the percentage does not mean

Define energy kept as the share of squared singular values retained. On this dataset:

  1. k = 1. 69.98 percent kept, 8,025 numbers stored, a 23.9 times saving.
  2. k = 2. 91.47 percent, 16,050 numbers, 12.0 times.
  3. k = 3. 96.82 percent, 24,075 numbers, 8.0 times.
  4. k = 4. 98.16 percent, 32,100 numbers, 6.0 times.
  5. k = 5. 98.26 percent. One more component bought a tenth of a percent.
  6. k = 6. 98.36 percent. The curve has gone flat.
  7. Where the elbow is. Between 4 and 5, which is where the singular values drop by a factor of 3.65 into a floor whose twenty values have mean 1.784 and standard deviation 0.046.
  8. The honest caveat. That flat floor is the signature of independent noise, and this dataset was generated with exactly four components. The elbow is designed in, not discovered, and a page that used it as evidence about the world would be lying.

The storage arithmetic is worth stating plainly: rank-k costs k(m + n + 1) numbers against mn, so the saving only exists when k is well under mn/(m+n). For an 8,000 by 24 matrix that threshold is about 24, so every k here wins. For a nearly square matrix it would not.

7. Where this is the whole technique

Three named uses. Image and signal compression store the truncated factors instead of the pixels. Latent semantic analysis truncates a term-document matrix and calls the surviving directions topics. And low-rank adaptation freezes a large pretrained weight matrix and trains only a rank-r correction to it, which is this idea used deliberately rather than as a compromise; the hub covers it in LoRA, in the LLM papers course.

The limit that matters most. "Best" here means best over a fully observed matrix in these norms. Change either and the theorem does not apply. A ratings matrix where most entries are missing is the standard case where it does not, and filling the blanks in does not recover the guarantee: it produces the best approximation to the matrix you invented, which is not the one you wanted.

8. Worked in full

  1. Take the matrix from lesson 66. A = [[3, 2, 2], [2, 3, -2]], whose SVD was found by hand.
  2. Recall it. σ1 = 5 with u1 = (1, 1)/√2 and v1 = (1, 1, 0)/√2; σ2 = 3.
  3. Predict the error before computing it. The theorem says the best rank-one approximation must be wrong by exactly σ2 = 3 in the spectral norm. Write that down first.
  4. Build it. u1v1T is (1/2)[[1, 1, 0], [1, 1, 0]], so A1 = [[2.5, 2.5, 0], [2.5, 2.5, 0]].
  5. Form the residual. A - A1 = [[0.5, -0.5, 2], [-0.5, 0.5, -2]].
  6. Measure it. Frobenius: √(0.25 + 0.25 + 4 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 4) = √9 = 3. Spectral: also 3, because the residual has rank one and the two norms coincide there.
  7. Sanity check. The prediction in step 3 was 3 and the measurement is 3. Independently, ‖A‖F2 = 34 and ‖A1‖F2 = 25, and 34 - 25 = 9 = 32. That is the statement that the kept and discarded parts are perpendicular. If your residual fails ‖A‖2 = ‖Ak‖2 + ‖A - Ak‖2, the truncation was built wrong.
  8. What changes if. Go to k = 2. Nothing is discarded, because A has rank 2, so A2 = A exactly and the error is zero. Approximating a matrix that is already low rank costs nothing at all.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A matrix has singular values 10, 8, 1 and 0.5. You truncate at k = 2. What is the spectral-norm error?
The spectral error is exactly sigma k plus one, which is the largest value you discarded, here 1. Option 3 is the correct Frobenius error, the root of 1 plus 0.25, so it answers a different question and is the distractor worth understanding. Option 1 takes the smallest discarded value instead of the largest, and option 4 is neither norm.
Is it true that keeping more singular values always gives a better result?
It always reduces the reconstruction error of this particular matrix, by the error formula, but on a noisy measurement the extra components fit the noise, and on a matrix with missing entries the guarantee does not hold at all. Option 1 states a true fact and stops one step short of the question. Option 2 conflates fitting with being useful. Option 4 is false: past the rank the error is exactly zero and stays there.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A 100 by 40 matrix has singular values whose squares sum to 500, and the first three squared values are 300, 90 and 40. Compute the energy kept at k = 3, the Frobenius error, and the storage ratio against holding every entry.

Hint

Energy is a ratio of sums of squares, and the error is the square root of what is left over. Storage for a rank-k factorisation is k(m + n + 1).

Show the worked solution
  1. Add the kept squares. 300 + 90 + 40 = 430.
  2. Energy kept. 430 ÷ 500 = 0.86, so 86 percent.
  3. Frobenius error. √(500 - 430) = √70 = 8.3666.
  4. Storage. 3 × (100 + 40 + 1) = 423 numbers against 100 × 40 = 4000, a ratio of 9.46.

Sanity check. Kept energy and discarded energy must add to exactly one: 0.86 + 70/500 = 0.86 + 0.14 = 1.00. If they do not, a value was used where a squared value belonged. And the error must be smaller than the Frobenius norm of the whole matrix, which is √500 = 22.36. It is.

Your teacher. If the dimension count in move 3 is the part that will not settle, ask an agent to make it concrete in three dimensions: pick k = 1, so B kills a plane and W is a plane, and two planes in three dimensions always meet in at least a line. That line is the vector the proof needs.
