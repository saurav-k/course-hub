Maximum likelihood picks the parameter that makes your data least surprising

working 12 min The likelihood, why the log is not optional, and the Bernoulli derivation in full. · code/0162

The one-minute version

  • You know how to compute P(data | parameter). Slide the parameter until that number is as big as it goes.
  • That is the likelihood, and the winner is the maximum likelihood estimate.
  • Take the log. It does not move the answer and it turns a product that underflows to exactly zero into a sum that does not.
  • For a Bernoulli the answer is k/n, and this page derives it in five lines.
  • A likelihood is not a distribution over the parameter. Integrated over p it comes to 1.86e-13, not 1, and reading its height as "how probable this parameter is" is the commonest misreading in the module.

1. Flip the conditional you already know how to compute

Module 07 spent fifteen pages on P(data | parameter). Give it a coin's bias and it tells you how probable seven heads in ten tosses is. That direction is settled.

Estimation wants the other direction, and maximum likelihood gets there without needing it. Keep the same expression, stop treating the parameter as fixed, and start treating the data as fixed. You saw what you saw. Ask which parameter would have made that outcome most probable, and answer with that one.

L(θ) = P(the data you saw | θ), read as a function of θ θ (theta): the parameter, now the thing that varies. the data: fixed, because it already happened. L: the likelihood. It is the same formula as the probability, with the roles of what-is-known and what-varies swapped, and that swap is the whole idea.
The reframing Nothing new is computed. The likelihood is the probability formula you already have, read along a different axis. What changes is which symbol you slide.

2. The recipe, in three steps

  1. Write the probability of the whole dataset. If the observations are independent, that is the product of the per-observation probabilities.
  2. Take the log. The product becomes a sum, and the argmax does not move.
  3. Maximise. Usually by differentiating and setting to zero - though Lesson 163 shows a case where that step is wrong.
Step three is not always "differentiate". For the distributions on Lesson 163 it usually is, and once it is not. That page works the Bernoulli algebra under the curve in section 5, then the Poisson, then a case where the derivative has nothing to do.

Two onward roads, named and not taken: under mild conditions the MLE is asymptotically normal, which is what lets Lesson 166 put an interval round it, and the Cramer-Rao bound sets a floor on any unbiased estimator's variance, which the MLE attains in the limit.

3. Why the log is not a convenience

The log is monotone, so it cannot move an argmax: if L(a) > L(b) then log L(a) > log L(b). That is the licence. The reason you actually need it is arithmetic.

Measured on the module's session log: at 10 observations the raw product is 8.34e-04, at 200 it is 1.37e-58, at 700 it is 3.45e-206, and at 2,000 it is 0.000000e+00. The log column reads −7.09, −133.24, −473.09 and −1359.79.

And the argmax really does not move. On the first 120 rows, where the raw product is still representable, the argmax of L and the argmax of log L are both 0.425000, which is also k/n on those rows.

4. Worked: the returning-visitor rate

The session log has n = 20,000 rows, of which k = 8,238 are returning visitors.

  1. Apply the derivation. p-hat = k/n = 8,238 ÷ 20,000 = 0.411900.
  2. Evaluate the log-likelihood there. l(0.4119) = −13,550.85.
  3. Try a candidate to the left. l(0.380) = −13,593.61, which is 42.76 worse.
  4. Try one to the right. l(0.450) = −13,609.87, which is 59.01 worse.
  5. Go further out. l(0.200) = −15,883.16, worse by 2,332.31, and l(0.700) = −17,099.42, worse by 3,548.56.
  6. Read the shape. Every candidate is below the peak and the curve falls away steeply on both sides, which is what "maximum" means and what makes the estimate well determined.

Sanity check. Every candidate's log-likelihood must be negative, since it is the log of a probability, and every one must be below the value at k/n. Both hold. If a candidate had beaten k/n, the derivation on Lesson 163 would be wrong.

What changes if you have ten rows instead of twenty thousand. The peak stays at k/n but the curve becomes far flatter, so parameters well away from the estimate are nearly as good an explanation of the data. The estimate is unchanged and your confidence in it is not - and that gap between "the answer" and "how sure the answer is" is exactly what Lesson 160's standard error measures and what the interval pages formalise.

5. What maximum likelihood does not give you

A likelihood is not a probability distribution over the parameter. Integrate L(p) over p from 0 to 1 on 40 observations and you get 1.86e-13, not 1, and nothing makes it 1. The curve is a density in the data, read as a function of p. Reading its height as "how probable this p is" is the commonest misreading in this module, and giving the curve a genuine probability interpretation is precisely what Lesson 165 does by multiplying in a prior.

Two more honest limits. Maximum likelihood returns a point, not a range: it has no opinion about how sure you should be, and the interval pages supply that separately. And it has no opinion about what you believed beforehand: it listens only to the data, which is a virtue when the data is plentiful and a liability when it is thin.

6. Where this lands in machine learning

The Bernoulli derivation on Lesson 163, applied per example with p produced by a model rather than fitted directly, is the training objective of logistic regression. Lesson 164 pays that off by showing the same three steps producing squared error and log loss from two different distributional assumptions.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

The likelihood curve for p peaks at 0.41 with a height of 1.4e-58. What does that height tell you about the probability that p is 0.41?
The height is P(data | p = 0.41), a statement about the data. The curve does not integrate to 1 over p and never will, so it carries no probability for p at all. The first two options both read the curve as a distribution over the parameter, which is the misreading this page names. The third overshoots: the height is meaningful, it just answers the reverse question, and comparing heights across candidate p is exactly what the method does.
Why take the log of the likelihood before maximising it?
Monotonicity is the licence and underflow is the reason: at 2,000 observations the raw product is exactly zero and every candidate ties. The second option is the misreading from the previous question - the log changes nothing about normalisation. The third invents a bias correction that does not exist. The fourth is false: the raw likelihood is perfectly differentiable, it is just numerically useless.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You observe 7 successes in 10 independent trials.

Write down the likelihood and the log-likelihood, derive the maximum likelihood estimate from scratch, and evaluate the log-likelihood at your answer and at p = 0.5. Then say what the gap between the two tells you and what it does not.

Hint

Do not reuse k/n from section 3 - rebuild it. Differentiate k log p + (n−k) log(1−p) and set it to zero. For the last part, remember what the height of the curve is a statement about.

Show the worked solution
  1. The likelihood. L(p) = p7(1−p)3.
  2. The log-likelihood. l(p) = 7 log p + 3 log(1−p).
  3. Differentiate. l′(p) = 7/p − 3/(1−p).
  4. Set to zero. 7(1−p) = 3p, so 7 = 10p, so p-hat = 0.7.
  5. Evaluate at the estimate. l(0.7) = 7 log 0.7 + 3 log 0.3 = 7(−0.3567) + 3(−1.2040) = −2.497 − 3.612 = −6.109.
  6. Evaluate at a half. l(0.5) = 10 log 0.5 = −6.931.
  7. Read the gap. 0.823 in log units, so the data was e0.823 = 2.28 times more probable under p = 0.7 than under p = 0.5. That is a statement about the data. It does not say p = 0.7 is 2.28 times more probable than p = 0.5 - that would need a prior, and it is Lesson 165.

Sanity check. The estimate must be the observed proportion, 0.7, and both log-likelihoods must be negative with the one at 0.7 the larger. Both hold. Notice also how small the gap is: ten trials barely distinguish 0.7 from 0.5, which is section 4's flat-curve point arriving as a number.

Your teacher. The habit here is saying the conditional out loud before you use the number. "This is how probable the data was, given the parameter" keeps the likelihood on the right side of the bar, and almost every confusion on this page is that sentence said backwards. Ask an agent to plot a likelihood for 7 of 10 and for 700 of 1,000, and notice that the peak does not move while the curve's width collapses.
