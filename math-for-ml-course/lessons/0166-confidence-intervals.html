The ninety-five per cent belongs to the rule, not to your interval

working 14 min The first page of the testing half of module 09.

The one-minute version

  • The shape is always the same: estimate, plus or minus a critical value times a standard error.
  • The three z values worth knowing: 1.645, 1.96 and 2.576 for 90, 95 and 99 per cent.
  • The 95 per cent is a property of the procedure across repetitions, not of the interval in front of you.
  • Once you have looked, your interval either contains the parameter or it does not. There is no probability left in it.
  • Substituting the estimate into the standard error makes it a Wald interval, which misbehaves when the rate is near 0 or 1.

1. The picture the definition is about

Take a population whose true rate you happen to know, draw a fresh sample, and build an interval. Then do it again, nineteen more times. The programme that ships with this page does exactly that against the sessions data, where the returning rate over all 20,000 rows is 0.4119.

2. The mental model

Before you draw the sample, the interval is random: its endpoints are functions of data you have not seen, and the probability that it will cover the parameter is 0.95. After you draw it, the endpoints are two numbers on your screen and the parameter is one fixed number, so the interval either covers it or it does not. Nothing is left to be probable about.

The reframing A confidence interval is not a range the parameter is probably in. It is the output of a rule that would be right ninety-five times in a hundred if you kept using it. Lesson 171 is where the other sentence, the one you actually wanted, becomes available, and it shows what that sentence costs.

3. The formula, and the theorem under it

p̂ ± z √( p̂(1 - p̂) / n ) Read as: p̂ is the proportion observed in the sample, n is the sample size, and z is the critical value taken from the standard normal, which is 1.96 for 95 per cent. The square root is the estimated standard error of the proportion.
Theorem The Wald interval for a proportion has asymptotic coverage 1 - α: as n grows, the probability that the interval covers the true p tends to 1 - α.

What is assumed, in words first: the n observations are independent and each is a success with the same probability p. Nothing else. The shape of the argument is three moves: standardise the estimator so the central limit theorem applies, read a probability off the standard normal, then rearrange the inequality inside that probability so that p is in the middle rather than at the edge.

Step 1. By the central limit theorem, quoted from module 08,

Z = (p̂ - p) / √(p(1-p)/n) → Normal(0, 1) Read as: subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation turns the sample proportion into a quantity whose distribution settles down to the standard normal as n grows, whatever p is.

Step 2. So P(|Z| ≤ z) → 1 - α with z the 1 - α/2 quantile.

Step 3, and this is the step that does the real work. The event |Z| ≤ z is the same event as |p̂ - p| ≤ z√(p(1-p)/n), and that is the same event as p lying within that distance of p̂. The inequality has not changed; only which symbol is written in the middle has. That rearrangement is the whole trick, and it is why an interval built around the estimate can carry a guarantee about the parameter.

The honest boundary. Step 3 leaves the unknown p inside the standard error, and the practical formula substitutes p̂ for it. That substitution is an extra approximation on top of the central limit theorem, and it is what makes this the Wald interval rather than an exact one. It behaves badly when p is near 0 or 1, or when n is small: at zero successes it collapses to the single point zero, which lesson 171 shows next to an interval that does not.

4. Does the guarantee actually hold?

At n = 50 the measured coverage is 0.9384, below the nominal 0.95: the normal approximation has not fully arrived. By n = 800 it is 0.9523. Meanwhile the width goes 0.2699, 0.1361, 0.0682, 0.0341 as n quadruples each time, which is the 1/√n in the formula made visible.

5. Why 1.96, and when it is not 1.96

Using 2 instead of 1.96 widens the interval by about 2 per cent, which is usually harmless and is worth knowing you are doing. The change that is not harmless is z against t. When the standard deviation is itself estimated and n is small, the critical value comes from the t distribution with n - 1 degrees of freedom.

6. Worked in full

A landing page is shown to 800 visitors and 340 of them return.

  1. Compute the estimate. p̂ = 340 / 800 = 0.425.
  2. Compute the standard error. √(0.425 × 0.575 / 800) = √(0.244375 / 800) = √0.000305469 = 0.017478.
  3. Choose the critical value. 95 per cent two-sided, a proportion, 800 observations, so z = 1.96.
  4. Compute the half-width. 1.96 × 0.017478 = 0.034257.
  5. Assemble. 0.425 ± 0.034257, so [0.390743, 0.459257].
  6. Sanity check. The half-width should be roughly 1/√800 = 0.0354 scaled by √(p̂(1-p̂))= 0.494, giving about 0.0175 before the 1.96, and 0.0343 after. It matches, so no factor has been dropped. And the interval must lie inside [0, 1], which it does.
  7. What changes if. Quadruple the sample to 3,200 with the same rate. The standard error halves to 0.008739 and the interval narrows to [0.407872, 0.442128]. Four times the data bought twice the precision, not four times.
  8. What it means here. The data is compatible with a true return rate anywhere from about 39 to about 46 per cent. If the decision you are about to make would change at 42 per cent, this sample cannot make it for you.

7. Two intervals that overlap

Overlapping intervals do not mean no difference. Two 95 per cent intervals can overlap while a proper test of the difference still rejects, because the interval for a difference has its own standard error and it is not the sum of the two widths. Lesson 167 does exactly that comparison on real numbers, and finds the two readings agreeing on the verdict while only the test reports how close it was.

8. The programme

code/0166-confidence-intervals.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
# the guarantee is about the rule, so measure the rule
covered = 0
for _ in range(repeats):
    sample = rng.choice(population, size=n, replace=False)
    lo, hi = wald_interval(sample.mean(), n, z=1.96)
    covered += (lo <= p_true <= hi)
print(covered / repeats)        # -> 0.9523 at n = 800

Run it against datasets/sessions.csv and the measured coverage lands within a few thousandths of the promise at every size above 50. The loop is the definition: it is the only way to see a property of a procedure, because no single interval can show it to you.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your 95 per cent interval for a rate is [0.39, 0.46]. What does the 95 per cent describe?
The guarantee attaches to the procedure across repetitions. Once the interval is computed it either contains the rate or it does not. Option 1 is the misreading this page exists to prevent, and it is the sentence a credible interval is allowed to say. Option 3 describes a prediction interval, a different object. Option 4 describes reproducibility, which no confidence interval reports.
You want to halve the width of a proportion interval. What happens to the sample size?
The standard error carries a square root, so precision improves with the root of the sample and four times the data buys twice the precision. The measured widths on this page go 0.2699, 0.1361, 0.0682, 0.0341 as n quadruples, which is that rule. Option 1 assumes a linear relationship, option 2 inverts it, and option 3 confuses the centre of the interval with its width.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Of 400 users shown a new checkout, 96 completed a purchase. Build a 95 per cent confidence interval for the completion rate, then say what would have to change for the interval to exclude 20 per cent.

Hint

Estimate first, then standard error, then critical value, then half-width. For the second part, ask how far 0.20 sits from your estimate in units of the standard error.

Show the worked solution
  1. Estimate. p̂ = 96/400 = 0.24.
  2. Standard error. √(0.24 × 0.76 / 400) = √0.000456 = 0.021354.
  3. Half-width. 1.96 × 0.021354 = 0.041854.
  4. Interval. 0.24 ± 0.041854, so [0.198146, 0.281854].
  5. Does it exclude 0.20? No, only just: 0.20 sits inside by about 0.0019.
  6. What would change it. 0.20 is (0.24 - 0.20)/0.021354 = 1.873 standard errors from the estimate, and it needs to be past 1.96. Holding the rate fixed, the standard error must fall by the factor 1.873/1.96 = 0.9556, which needs n = 400/0.95562 = 438 users.

Sanity check. The interval must sit inside [0, 1] and be centred on 0.24, and it is. A half-width near 0.042 on 400 observations is the right order: at this rate the rule of thumb 1/√n = 0.05 scaled by √(0.24 × 0.76) = 0.427 gives 0.021 before the 1.96. If you got a half-width above 0.1, the sample size probably went in un-square-rooted.

Your teacher. If the difference between the two sentences in section 2 still feels like word play, ask an agent to simulate one thousand intervals and print how many covered, then ask it which particular interval among them has a 95 per cent chance of covering. The question has no answer, and finding that out is the point.
