Orthogonality and projection: the closest point you can actually reach

working 10 min When the target is out of reach, the best available answer is the one whose error is perpendicular to everything you can reach.

The one-minute version

  • Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero, which is a right angle in any number of dimensions.
  • The closest point in a subspace to a target is the one whose residual is orthogonal to the whole subspace. Nothing else is closer, and Pythagoras is why.
  • Orthonormal is stronger than orthogonal: unit length as well as right angles. An orthogonal matrix needs the stronger one.
  • For an orthogonal matrix, the inverse is the transpose, and the map changes no length and no angle.

The problem, and the answer

Lesson 48 ended with a target sitting off the column space, unreachable by any input. The sensible thing to do is not to give up but to ask for the reachable point that comes closest. That point has a name, the projection, and it has a defining property that is easier to use than "closest".

The orthogonality principle: perpendicular is closest

What is assumed. S is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of A, which lesson 48 called the column space. p is a point of S whose residual r = b − p is orthogonal to every vector in S. q is any other point of S.

The shape of the argument. Split the error at q into two pieces, one of which is the error at p and the other of which lies in S. Those two pieces are perpendicular, so Pythagoras applies, so the error at q is longer.

The step that does the real work is the splitting, and it is a piece of pure bookkeeping: subtract and add p.

||b − q||2 = ||(b − p) + (p − q)||2 = ||b − p||2 + 2(b − p)T(p − q) + ||p − q||2 Read as: b is the target, p the perpendicular candidate and q any rival. The first equality adds and subtracts p, which changes nothing. The second expands the squared norm of a sum exactly as lesson 43 did, using ||x||2 = xTx.

Now look at the middle term. Both p and q lie in S, and S is closed under subtraction, so p − q lies in S too. The residual b − p was assumed orthogonal to everything in S, so that inner product is zero and the whole middle term vanishes. What is left is

||b − q||2 = ||b − p||2 + ||p − q||2 Read as: the squared error at the rival equals the squared error at the projection plus the squared distance between the two candidates. Every term is a squared length and so cannot be negative.

The last term is never negative, and it is zero only when q = p. So every other point of S is strictly further from b, and the perpendicular candidate is the unique closest one. ■

This also proves something the picture only suggested: the projection is unique. There is one closest point, not a set of equally good ones.

The reframing "Which point is nearest" is a minimisation over infinitely many candidates. "Which point has a perpendicular residual" is a handful of equations, one per column of A. The theorem above is what licenses trading the first question for the second, and lesson 51 does exactly that trade.

Orthogonal, orthonormal, and the matrix that needs both

Three terms sit close together here and only one of them is about matrices.

Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero. A set is orthonormal when its members are mutually orthogonal and every one has length 1. An orthogonal matrix is a square matrix whose columns are orthonormal, and whose rows then are too.

The naming is genuinely bad and it catches people. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville stop to warn about it: "Pay careful attention to the definition of orthogonal matrices. Counterintuitively, their rows are not merely orthogonal but fully orthonormal. There is no special term for a matrix whose rows or columns are orthogonal but not orthonormal." Scaling all three columns of an orthogonal matrix by 3 keeps every angle at 90 degrees and stops it being an orthogonal matrix.

The payoff is worth the naming trouble. Orthonormal columns mean QTQ = I, because entry (i, j) of that product is the dot product of column i with column j, which is 1 when they are the same column and 0 otherwise. For a square Q that says the transpose is the inverse.

QTQ = QQT = I,  so  Q−1 = QT Read as: Q is a square matrix with orthonormal columns, QT is its transpose and I is the identity. The inverse of Q is obtained by swapping its rows and columns, which lesson 45 counted as costing zero flops.

Lesson 47 spent a page on how expensive an inverse is. For this one family of matrices it is free, and that is why rotations, reflections and the Q in a QR factorisation are everywhere in numerical work. A rotation also changes no length and no inner product, which the program confirms on 12,000 real sensor readings at once.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. a1 = (2, 3, 1) and a2 = (1, 4, 2) span a plane, and b = (1, 1, 1) is the target, which lesson 48 showed is not on it.
  2. State what is wanted. Coefficients c1 and c2 making p = c1a1 + c2a2 as close to b as possible.
  3. Use the theorem instead of the definition. Require the residual to be perpendicular to both spanning vectors: a1T(b − p) = 0 and a2T(b − p) = 0. Two equations, two unknowns.
  4. Solve them. The coefficients come out as c1 = 0.368421 and c2 = 0.052632.
  5. Build the projection. 0.368421×(2, 3, 1) + 0.052632×(1, 4, 2) = (0.789474, 1.315789, 0.473684).
  6. Take the residual. b − p = (0.210526, −0.315789, 0.526316).
  7. Check perpendicularity by hand. a1 · r = 2(0.210526) + 3(−0.315789) + 1(0.526316) = 0.421052 − 0.947367 + 0.526316 = 0. And a2 · r = 0.210526 − 1.263156 + 1.052632 = 0.
  8. Say what would change. If b had been on the plane, the residual would be the zero vector and the projection would be b itself, which is the case lesson 47 called "exactly one solution".

Sanity check. Both dot products in step 7 must come out to zero, not to something small, because they are the equations that were solved in step 3. A residual of 10−3 means the coefficients are wrong; a residual of 10−16 is floating point and is fine. And 20,000 random challengers drawn near the projection failed to beat it, every one.

What it means. The projection is the best prediction a model of this shape can make. The residual is not a mistake to be fixed by trying harder: it is the part of the target that lives outside everything the model can express, and no choice of coefficients will ever reach it. That is a statement about the model's capacity, and lesson 49 is what counts it.

code/0050-orthogonality-and-projection.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

a1, a2 = np.array([2., 3., 1.]), np.array([1., 4., 2.])
A = np.column_stack([a1, a2])
b = np.array([1., 1., 1.])

c = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b)      # perpendicularity, as two equations
p = A @ c
r = b - p
print(np.round(c, 6), np.round(p, 6))      # [0.368421 0.052632] [0.789474 1.315789 0.473684]
print(a1 @ r, a2 @ r)                      # both zero

The full program also confirms that 20,000 random points in the plane are all further away, checks that the projection matrix is symmetric and idempotent with trace equal to the dimension it projects onto, and rotates 12,000 sensor readings to show that lengths and inner products both survive unchanged.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What is the defining property of the best approximation inside a subspace?
The closest point is the one whose residual is orthogonal to the whole subspace, and Pythagoras turns that into a proof that nothing else is nearer. The second describes an exact solution, which is the case where the target was reachable and is not the interesting one. The third is false: some coefficients can be zero. The fourth confuses the projection with the origin, which is the shortest vector in any subspace.
A square matrix Q satisfies QTQ = I. Which claim follows?
Q transpose Q = I says the columns are orthonormal, and for a square matrix that makes the transpose the inverse. The first is too weak and is the classic trap: orthogonal columns alone would give a diagonal product, not the identity, because the diagonal entries would be the squared lengths. The second is impossible for an invertible matrix. The fourth has nothing to do with orthogonality.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Let a1 = (2, 3, 1), a2 = (1, 4, 2) and c = (1, 1, 1).

  1. Why can c not be written exactly as a combination of the two?
  2. Set up the equations for the closest point in their span, using only perpendicularity.
  3. The solution is c1 = 0.368421 and c2 = 0.052632. Compute the projection and the residual.
  4. Verify perpendicularity with two dot products, and say what it would have meant if only one of them came out zero.
Hint

For part 2 you do not need to minimise anything. Write the residual as c − c1a1 − c2a2 and demand that its dot product with each spanning vector is zero. That is two equations in the two unknowns.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the span. a1 and a2 are independent, so their span is a plane through the origin in three-dimensional space, and lesson 48 showed c is not on it.
  2. Write the two conditions. a1 · (c − c1a1 − c2a2) = 0 and the same with a2. Expanded, that is 14c1 + 16c2 = 6 and 16c1 + 21c2 = 7.
  3. Build the projection. 0.368421×(2, 3, 1) = (0.736842, 1.105263, 0.368421), and 0.052632×(1, 4, 2) = (0.052632, 0.210526, 0.105263). Adding: p = (0.789474, 1.315789, 0.473684).
  4. Take the residual. r = (1 − 0.789474, 1 − 1.315789, 1 − 0.473684) = (0.210526, −0.315789, 0.526316).
  5. First dot product. 2(0.210526) + 3(−0.315789) + 1(0.526316) = 0.421052 − 0.947367 + 0.526316 = 0.
  6. Second dot product. 1(0.210526) + 4(−0.315789) + 2(0.526316) = 0.210526 − 1.263156 + 1.052632 = 0.
  7. Answer the last part. One zero and one non-zero would mean the residual is perpendicular to one spanning vector but not to the plane, so it is not the projection. Both conditions are needed, and in general one per spanning vector.

Sanity check. The projection must be shorter than c, because it is c with a perpendicular piece removed: ||p|| = 1.6116 against ||c|| = 1.7321. And Pythagoras must hold: ||p||2 + ||r||2 = 2.5972 + 0.4028 = 3 = ||c||2.

Your teacher. If the theorem still feels like an assertion, ask an agent to take your projection and generate a few thousand random points in the same plane, then print the smallest error any of them achieved. It will never beat yours, and watching it fail a few thousand times is a more persuasive experience than the algebra for most people.
