Every Gaussian is a standard one that has been stretched and turned

working 13 min The formula looks forbidding. Every piece of it is the covariance matrix doing geometry.

The one-minute version

  • The exponent is squared Mahalanobis distance: how many standard deviations out you are, measured in the cloud's own geometry.
  • The constant in front carries the square root of the determinant, which is the volume of the ellipse, so a wider cloud has a lower peak.
  • Any Gaussian can be written x = μ + Bz with z standard normal, so drawing one is stretching, turning and shifting standard noise.
  • That identity, written so a gradient can pass through it, is the reparameterisation trick.

1. The mental model: start from one dimension and widen it

Put the two densities side by side and only one thing changes. In one dimension the exponent holds a squared distance divided by a variance. In several dimensions it holds a quadratic form built from the covariance matrix, and that form is doing exactly the same job.

2. The mechanism, term by term

p(x⃗) = (2π)-d/2 · det(Σ)-1/2 · exp( -½ (x⃗ - μ⃗)T Σ-1 (x⃗ - μ⃗) ) Read as: the density at a point x. d is how many dimensions, μ is the centre, and Σ is the covariance matrix. The first two factors do not depend on x at all and exist only to make the whole thing integrate to one. The exponent is the only part that changes as you move, and it is the quadratic form from the previous page with a minus one half in front.

The exponent has a name. It is the squared Mahalanobis distance, and it is what "how far out is this point" means once the cloud has a shape.

dM(x⃗)2 = (x⃗ - μ⃗)T Σ-1 (x⃗ - μ⃗) Read as: the squared Mahalanobis distance from x to the centre. Setting Σ to the identity gives back ordinary squared Euclidean distance, so this is Euclidean distance after the covariance has been divided out.

The determinant in front is the volume factor. A cloud spread over more space must be lower everywhere, because the total has to stay at one, and det(Σ) is proportional to the squared volume of the ellipse.

3. Stretch, turn, shift: where a Gaussian comes from

Theorem If x is multivariate normal with mean μ and covariance Σ, there is a matrix B with BBT = Σ such that z = B-1(x - μ) is standard normal. Equivalently x = μ + Bz.

Proof. The shape of the argument is: build B, then check that the transformed variable has the mean and covariance it should, and use the fact that a linear map of a Gaussian is Gaussian.

Σ is symmetric and positive definite, so it has a Cholesky factorisation Σ = BBT with B lower triangular and invertible. Take that B and define z = B-1(x - μ). Its mean is zero because expectation is linear and x has mean μ. Its covariance is the step that matters.

Cov(z⃗) = B-1 Σ (B-1)T = B-1 (BBT) (BT)-1 = I Read as: applying a matrix A to a random vector turns its covariance into AΣA transposed. Substituting Σ = BB transposed lets B inverse cancel B on the left and the transposes cancel on the right, leaving the identity matrix.

That cancellation is the whole proof. A Gaussian with covariance the identity and mean zero is standard normal, so z is standard normal, and rearranging gives x = μ + Bz.

The reframing Write the one-dimensional case of x = μ + Bz and you get z = μ + σε with ε standard normal. That is the reparameterisation trick. Its whole purpose is that the randomness now sits in ε, which carries no parameters, so a derivative with respect to μ or σ can pass straight through. Nothing was invented for deep learning; the identity was already here, written the other way round.

4. Where Euclidean distance gets it wrong

A diagonal covariance matrix means the coordinates are independent, and for the Gaussian that equivalence really does run both ways. It is worth flagging that this is special: for a general joint distribution, zero covariance does not give independence, as lesson 144 showed with a real column pair.

The equivalence between zero covariance and independence is a property of the Gaussian, not of probability. Assuming it elsewhere is how a model ends up treating two variables as unrelated because their correlation was near zero.

The practical payoff of the quadratic form is that it ranks points differently from ordinary distance, and it is right to.

5. Worked example: which request is stranger?

Fit a Gaussian to the logs of prompt tokens, output tokens and latency, on all 40,000 requests. The log scale is the honest one here, because those columns are lognormal and fitting a Gaussian to the raw values would be the error this course warns about.

  1. Fit the two parameters. The mean vector is (5.558, 4.305, 5.061) in log units, and Σ is the 3 by 3 covariance of those logged columns.
  2. Take the determinant. det(Σ) = 0.06766. It is well below the product of the three variances, because the columns are correlated and a tilted cloud encloses less volume than its spreads alone suggest.
  3. Pick two candidate rows. Call them A and B, both a little away from the centre.
  4. Measure A the ordinary way. Euclidean distance from the mean, 0.886.
  5. Measure B the ordinary way. Euclidean distance 1.329, so B looks half as far again from the centre.
  6. Now measure with the covariance. Mahalanobis distance gives A 2.171 and B 1.376.
  7. Read the reversal. A is Euclidean-nearer and Mahalanobis-further. Ranked by ordinary distance A is the 14,905th most distant row; ranked by Mahalanobis it is the 32,339th.
  8. Say why, and what changes if. A sits across the cloud's thin direction and B along its long one, and the covariance knows the difference. Had the three columns been uncorrelated, Σ would be diagonal and the two rankings would agree.
code/0146-the-multivariate-gaussian.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
X = np.log(frame[["prompt_tokens", "output_tokens", "latency_ms"]].to_numpy(float))
mu, sigma = X.mean(axis=0), np.cov(X, rowvar=False, ddof=1)

B = np.linalg.cholesky(sigma)              # B B^T = Sigma
assert np.allclose(B @ B.T, sigma)

z = np.linalg.solve(B, (X - mu).T).T       # whiten
assert np.allclose(z.mean(axis=0), 0, atol=1e-9)
assert np.allclose(np.cov(z, rowvar=False, ddof=1), np.eye(3), atol=1e-9)

mahalanobis = np.linalg.norm(z, axis=1)    # same as sqrt of the quadratic form

The full program evaluates the density twice, once from the formula and once by whitening and correcting with the Jacobian, and the two agree to machine precision. That agreement is the proof above, run rather than read.

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does the exponent's quadratic form measure?
It is squared Mahalanobis distance, which is Euclidean distance after the covariance is divided out. The fourth option is what you get only when Σ is the identity, a special case mistaken for the rule. The second confuses the exponent with the density it sits inside, and the third belongs to the determinant in front.
Why is a latent sampled as mu plus sigma times epsilon rather than drawn directly?
Moving the randomness into epsilon, which carries no parameters, leaves mu and sigma on a differentiable path. The fourth option is wrong because the sample stays random. The third confuses this identity with the KL term that pulls the posterior towards the prior, which is a separate part of the objective.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A two-dimensional Gaussian is centred at the origin with

Σ = [[9, 0], [0, 1]] Read as: the first coordinate has variance 9, the second has variance 1, and the covariance between them is 0.

Find the Mahalanobis distance of the points (3, 0) and (0, 2), say which is the more surprising observation, and compare that answer with what Euclidean distance says.

Hint

A diagonal matrix is its own easiest case: the inverse is the reciprocal of each diagonal entry, so the quadratic form is a sum of squared coordinates each divided by its own variance.

Show the worked solution
  1. Invert Σ. For a diagonal matrix, take the reciprocal of each diagonal entry: Σ-1 = [[1/9, 0], [0, 1]].
  2. Write the quadratic form. It becomes x12/9 + x22/1.
  3. Evaluate at (3, 0). 9/9 + 0 = 1, so the Mahalanobis distance is √1 = 1.
  4. Evaluate at (0, 2). 0 + 4 = 4, so the distance is √4 = 2.
  5. Rank them. (0, 2) is twice as far in Mahalanobis terms, so it is much the more surprising point.
  6. Now Euclidean. (3, 0) is 3 units away and (0, 2) is 2 units away, so ordinary distance ranks them the other way round.
  7. Explain the reversal. The first coordinate has a standard deviation of 3, so being 3 out along it is one ordinary standard deviation, entirely unremarkable. The second has a standard deviation of 1, so being 2 out along it is two standard deviations.
  8. What changes if. Make the variances equal and Σ becomes a multiple of the identity, the ellipses become circles, and the two distances agree up to a constant factor. The disagreement exists only because the cloud has a shape.

Sanity check. A point one standard deviation out along any single axis must give a Mahalanobis distance of exactly 1, and (3, 0) does. If your two answers came out in the same order as the Euclidean ones, you probably multiplied by Σ rather than by its inverse.

Your teacher. If you have an anomaly detector that thresholds on Euclidean distance in a feature space, compute the covariance matrix of those features and look at how far it is from diagonal. Every off-diagonal entry is a direction where your detector is measuring the wrong thing.
