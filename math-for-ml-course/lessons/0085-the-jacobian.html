The Jacobian is the chain rule when both ends are vectors

working 12 min One matrix per layer, and backpropagation is what you get by multiplying them.

The one-minute version

  • A layer takes a vector and returns a vector, so there is no single slope. There are n by m of them, and the Jacobian is where you keep them.
  • Composing maps multiplies Jacobians, which is the chain rule with the numbers arranged in a grid.
  • Autodiff never builds one. It computes v transpose J, and that single line is one backpropagation step.
  • For a square map, the absolute determinant is the factor volumes get multiplied by, which is what makes a change of variables in a density work.

1. A layer has no single slope

Feed a three-vector into a layer and get a two-vector out. Nudge the first input. Both outputs move, by different amounts. Nudge the second input and both move again, differently.

There are six numbers here, one per input-output pair, and none of them alone answers "how does this layer respond". You need all six, arranged so that the arrangement itself is useful.

2. The Jacobian

Jij = ∂fi / ∂xj ,   so J is n by m for f : Rm → Rn f: the map, taking m numbers in and returning n out. fi: its i-th output. xj: its j-th input. Jij: how much output i moves when input j is nudged. Rows index outputs and columns index inputs, and getting that backwards is the single most common error in this territory.

Every entry is a partial derivative you already know how to take from Lesson 83. The Jacobian adds no calculus at all. It adds a filing system, and the filing system is what makes the next step possible.

3. The theorem, and it is the one from Lesson 81 in a grid

Theorem (the multivariate chain rule). If g is differentiable at a and f is differentiable at g(a), then f ∘ g is differentiable at a and its Jacobian is the matrix product Jf(g(a)) Jg(a).

What is assumed: differentiability of each map at the right point, in the full sense of Lesson 83, not merely that the partials exist. The shape of the argument: write each map as its linear part plus a remainder, substitute, and show the leftover terms are too small to matter. The step that does the real work is bounding the composed remainder, because that is where differentiability of the outer map is actually spent.

  1. Write both maps linearly. With b = g(a), A = Jf(b) and B = Jg(a): g(a+h) = b + Bh + r(h) and f(b+k) = f(b) + Ak + s(k), with both remainders vanishing faster than their arguments.
  2. Substitute. Put k = Bh + r(h), which tends to zero with h. Then f(g(a+h)) = f(g(a)) + ABh + [Ar(h) + s(k)].
  3. Bound the first leftover. ||Ar(h)|| is at most ||A|| ||r(h)||, and ||r(h)||/||h|| tends to zero, so this term is negligible against ||h||.
  4. Bound the second. ||k|| is at most ||B|| ||h|| + ||r(h)||, so k is of order ||h||. Then ||s(k)||/||h|| = (||s(k)||/||k||) × (||k||/||h||), the first factor tending to zero and the second bounded. When k is zero the term is zero and there is nothing to bound.
  5. Conclude. The composition equals its value plus ABh plus something negligible, which is the definition of differentiability with Jacobian AB. QED

Set every dimension to one and this is Lesson 81 exactly, with the matrix product becoming a product of numbers.

4. The line backpropagation actually runs

The reframingA gradient goes in, gets multiplied by a transposed Jacobian, and a gradient comes out. That is one backpropagation step and there is nothing else in it.
∇xz = ( ∂y/∂x )T ∇yz z: the scalar loss at the end of the chain. y: the intermediate vector this layer produced. x: the vector this layer consumed. ∂y/∂x: this layer's Jacobian. ∇yz: the gradient that has already been carried back to y. The transpose is what makes the shapes chain: an n by m Jacobian transposed is m by n, which turns a length-n gradient into a length-m one.

Notice what is not in that line: the Jacobian is never built. The expression is a matrix acting on a vector, and every autodiff library evaluates it as a vector-Jacobian product without ever materialising the matrix.

5. The determinant, and what it measures

When a map takes n numbers to n numbers, its Jacobian is square and has a determinant. That number is not an algebra exercise.

Theorem. For f differentiable at a, the ratio of the volume of f(S) to the volume of S tends to |det Jf(a)| as the region S shrinks onto a.

The shape of the argument, at the level this course works at. Near a the map is its linear part plus a remainder that shrinks faster than the region does. A translation changes no volume, and a linear map multiplies volume by the absolute determinant, which is the defining property of the determinant established in Module 04. The neglected remainder contributes nothing in the limit. The step that does the real work is that last clause, and it is exactly why the statement is about a limit rather than about any fixed region.

6. Worked: one layer, three checks

Take the map a real layer applies, tanh(Wx + b), from R3 to R2, composed with a second from R2 to R2.

  1. Differentiate the first layer. The tanh derivative is 1 − tanh², applied elementwise, which scales each row of W1. The result is 2 by 3.
  2. Differentiate the second. Same rule, giving 2 by 2.
  3. Multiply. 2 by 2 times 2 by 3 gives 2 by 3, which is the composed map's shape.
  4. Check against the definition. Finite-difference the composed map directly. The largest disagreement is 4.687 × 10−11.
  5. Check the vector-Jacobian product. Building the product and multiplying, against accumulating right to left, agree to 5.551 × 10−17.
  6. Check the volume claim. Restrict to a square map and push a shrinking square through it. The area ratio goes 0.019191, then 0.019124, then 0.019123, against |det J| = 0.019123.

Sanity check. The composed Jacobian must be 2 by 3, because the composed map eats three numbers and returns two. If the shapes will not chain, the multiplication order is wrong, and that is the first thing to look at rather than the last.

What changes if the second layer is widened to return seven numbers? The composed Jacobian becomes 7 by 3 and the vector-Jacobian product needs a seven-entry vector to start from. Nothing about the method changes, which is the whole reason for writing it as a matrix product.

In words. Two layers compose by multiplying their Jacobians, and the shape rule tells you the answer's dimensions before you compute a single entry.

def vjp(v, x):
    """v^T J for the composed map, the way backpropagation does it:
    right to left, one transposed Jacobian at a time, never forming
    the product of the two."""
    u = layer1(x)
    v = v @ jac_layer2(u)      # v now lives in the middle layer's space
    return v @ jac_layer1(x)   # and now in the input's

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

For a map from R to the power m into R to the power n, what shape is the Jacobian?
Rows index outputs and columns index inputs, so an n by m matrix. The first option is the transpose and is by far the commonest slip, which is why every worked example on this page states the shape before computing an entry. The second and fourth each assume the map is square, which a layer almost never is.
Why does reverse-mode autodiff compute v transpose J rather than building J?
A 4096 by 4096 layer has a Jacobian of 16.7 million entries used exactly once. The second is false: the Jacobian of a nonlinear layer is perfectly computable, it is just not worth computing. The third is false and measurable, and the program puts the gap between the two routes at 5.6e-17. The fourth is a true statement about a Jacobian answering a different question, which is what makes it the strongest distractor here.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Take the linear map

y₁ = −2x₁ + x₂ ,    y₂ = x₁ + x₂ Read as: two outputs, each a weighted sum of the same two inputs. A linear map, so its Jacobian is the same everywhere and the approximation in the theorem is exact rather than local.

Write the Jacobian, compute its determinant, state what happens to the area of the unit square, then confirm the answer a second way using the images of the two basis vectors.

Hint

The second route needs no calculus at all. Where do (1, 0) and (0, 1) land, and what is the area of the parallelogram they span?

Show the worked solution
  1. Read off the partials. ∂y₁/∂x₁ = −2, ∂y₁/∂x₂ = 1, ∂y₂/∂x₁ = 1, ∂y₂/∂x₂ = 1.
  2. Arrange them. Rows are outputs, so J = [[−2, 1], [1, 1]].
  3. Take the determinant. (−2)(1) − (1)(1) = −3.
  4. Read the consequence. Areas are multiplied by |−3| = 3, and the negative sign says the map flips orientation.
  5. Check without calculus. (1, 0) maps to (−2, 1) and (0, 1) maps to (1, 1). The parallelogram they span has area |(−2)(1) − (1)(1)| = 3.

Sanity check. The two routes must give the same number, and for a linear map the Jacobian's columns are literally the images of the basis vectors, so they cannot disagree. If they do, the Jacobian was written transposed.

Your teacher. If the transpose in the backpropagation line is what did not land, ask an agent to write out the shapes for a 5 to 3 to 1 network and check that the gradient coming back is length 5 rather than length 3. If the determinant is the sticking point, ask it to draw the unit square and its image under [[2, 0], [0, 3]] first, where the answer is obviously 6, and then under this page's matrix.
