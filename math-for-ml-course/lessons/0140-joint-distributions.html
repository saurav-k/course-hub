Your features are not four experiments, they are one

working 11 min Everything in this module is built on one object, and this page introduces it.

The one-minute version

  • Two measurements taken from the same thing share a joint distribution, and that one object holds everything there is to know about both.
  • A row of your data matrix is one draw from that joint. The table is a sample from it, not a different kind of thing.
  • A marginal is the joint with a variable summed away, and it is what you get when you look at one column and forget the rest.
  • Independence is factorisation: the joint is the product of its marginals. It is an assumption you buy tractability with, not a fact you are given.

1. The mental model: one table, not four columns

A serving log records four things about every request: which tier the customer is on, how many tokens the prompt held, how long the request took, and whether the cache was hit. It is tempting to read that as four separate measurements that happen to be stored side by side.

It is not. All four came out of the same request. A slow request is slow because it had a long prompt and landed on a busy machine, so the four numbers arrived together and they carry information about each other. The object that describes them together is the joint distribution, and it is the only object that describes them honestly.

For two discrete variables the joint distribution is a table. Every cell holds the probability of one combination, and because every observation lands in exactly one cell, the cells add to one.

2. The mechanism: PMF, PDF and CDF, all three

For discrete variables the table above is the joint probability mass function, written as one function of two arguments.

p(x, y) = P(X = x and Y = y) Read as: p of x comma y is the probability that the first variable X takes the value x at the same time as the second variable Y takes the value y. Capital letters are the random variables, lowercase letters are particular values they might take.

For continuous variables there is no table, because there are no cells to fill. A single exact pair of latency and token count has probability zero, the same way a single exact latency does. What exists instead is a joint density, a surface over the plane, and probability is volume underneath it.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b, c ≤ Y ≤ d) = ∫ab ∫cd f(x, y) dy dx Read as: the probability that X lands between a and b while Y lands between c and d is the volume under the density surface f over that rectangle. The two integral signs are the two directions you sweep to get a volume rather than an area.

The joint cumulative distribution function is the one definition that covers both cases without changing form, which is why it is worth knowing even though it is rarely the thing you compute.

F(x, y) = P(X ≤ x, Y ≤ y) Read as: the probability that X is at most x while Y is at most y. It is a running total in two directions at once, and it is defined identically whether the variables are discrete, continuous, or one of each.

3. Independence is factorisation, and it is expensive to do without

Two variables are independent exactly when the joint factorises into the product of the two one-variable distributions.

p(x, y) = pX(x) × pY(y)   for every x and y Read as: the joint probability of the pair equals the probability of x on its own times the probability of y on its own. The subscripts mark which single variable each factor describes. The clause "for every x and y" is load-bearing: one cell where it fails is enough to destroy independence.

The reason anyone reaches for that assumption is cost. A joint distribution over d binary features has one probability per combination, so it needs 2d minus one free numbers. At twenty features that is over a million, and at fifty it is more than the number of requests any service will ever serve.

Independence is an assumption about the world, and assuming it does not make it true. A naive Bayes spam filter assumes the words in a message are independent given the label, which is plainly false, and it works anyway. The same assumption applied to neighbouring pixels of an image fails badly, because neighbouring pixels are nearly copies of each other. The assumption is a trade, and you owe the reader of your model a sentence saying you made it.

4. Worked example: a joint table from 40,000 real requests

The dataset is inference_runs.csv, 40,000 served requests. Bucket latency at 120 ms and 260 ms and cross it against the customer tier.

  1. Count the combinations. Of the 40,000 requests, 2,074 were enterprise and fast, 1,056 enterprise and normal, 66 enterprise and slow, and so on across the nine cells.
  2. Divide by the total. 2,074 ÷ 40,000 = 0.0518, which is the top-left cell of the figure above.
  3. Check the cells add to one. They sum to 1.0000 exactly, because every request landed in exactly one cell.
  4. Sum a row to get a marginal. The enterprise row is 0.0518 + 0.0264 + 0.0016 = 0.0799, so about 8 percent of traffic is enterprise.
  5. Check that marginal a second way. Count the enterprise rows directly, ignoring latency entirely: 3,196 ÷ 40,000 = 0.0799. The same number, and it had to be.
  6. Test independence on one cell. If tier and speed were independent, the enterprise-and-slow cell would be 0.0799 × 0.2322 = 0.0186.
  7. Compare against what happened. The cell actually holds 0.0016, which is about one eleventh of that. One cell is enough: these two variables are not independent.
  8. Say what that means. Enterprise requests are far less likely to be slow than an independence assumption would predict, which is the whole point of buying the tier.
code/0140-joint-distributions.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
frame["band"] = pd.cut(frame["latency_ms"], [0, 120, 260, float("inf")],
                       labels=["fast", "normal", "slow"], right=False)

counts = pd.crosstab(frame["tier"], frame["band"])
joint = counts / counts.to_numpy().sum()

print(joint.round(4))
print("cells sum to", joint.to_numpy().sum())

# A marginal, by two routes that must agree.
print(joint.sum(axis=1))
print(frame["tier"].value_counts(normalize=True))

The full program measures distance from independence on a genuinely dependent pair and on a pair that is independent by construction, and gets 0.0871 against 0.0015. The second number is not exactly zero and never will be, because it is estimated from 40,000 rows and sampling noise keeps it a little above.

5. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Four features are recorded for each of 40,000 requests. How many random variables is that?
The four numbers came out of the same request, so they share one joint distribution and carry information about each other. The first option is the mistake this page exists to prevent. The third confuses samples with variables: 40,000 is how many draws you have, not how many variables. The fourth describes repeated measurement of one quantity, which is a different setup entirely.
You know both marginals of a joint table. What can you conclude about the cells?
Margins pin the row and column totals and leave the interior free, which is exactly why a joint holds more than its marginals do. The first option is the misconception: many different tables share the same margins. The second is not a rule anywhere. The third overcorrects, because margins are genuine information, just not enough of it.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A/B traffic is split evenly between two model versions, A and B. Of 10,000 requests, 4,100 went to A and were rated good, 900 went to A and were rated bad, 3,600 went to B and were rated good, and 1,400 went to B and were rated bad. Build the joint distribution, read off both marginals, and decide whether version and rating are independent.

Hint

Divide every count by 10,000 first. Then compare one cell against the product of its own row and column marginals. You need only one cell to disprove independence, but you need all four to prove it.

Show the worked solution
  1. Divide by the total. The four joint probabilities are 0.41, 0.09, 0.36 and 0.14, and they sum to 1.00.
  2. Row marginals. P(A) = 0.41 + 0.09 = 0.50 and P(B) = 0.36 + 0.14 = 0.50, which matches the even split.
  3. Column marginals. P(good) = 0.41 + 0.36 = 0.77 and P(bad) = 0.09 + 0.14 = 0.23.
  4. Predict one cell under independence. P(A) × P(good) = 0.50 × 0.77 = 0.385.
  5. Compare. The cell actually holds 0.41, which is 0.025 above the prediction.
  6. Conclude. Not independent. Version A is rated good more often than an independence assumption predicts, and version B correspondingly less.
  7. Say how much. A is good 0.41 / 0.50 = 82 percent of the time against B's 0.36 / 0.50 = 72 percent, a ten point gap.

Sanity check. the four cells must sum to 1.00, both marginals must sum to 1.00, and every deviation from independence must cancel across the table. Here the deviations are +0.025, -0.025, -0.025, +0.025. If yours do not cancel, a cell is wrong.

Your teacher. Take any two columns from a table you already own and build their joint by hand, then check one cell against the product of its marginals. If you cannot say out loud what a single cell means in the language of your own product, the table is not yet a joint distribution to you, it is still a spreadsheet.
