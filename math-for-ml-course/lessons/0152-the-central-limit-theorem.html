The rule is not n above thirty. The rule is skewness

frontier 14 min A nominal 95 percent interval on a 1.2 percent rate covers 30 percent of the time at n = 30.

The one-minute version

  • Standardise a sum or standardise a sample mean and you get the same numbers, so the two forms are one theorem.
  • The conditions are independence and a finite, non-zero variance. The shape of the pieces is not a condition.
  • The theorem says nothing about how large n must be. Berry-Esseen does, and its answer is driven by skewness.
  • Measured: at n = 30 a nominal 95 percent interval covers 94 percent for a normal and 30 percent for a rare binary event.

1. The mental model: the shape of the pieces stops mattering

Take anything with a finite variance. Average enough independent copies of it, centre and rescale, and what comes out is a bell curve. The remarkable part is not that a bell appears; it is that it does not matter what you started with.

2. The two forms are one standardisation

The theorem is usually met twice, once for a sum and once for a sample mean, and it looks like two results. It is one.

Zsum = (Sn - nμ) ÷ (σ√n)     Zmean = (X̄n - μ) ÷ (σ/√n) Read as: on the left, take the sum of n draws, subtract what it averages, and divide by its own standard deviation. On the right, do the same for the sample mean. Sn is the sum, X̄n the average, μ and σ the single-draw mean and standard deviation.

Divide the sum's numerator and denominator by n and you land exactly on the mean's expression. Dividing by n divides the centre by n and the spread by n, so the ratio never moved. Measured on 5,000 repeats, the largest gap between the two standardised samples is at the level of floating-point noise.

Theorem Let X1, X2, ... be independent draws from one distribution with mean μ and variance σ2 that is finite and not zero. Then the standardised sum, and equivalently the standardised sample mean, converges in distribution to the standard normal.
Where the proof stops in this course. The usual proof takes the moment generating function of the standardised sum, uses the product rule from lesson 148 to turn it into an n-th power, expands to second order and takes a limit, which gives et2/2, the standard normal's MGF. Every step is available to you except the last one: concluding that matching transforms means matching distributions needs a continuity theorem this course does not build, and the fully general version replaces the MGF with a characteristic function so it works when no MGF exists. That gap is real and worth naming rather than papering over. What the argument does show, and what is worth carrying, is why the limit is normal: it is what an n-th power of an expression agreeing with 1 + t2/2n has to converge to.

3. What the theorem does not say

Three things, and each one costs somebody money regularly.

It says nothing about individual observations. Averages become normal; the underlying latency stays as skewed as it ever was.

It is worst exactly in the tails, which is where people most want to use it. Convergence is in distribution, which controls the bulk far better than the extremes.

And it says nothing about how large n must be. That is not an omission this page is filling in, it is a property of the statement: it is a limit, and a limit places no constraint on any finite n.

4. Berry-Esseen: the answer the rule of thumb is guessing at

There is a theorem about the rate, and reading its shape settles the question.

|Fn(x) - Φ(x)| ≤ 3ρ ÷ (σ3√n) Read as: the largest gap between the true distribution of the standardised mean and the standard normal is at most three times rho, divided by sigma cubed times the square root of n. Here rho is the average of the absolute cube of the deviation, and the ratio of rho to sigma cubed is a skewness-like quantity. The constant 3 is not the best known; van Beek improved it to 0.8.

Read the shape rather than the constant. The gap falls like one over root n, and the multiplier out front is how skewed the source is. A symmetric source has a small multiplier and converges quickly. A badly skewed one has a large multiplier and needs a much larger n for the same accuracy. There is no universal n in the formula because there cannot be one.

Be honest about that bound in practice. A probability gap can never exceed 1, so any n where the bound is above 1 is telling you nothing. On the latency column rho over sigma cubed is 3.43, so the bound only drops below 1 past n = 107. Berry-Esseen settles the shape of the answer; its constant is far too loose to size a real sample with.

5. The rule of thumb, measured

The honest way to settle this is to build a nominal 95 percent interval many times and count how often it actually contains the truth.

The practitioner's rule that actually works for a proportion is np ≥ 10 and n(1-p) ≥ 10. At p = 0.012 that needs n of at least 833, which is exactly where the bottom row turns green.

6. Worked example: sizing a rare-conversion test

  1. State the baseline. A checkout funnel converts at 2 percent, and you want a normal-approximation interval you can defend.
  2. Apply the rule. np ≥ 10 with p = 0.02 needs n ≥ 10 ÷ 0.02 = 500.
  3. Check the other side. n(1-p) = 500 × 0.98 = 490, comfortably clear, so the small side binds as it always does for a rare event.
  4. Compute the standard error there. √(0.02 × 0.98 ÷ 500) = √0.0000392 = 0.00626.
  5. Build the interval. 1.96 × 0.00626 = 0.0123, so plus or minus 1.23 percentage points.
  6. Read what that means before running anything. The interval is more than half the baseline itself, so this design cannot resolve anything smaller than roughly a 60 percent relative lift.
  7. Sanity check. Halving the interval must quadruple n, so 2,000 users per arm gives about ±0.61 points. That matches √4 = 2.
  8. What changes if the baseline is 0.2 percent. The rule now needs n ≥ 5,000 per arm just for the approximation to hold, and the interval there is still ±0.12 points against a 0.2 point baseline. At that rarity the right move is an exact binomial interval rather than a normal one.
code/0152-the-central-limit-theorem.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260901)

def coverage(draw, truth, n, reps=4_000):
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(reps):
        s = draw(n)
        half = 1.96 * s.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
        hits += (s.mean() - half) <= truth <= (s.mean() + half)
    return hits / reps

print("normal   n=30", coverage(lambda k: rng.normal(0, 1, k), 0.0, 30))
print("rate .012 n=30",
      coverage(lambda k: (rng.random(k) < 0.012).astype(float), 0.012, 30))

7. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which condition does the classical Central Limit Theorem actually require?
Finite non-zero variance, plus independence, is the whole of it. The first option is what the theorem exists to make unnecessary. The second is a rule of thumb with no theorem behind it. The fourth inverts the assumptions: independence is the load-bearing half, and pairwise independence is enough for the law of large numbers but not for this.
An A/B test measures a 1.2 percent rate with 30 users per arm and builds a nominal 95 percent interval. Real coverage?
Measured at 0.304 over 4,000 repeats. At p = 0.012 and n = 30 the expected number of conversions is 0.36, so most samples contain none at all and the interval collapses to a point that misses. The first option applies the rule of thumb precisely where its assumptions fail, and the fourth confuses a nominal level with a delivered one.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Your revenue-per-session metric is strongly right-skewed: most sessions spend nothing and a few spend a great deal. A colleague proposes an A/B test with 200 sessions per arm and a normal-approximation interval, citing the n above thirty rule.

Find what is wrong with that argument, say what evidence would settle it, and give two alternatives that do not require the approximation to hold.

Hint

The rule of thumb is not a theorem, and the quantity it fails to mention is the one Berry-Esseen puts in the numerator. Think about what you could compute from the existing data before running anything.

Show the worked solution
  1. Name the flaw. The rule of thumb quotes n and says nothing about the source, while Berry-Esseen puts the skewness-like ratio rho over sigma cubed directly in the numerator of the error.
  2. Say why this metric is the bad case. Revenue per session is a spike of zeros plus a heavy right tail, which is exactly the shape with a large third moment.
  3. Recall the measured evidence. A lognormal source with sigma 2 delivered 0.647 coverage at n = 30 and still only 0.845 at n = 1,000, so this family converges far more slowly than thirty observations.
  4. Give the decisive evidence. Do not argue. Bootstrap it: resample 200 sessions from last month's data many times, build the interval each time, and count how often it covers the full-month mean.
  5. Why that settles it. It measures coverage on the actual distribution rather than assuming one, which is the same experiment the figure on this page reports.
  6. Alternative one. Use a bootstrap interval instead of a normal one, which makes no shape assumption at all.
  7. Alternative two. Change the metric. Test the conversion rate and the mean spend among converters separately, so neither has the zero-spike plus heavy-tail shape that causes the trouble.
  8. What changes if the sample is much larger. At tens of thousands of sessions the approximation becomes defensible for this metric, so the honest recommendation is either far more traffic or a different tool, not 200 sessions with a normal interval.

Sanity check. Any answer that fixes the problem by raising n alone must raise it a great deal, since the error falls only as one over root n: going from 200 to 800 sessions halves the gap to normality, it does not remove it. If your answer was "use n = 30 or more", you have restated the rule the question is about.

Your teacher. Take the most skewed metric your team reports a confidence interval on, and run the bootstrap coverage check in step four on last month's data. It takes twenty lines. Whatever number comes back is the real confidence level of every interval you have published for that metric.

Primary source to go deeper

Charles Grinstead and Laurie Snell, Introduction to Probability (American Mathematical Society), chapter 9, which states the theorem for the discrete and continuous cases and says in its own words that it "says nothing about how large n has to be" before offering thirty as a rule of thumb. For the rate, see Durrett, Probability: Theory and Examples, Theorem 3.4.17 for Berry-Esseen and Theorem 3.4.10 for the Lindeberg-Feller version that drops the identical-distribution assumption.
