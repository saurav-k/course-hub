Module 04 · Eigenvalues, SVD and PCA · Lesson 61

One polynomial holds every eigenvalue

working 11 min Two coefficients of that polynomial are numbers you already know, which is where the free check comes from.

The one-minute version

  • An eigenvalue makes A - λI collapse some direction, and that means its determinant is zero.
  • So every eigenvalue is a root of one polynomial, the characteristic polynomial.
  • For a two-by-two it is λ2 - trace · λ + determinant, so both coefficients are already known before you expand anything.
  • That gives two free checks: the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant.
  • It is the cleanest definition and a terrible algorithm, and no numerical library computes eigenvalues this way.

1. From a missing direction to a zero determinant

Lesson 01 asked for a non-zero v with Av = λv. Move everything to one side and that reads (A - λI)v = 0. So the matrix A - λI must send some non-zero vector to the origin, which is exactly what it means for a square matrix to be singular, and a square matrix is singular exactly when its determinant is zero.

det(A - λI) = 0 Read as: the characteristic equation. A is the matrix, I is the identity of the same size, and λ is the unknown number we are solving for. Every eigenvalue of A is a solution, and every solution is an eigenvalue.

Expanding the left side for a general two-by-two [[a, b], [c, d]] gives (a - λ)(d - λ) - bc, which multiplies out to λ2 - (a + d)λ + (ad - bc). Look at the two coefficients. The first is the trace and the second is the determinant, both of which you can read off the matrix without doing any algebra at all.

2. The two identities, and why they hold

The two coefficients are not a coincidence of notation. They are the eigenvalues in disguise.

The theorem For a square matrix with eigenvalues listed with multiplicity, the trace is their sum and the determinant is their product.

Proof for the two-by-two case, in full. Expanding directly gives det(A - λI) = λ2 - (a + d)λ + (ad - bc). The same polynomial is monic with roots λ1 and λ2, so it also equals (λ - λ1)(λ - λ2), which multiplies out to λ2 - (λ1 + λ2)λ + λ1λ2. Two polynomials that agree for every value of lambda have equal coefficients. Matching the lambda coefficient gives λ1 + λ2 = a + d, and matching the constant gives λ1λ2 = ad - bc.

The step that does the real work is matching coefficients. It is allowed only because the two expressions agree for every lambda rather than merely at the roots. If they only agreed at the two roots, nothing about the coefficients would follow.

The honest boundary. For a general size the same argument runs, with two things this course does not build: factoring into linear factors needs the fundamental theorem of algebra over the complex numbers, and picking out the second coefficient needs the observation that only the product of the diagonal entries contributes to it. The determinant half is easier and needs neither: set λ = 0 in det(A - λI) = ∏(λi - λ) and read off the answer immediately.

3. What a zero determinant is saying

4. Worked in full, on the matrix from lesson 60

  1. Take the matrix. A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]], the same one whose eigendirections we hunted last page.
  2. Shift by lambda. A - λI = [[4 - λ, 1], [2, 3 - λ]].
  3. Take the determinant. (4 - λ)(3 - λ) - (1)(2).
  4. Expand. 12 - 7λ + λ2 - 2 = λ2 - 7λ + 10.
  5. Notice what you already had. The 7 is 4 + 3, the trace. The 10 is 12 - 2, the determinant. Steps 2 to 4 could have been skipped.
  6. Solve. λ = (7 ± √(49 - 40)) / 2 = (7 ± 3) / 2, so 5 and 2.
  7. Sanity check. 5 + 2 = 7 and 5 × 2 = 10. Both coefficients reproduced, and these are the same two numbers lesson 60 found by a completely different route.
  8. What changes if. Replace the lower-left 2 with -2. The determinant becomes 12 + 2 = 14, the discriminant becomes 49 - 56 = -7, and there is no real root. Both identities still hold, over the complex numbers.

5. The warning that matters

Never compute eigenvalues this way. Finding the roots of a polynomial is badly behaved as the degree grows: a tiny change in a coefficient can move a root enormously. Real libraries use iterative methods that never form the polynomial at all. This page shows the route once because it is the clearest definition, and the course does not use it again past a two-by-two.

6. The identities at a scale that shows their limits

code/0061-characteristic-and-invariants.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(cov)          # the 24-channel covariance
assert np.isclose(np.trace(cov), eigenvalues.sum())
# -> holds to an absolute tolerance: a sum of 24 small numbers is well behaved

assert np.isclose(np.linalg.det(cov), np.prod(eigenvalues), rtol=1e-6)
# -> needs a RELATIVE tolerance: a product of 24 numbers each around 1e-3
#    lands near 1e-74, so an absolute comparison would be meaningless

Both identities are exact in mathematics. Only one of them is comfortably checkable in floating point. On the 24-channel dataset the trace and the sum of eigenvalues agree at 0.431864915561, while the determinant and the product of eigenvalues both come out near 3.65e-74, close enough to the bottom of double precision that the comparison has to be made relatively. That is worth knowing before you write an assertion of your own.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does no numerical library compute eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial?
A small change in a coefficient can move a root a long way, so the route that defines an eigenvalue is not the route that computes one. The first option is false, since every square matrix has a characteristic polynomial. The second names a real cost that is not the reason. The third is true of some matrices and is not a defect of the method.
A three-by-three matrix has trace 6 and determinant 0. What follows?
A zero determinant is a zero product, so at least one eigenvalue is zero, and a matrix with a zero eigenvalue collapses a direction and cannot be inverted. The first option would force the trace to be zero too. The third is consistent with the trace but multiplies to 8, not 0. The fourth is contradicted, since zero is real.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Find the eigenvalues of the matrix below, and check both coefficients of its characteristic polynomial.

A = [[6, -2], [-2, 9]] Read as: a symmetric two-by-two matrix. The two diagonal entries are 6 and 9, and the repeated off-diagonal entry is -2.
Hint

Write down the trace and the determinant first. You already have the whole polynomial once you have those two numbers, so there is nothing to expand.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the trace. 6 + 9 = 15.
  2. Read the determinant. (6)(9) - (-2)(-2) = 54 - 4 = 50.
  3. Write the polynomial. λ2 - 15λ + 50 = 0.
  4. Solve. The discriminant is 225 - 200 = 25, so λ = (15 ± 5) / 2, giving 10 and 5.

Sanity check. 10 + 5 = 15 and 10 × 5 = 50, so both coefficients are reproduced. Both roots are also positive, which lesson 64 will call positive definite, and both are real, which lesson 62 will show is guaranteed for any symmetric matrix.

Your teacher. If step 5 of the worked example felt like a trick, ask an agent to expand det(A - λI) for a symbolic [[a, b], [c, d]] and point at where the trace and the determinant appear. Seeing it once in letters is what makes it stop looking like a coincidence.
