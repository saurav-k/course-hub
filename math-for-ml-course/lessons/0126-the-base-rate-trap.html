Module 07 · Probability · Lesson 126

A detector that catches 92 percent of abuse can still be wrong three times in four

working 12 min Why a rare positive class destroys precision, and what to report instead. · Hoffrage and Gigerenzer (2004)

The one-minute version

  • When the positive class is rare, the prior dominates the posterior, and no amount of detector quality rescues it.
  • The same numbers written as counts rather than percentages make the answer obvious, and that change alone moves how often trained readers get it right.
  • Precision is the posterior. On this module's log a detector with 92 percent recall has a precision of 0.2395.
  • Raising recall does not fix it. Only the false-positive rate and the base rate move precision.
  • On the same file, "always predict clean" scores 99.604 percent accuracy and catches nothing.

1. Answer this before you read on

Try it first A condition affects 1 person in 1,000. A test for it has a false-positive rate of 5 percent, and it catches essentially every real case. Someone you know nothing else about tests positive. What is the chance they have the condition? Commit to a number before continuing.

Most people answer close to 95 percent. The answer is about 2 percent.

2. Worked in probabilities

  1. Name the three inputs. Prior P(condition) = 0.001. Likelihood P(positive | condition) = 1.0. The other likelihood P(positive | no condition) = 0.05.
  2. Assemble the evidence. 1.0 × 0.001 + 0.05 × 0.999 = 0.001 + 0.04995 = 0.05095.
  3. Divide. 0.001 ÷ 0.05095 = 0.0196, so about 2 percent.

The arithmetic is three lines and it convinces nobody. Now write the identical problem a second way.

3. The identical problem, in counts

  1. Take 100,000 people. 100 of them have the condition.
  2. Test the 100 who have it. Essentially all 100 test positive.
  3. Test the 99,900 who do not. Five percent of them test positive anyway, which is 4,995 people.
  4. Count the positives. 100 + 4,995 = 5,095 positive results, of which 100 are real.
  5. Read the answer off. 100 ÷ 5,095 = 0.0196.

4. This defeats trained readers, and the format is why

In 1978 that screening question was put to 60 house officers, students and physicians at a teaching hospital. The most frequent answer was 95 percent, given by 27 of the 60. Eleven gave the correct 2 percent.

The interesting result is not that people were wrong. It is what fixes it. A later study gave 48 physicians four diagnostic problems, each in two formats. Presented as probabilities and percentages, they reached the correct answer in 10 percent of cases. Presented as natural frequencies, the counts of section 3, the same doctors on the same problems reached it in 46 percent.

The reframing Nothing about the mathematics changed between those two formats. Only the presentation did. When a number has to survive contact with a decision, write it as a count of things, not as a rate.

A second, independent problem makes the same point with different numbers: a screening test with a 1 percent base rate, 79 percent sensitivity and a 9.6 percent false-positive rate gives a posterior of 7.67 percent, and in the reported study 95 of 100 physicians estimated somewhere between 70 and 80 percent.

5. Where this bites: precision is the posterior

Recall is P(flag | abusive) and you measure it on a labelled set. Precision is P(abusive | flag) and it is what the person answering the alert actually needs. Lesson 125 showed those are opposite conditionals, so they are related by Bayes and not by intuition.

The module's log has 99 abusive requests among 25,000, a base rate of 0.003960. Run the abuse detector at a threshold of 0.5.

  1. Measure recall. 91 of the 99 abusive requests are flagged, so recall is 91 ÷ 99 = 0.9192.
  2. Measure the false-positive rate. 289 of the 24,901 clean requests are flagged, so 289 ÷ 24,901 = 0.0116.
  3. Apply Bayes. 0.9192 × 0.003960 ÷ (0.9192 × 0.003960 + 0.0116 × 0.996040) = 0.2395.
  4. Check by counting. 380 requests were flagged and 91 were really abusive, so 91 ÷ 380 = 0.2395.
  5. Say it in words. The detector catches 92 percent of abuse, and three alerts in four are wrong.

Sanity check. Precision must lie between 0 and 1, and for a rare positive class with a non-trivial false-positive rate it must fall far below recall. If precision had come out near 0.92, the base rate was left out.

6. Raising recall is the wrong lever

At threshold 0.60 the detector has recall 0.7374 and a false-positive rate of 0.0019, and precision rises to 0.6033: 73 of 121 alerts are real. It caught 18 fewer abusive requests and removed 241 false alarms.

7. The base rate moves precision more than the model does

This is also why a precision measured on a balanced evaluation set does not transfer to production. Rebalancing a test set to 50 percent positives would report 98.75 percent precision for the very same detector that achieves 23.95 percent on real traffic. Nothing was faked; the base rate was.

8. What to report instead

Accuracy on this file is worthless, and it is worth seeing why in one line: always predicting "clean" gives 24,901 ÷ 25,000 = 99.604 percent accuracy while catching zero abuse. Any metric a constant beats is not measuring the thing you care about.

Report three things together: precision at a stated recall, on the production base rate, and the absolute count of false positives. The third is the one that turns a metric into a decision, because review effort is spent per alert, not per percentage point.

def precision_from_bayes(prior, recall, false_positive_rate):
    """The same number as a posterior, from the three inputs Bayes needs."""
    numerator = recall * prior
    return float(numerator / (numerator + false_positive_rate * (1.0 - prior)))

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A detector holds recall at 0.92 and its false-positive rate at 0.012. The abuse rate rises from 0.4 percent to 4 percent. What happened to precision?
Precision is a posterior, so a tenfold rise in the prior lifts it from 23.95 to 76.56 percent with the detector unchanged. The first option is the misconception the whole page exists to remove. The third confuses precision with recall, and recall is exactly the quantity that did not move here. The fourth is wrong because the three inputs Bayes needs are all already known.
On this file, what accuracy does a model that always predicts "clean" achieve?
24,901 of the 25,000 requests are clean, so a constant predictor is right 99.604 percent of the time. The first option would be right for a balanced problem, and the base rate here is 0.4 percent. The fourth confuses accuracy with recall: recall really is zero, and accuracy is near perfect, and that gap is the reason accuracy is the wrong metric here.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

At threshold 0.60 the same detector has recall 0.7374 and a false-positive rate of 0.0019, on the same file of 99 abusive requests among 25,000.

Find its precision and the absolute number of false alarms. Then decide which of the two thresholds you would ship, if a reviewer takes 90 seconds per alert and a missed abuse costs 20 minutes of incident time.

Hint

Precision needs the same three inputs as before. For the decision, put both options into one unit: minutes.

Show the worked solution
  1. Precision. 0.7374 × 0.003960 ÷ (0.7374 × 0.003960 + 0.0019 × 0.996040) = 0.6033.
  2. False alarms. Counting them gives 48, and the rounded rate recovers it: 0.0019 × 24,901 = 47.3. So 73 real plus 48 false is 121 alerts.
  3. Cost at threshold 0.50. 380 alerts × 1.5 minutes = 570 minutes of review. 8 abusive requests missed × 20 minutes = 160. Total 730 minutes.
  4. Cost at threshold 0.60. 121 alerts × 1.5 = 182 minutes. 26 missed × 20 = 520. Total 702 minutes.
  5. Decide. Threshold 0.60 wins, by 28 minutes out of 700 - which is close enough that the answer is really "these two are equivalent, go and measure the two costs properly".

Sanity check. Precision at 0.60 must exceed precision at 0.50, because a higher threshold discards proportionally more clean rows than abusive ones. As a second check, notice how fragile the decision is: at 30 minutes per missed abuse the totals become 810 and 962, and threshold 0.50 wins instead. A decision that flips on one input is a decision that needs that input measured, not assumed.

Your teacher. Whenever someone quotes you a detection rate, ask two questions: what is the base rate, and how many false positives is that per day in absolute numbers. Those two turn a percentage into a staffing decision. If you want to feel the effect rather than read it, take the numbers in section 5 and recompute precision with the base rate at one in ten thousand; ask an agent to check your arithmetic rather than to do it.
