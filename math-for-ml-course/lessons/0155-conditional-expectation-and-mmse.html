Your loss function chose what your model is trying to be

frontier 13 min Squared error targets the conditional mean. That is a theorem, not a tendency.

The one-minute version

  • E[Y|X] is a function of X, not a number, so it is itself a random variable.
  • The tower property says averaging it recovers E[Y]: average the slice means, weighted by how often each slice happens.
  • Among all functions of X, E[Y|X] is the one minimising expected squared error. Regression is trying to find it.
  • So MSE learns the conditional mean and MAE learns the conditional median, and on skewed targets those differ.

1. The mental model: one mean per slice, threaded into a curve

Condition on X and you get a distribution over Y, as lesson 141 showed. Take its mean and you get one number for that slice. Do it for every slice and the numbers trace a curve.

That curve is E[Y|X]. It is a function, and evaluating it at a particular x gives E[Y|X = x], a number. The distinction between the function and its values is the step that stalls people, and it is worth being explicit: because which slice you are in depends on X, and X is random, E[Y|X] is a random variable.

2. The tower property

E[ E[Y|X] ] = E[Y] Read as: take the conditional mean, which is a random variable because it depends on X, and average it over the distribution of X. The result is the plain unconditional mean of Y. The inner expectation is over Y given X, the outer one over X.

In words: average the slice means, weighting each by how often that slice occurs, and you are back where you started. It is the formal version of something you already do when you compute a weighted average of group means.

3. The theorem: the conditional mean is the best predictor

Theorem Suppose E[Y2] is finite. Among all functions g of X, the one minimising E[(Y - g(X))2] is g(X) = E[Y|X].

Proof. The shape of the argument is to split the error into two pieces that cannot interfere, and then observe that only one of them is yours to control.

Write m(X) for E[Y|X] and take any competing predictor g(X). Add and subtract m(X) inside the squared error.

E[(Y - g(X))2] = E[( (Y - m(X)) + (m(X) - g(X)) )2] Read as: the error of g is rewritten by inserting m(X) and taking it away again, which changes nothing, and then grouping the result into two brackets: the part m could not predict, and the part by which g differs from m.

Expanding the square gives three terms: the squared residual of m, the squared gap between g and m, and twice their product. The step doing the real work is showing that the cross term vanishes.

E[ (Y - m(X)) · (m(X) - g(X)) ] = 0 Read as: the average of the residual multiplied by any function of X is zero. It follows by conditioning on X first: given X, the second factor is a fixed number and comes out, and what is left is E[Y - m(X) | X], which is zero by the definition of m.

So the total error is the sum of two non-negative pieces with no interaction.

E[(Y - g(X))2] = E[(Y - m(X))2] + E[(m(X) - g(X))2] Read as: the error of any predictor equals the irreducible error of the conditional mean plus the squared distance from your predictor to that conditional mean. The first term does not involve g at all.

The first term is fixed. The second is a square, so it is at least zero, and it is zero exactly when g equals m. That completes the proof, and it also delivers a picture: E[Y|X] is the orthogonal projection of Y onto the space of functions of X, and the vanishing cross term is the right angle.

4. What this decides about your model

If your model is trained on squared error and has enough capacity, it is trying to output E[Y|X]. That is not a tendency, it is what the loss is minimised by.

When the true conditional distribution is bimodal, its mean can sit where nothing ever happens. A model asked to predict the next video frame under squared error will output the average of the plausible futures, which is a blur that is not a plausible future at all. That artefact is not a capacity failure and more data will not fix it; the loss asked for the mean and the mean is a blur.

Change the loss and you change the target. Absolute error is minimised by the conditional median, and on a skewed target those are visibly different numbers.

5. The law of total variance

Var(Y) = E[Var(Y|X)] + Var(E[Y|X]) Read as: the total spread of Y splits into the average spread left inside each slice, which no function of X can remove, plus the spread of the slice means themselves, which is what conditioning on X bought you. The two are usually called unexplained and explained variance.

It is the same split the proof produced, written for variances instead of errors, and it is the seed of the bias-variance decomposition that Module 09 develops.

6. Worked example: what a load bucket buys you

Predict latency from an eight-level GPU-load bucket, on all 40,000 requests.

  1. Take the unconditional mean. E[Y] = 201.34 ms, with variance 26,719.0.
  2. Compute one mean per bucket. That is E[Y|X], eight numbers, one for each level.
  3. Check the tower property. Averaging those eight, weighted by bucket size, returns 201.34 ms exactly.
  4. Measure the unexplained part. The size-weighted average of the eight within-bucket variances is 23,363.9.
  5. Measure the explained part. The variance of the eight slice means, weighted the same way, is 3,355.1.
  6. Check they add up. 23,363.9 + 3,355.1 = 26,719.0, which is Var(Y) to the last digit.
  7. Read the share. The bucket explains 3,355.1 ÷ 26,719.0 = 12.6 percent of the variance in latency.
  8. What changes if you condition on more. Adding tier and output tokens moves variance from the first column to the second and can never move it the other way, because conditioning on more can only shrink the average within-slice spread. The 23,363.9 is not a floor on all prediction, it is the floor given this one feature.
code/0155-conditional-expectation-and-mmse.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
frame["load"] = pd.qcut(frame["gpu_util"], 8, labels=False)
y = frame["latency_ms"].to_numpy(float)

m = frame.groupby("load")["latency_ms"].transform("mean").to_numpy()   # E[Y|X]

assert np.isclose(m.mean(), y.mean())                    # the tower property
within = ((y - m) ** 2).mean()                           # E[Var(Y|X)]
between = ((m - y.mean()) ** 2).mean()                   # Var(E[Y|X])
assert np.isclose(within + between, y.var())             # total variance

resid = y - m
print("residual against any function of X:", np.corrcoef(resid, m)[0, 1])

The full program attacks the theorem by brute force, trying six hand-built competitors and twenty thousand random perturbations of the eight slice values, and none of them beats the conditional mean. It also checks the residual is uncorrelated with several arbitrary functions of X, which is the right angle in the figure above, measured.

7. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A network trained on MSE faces a target whose true conditional law is bimodal at 0 and at 10. What does it output?
Squared error is minimised by the conditional mean, and the mean of a bimodal law can sit in its empty middle. The first option is what a mode-seeking loss would give and is the intuition people import by mistake. This is exactly why MSE-trained generative models blur, and why more capacity does not help: the loss asked for the mean.
What kind of object is E[Y|X]?
Its value depends on which slice you are in, and which slice you are in is random, so it is a random variable. The second option is E[Y], which is what E[Y|X] averages back down to under the tower property, and confusing the two is the most common stall on this page.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Delivery time given a route is 20 minutes with probability 0.5 and 60 minutes with probability 0.5, a genuinely bimodal conditional law.

Find the prediction an MSE-trained model makes and the prediction an MAE-trained model makes, compute each loss under both, and say what you would actually ship to a customer.

Hint

For the median of a two-point distribution with equal weights, any value between the two points minimises absolute error, so the answer is an interval rather than a point. Check that claim by computing the absolute loss at 20, at 40 and at 60.

Show the worked solution
  1. The conditional mean. 0.5 × 20 + 0.5 × 60 = 40 minutes.
  2. Squared loss at 40. 0.5 × (20-40)2 + 0.5 × (60-40)2 = 0.5 × 400 + 0.5 × 400 = 400.
  3. Squared loss at 20. 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 1,600 = 800, which is worse, as the theorem requires.
  4. Absolute loss at 40. 0.5 × 20 + 0.5 × 20 = 20.
  5. Absolute loss at 20. 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 40 = 20, the same. And at 60 it is also 20.
  6. Read that. Every value from 20 to 60 gives an absolute loss of 20, so MAE does not pick a unique answer here. The median is the whole interval.
  7. The point that matters. A delivery never takes 40 minutes. It takes 20 or it takes 60, and 40 is the one answer guaranteed to be wrong every single time.
  8. What to ship. Not a point estimate. Ship the two modes, or a range, or a probability: "20 minutes, or 60 if the route is congested, roughly even odds". Any single number is answering a question the customer did not ask, and the loss function chose that number rather than anyone deciding it should be the answer.

Sanity check. The squared loss at the mean must be the smallest available, and 400 is below 800. The absolute loss must be flat across the whole interval between two equally weighted points, so if you found a strict minimum somewhere inside it, recheck: the absolute value is piecewise linear and its slope is zero between the two atoms.

Your teacher. Take a model you own that is trained on squared error and plot the distribution of its targets within a few narrow bins of its inputs. If any of those conditional distributions has two humps, your model is predicting the valley between them and calling it an answer. The fix is a different output head or a different loss, not a bigger network.
