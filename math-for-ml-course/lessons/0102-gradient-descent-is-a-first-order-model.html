Gradient descent is a first-order model, taken one step at a time

working 11 min The rate is Bubeck, Convex Optimization: Algorithms and Complexity, Theorem 3.3. · arXiv:1405.4980

The one-minute version

  • Gradient descent is not "roll downhill". It is build a straight-line model here, trust it briefly, throw it away, build another.
  • The straight-line model has no bottom, so the step has to be bounded. That is where the learning rate comes from, and it is part of the derivation rather than a knob added afterwards.
  • On a convex smooth objective the gap falls like 1/t. Ten times the steps buys one more digit.
  • Every step reads every row of your data. Lesson 104 is what happens when you refuse to pay that.

1. The model you are actually stepping along

You are standing at a parameter vector θ. You cannot see the objective's whole surface, and computing it everywhere is out of the question. What you can afford is its value here and its slope here. So you build the only thing those two facts support: a flat plane through your current point, tilted to match the slope.

J(θ + d)  ≈  J(θ) + ∇J(θ)T d θ: where you are standing. d: a displacement, the step you are considering. J(θ): the objective's value here, one number. ∇J(θ): the gradient here, a vector with one entry per parameter, pointing in the direction of steepest increase. ∇J(θ)T d: the dot product of the gradient with your step, which is how much the linear model predicts the objective will change. ≈: this is an approximation, and the whole page is about how far you may trust it.

2. The catch that forces a learning rate

Now minimise that model. Push d along the negative gradient and the predicted value falls. Push twice as far and it falls twice as much. Push a thousand times as far and the model happily promises a thousand times the improvement.

A plane has no bottom. Taken literally, the first-order model says to step infinitely far and reach minus infinity, which is obviously wrong, and the reason it is wrong is that the model was only ever accurate near where it was built.

The reframing The learning rate is not a tuning parameter bolted onto gradient descent. It is the answer to "how far do I trust this approximation?", and something has to answer that question or the method does not terminate.

3. The update rule

θk+1 = θk − η ∇J(θk) θk: the parameters after k steps. η: the learning rate, a single positive number, the bound from the previous section. ∇J(θk): the gradient of the objective evaluated at the current parameters. The minus sign: the gradient points uphill and we are minimising, so we walk against it.

4. What "it converges" is actually worth

The rate below assumes the objective is convex and β-smooth, meaning its gradient never changes faster than a rate β. Under those, with the step set to 1/β:

J(θt) − J(θ⋆)  ≤  2 β ‖θ1 − θ⋆‖2 / (t − 1) θt: the parameters after t steps. θ⋆: a minimiser. The left side: how much worse you still are than the best possible, the "gap". β: the smoothness constant, the largest rate at which the gradient can change. ‖θ1 − θ⋆‖2: the squared distance from where you started to the answer. t − 1: the step count, and the only thing on the right that moves.

Read the shape rather than the constants. The gap falls like 1/t, so to halve it you double the work, and to gain one decimal digit you multiply the step count by ten. That is the good case, the one where the problem is convex and smooth and you chose the step well.

The proof, in four moves

  1. Every step makes progress. Smoothness gives a quadratic upper bound on the objective. Substitute the step θ − (1/β)∇J and the bound collapses to J(θk+1) − J(θk) ≤ −(1/2β) ‖∇J(θk)‖2. The objective strictly falls unless the gradient is zero.
  2. Convexity turns the gap into a gradient. The chord inequality from the previous page gives J(θk) − J(θ⋆) ≤ ‖∇J(θk)‖ × ‖θk − θ⋆‖, so a large remaining gap forces a large gradient.
  3. The distance to the answer never grows. This is the step that does the real work, and the one most summaries skip. Smoothness gives co-coercivity, and expanding ‖θk+1 − θ⋆‖2 with it leaves ‖θk − θ⋆‖2 minus a non-negative quantity. So every distance in step 2 is at most the starting distance.
  4. Combine, and telescope. Write dk for the gap. Steps 1 to 3 give dk+1 ≤ dk − dk2/(2βD2). Divide through by dkdk+1 and you get 1/dk+1 − 1/dk ≥ 1/(2βD2). Every step adds a fixed amount to the reciprocal of the gap. Summing from 1 to t − 1 gives the theorem.

Sanity check. The reciprocal growing linearly is exactly what a 1/t decay looks like from the other side, so step 4 and the stated rate are the same claim written two ways.

5. Worked: three steps by hand, at two learning rates

Take J(w) = (w − 3)2, whose answer is obviously 3. The derivative is J'(w) = 2(w − 3), so the update is w ← w − 2η(w − 3), which rearranges to w ← (1 − 2η)w + 6η. Everything about the behaviour is in that one factor.

  1. Set η = 0.1. The factor is 1 − 0.2 = 0.8.
  2. Step from w = 0. 0.8(0) + 0.6 = 0.6.
  3. Step again. 0.8(0.6) + 0.6 = 1.08.
  4. And again. 0.8(1.08) + 0.6 = 1.464, then 1.7712, then 2.0170.
  5. Notice the steps shrinking. They go 0.600, 0.480, 0.384, 0.307, 0.246, although η never changed. The gradient itself is shrinking as w approaches 3.
  6. Now η = 0.6. The factor is 1 − 1.2 = −0.2, which is negative.
  7. Step from w = 0. 3.6, then 2.88, then 3.024, then 2.9952, then 3.0010. It overshoots every time and still converges, because the size of the factor is below 1.

Sanity check. After five steps at η = 0.1 the iterate is 2.017, not two thirds of the way to 3. That is what a 1/t rate feels like from the inside, and it is the honest speed of the method on a problem whose answer you could have written down.

What changes at η = 1.0? The factor becomes exactly −1, and the sequence is 0, 6, 0, 6, forever. It neither converges nor diverges. That is a boundary, and it is the whole subject of the next page.

What it means. The two runs converge for different reasons and the single number 1 − 2η predicts both. Every question about whether a learning rate is safe reduces to a question about a number like that one, which is why the next page can make the whole thing quantitative.

code/0102-gradient-descent-is-a-first-order-model.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def numeric_gradient(design, target, theta, step=1e-6):
    """Central differences. Two objective evaluations per parameter."""
    gradient = np.zeros_like(theta)
    for index in range(len(theta)):
        forward, backward = theta.copy(), theta.copy()
        forward[index] += step
        backward[index] -= step
        gradient[index] = (objective(design, target, forward)
                           - objective(design, target, backward)) / (2.0 * step)
    return gradient

The program checks the analytic gradient against this numeric one on all thirteen parameters before it descends anything. On the credit dataset the worst relative disagreement is 1.1 × 10-8. Anything above about 10-5 means the analytic gradient is wrong, and every optimizer in the rest of this module would then be walking confidently in the wrong direction with nothing to tell you.

Show the worked solution
  1. Derive the update. J'(w) = 2(w − 3), so w ← w − 2η(w − 3) = (1 − 2η)w + 6η.
  2. At η = 0.1 the factor is 0.8. The sequence runs 0, 0.6000, 1.0800, 1.4640, 1.7712, 2.0170.
  3. Read it. Monotone increase, decelerating, still short of 3 after five steps.
  4. At η = 0.6 the factor is −0.2. The sequence runs 0, 3.6000, 2.8800, 3.0240, 2.9952, 3.0010.
  5. Read it. It overshoots on every step and the overshoot shrinks by a factor of five each time, because the size of −0.2 is 0.2.
  6. Predict η = 1.0. The factor is exactly −1. The sequence is 0, 6, 0, 6, 0, 6 and never settles: it neither converges nor diverges, and it is the same distance from the answer at every even step.
  7. Name the general rule you have just found. The iteration converges exactly when the size of 1 − 2η is below 1, which for this problem means η below 1. That is the boundary Lesson 103 generalises to any curvature.

Sanity check. Both of your sequences must approach 3, and the second must cross it while the first never does. If your η = 0.6 run is monotone you have used 1 − η instead of 1 − 2η, and dropped the factor of 2 that came from differentiating a square.
