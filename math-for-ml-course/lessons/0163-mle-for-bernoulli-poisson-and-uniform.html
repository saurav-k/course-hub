Setting the derivative to zero is a step in the recipe, not the recipe

working 12 min The Poisson MLE, the uniform MLE that calculus cannot find, and the bias it carries. · code/0163

The one-minute version

  • The Poisson MLE is the sample mean, and the derivation is four lines.
  • The uniform MLE is the sample maximum, and calculus never appears, because the likelihood has a wall rather than a peak.
  • That estimate is always an underestimate: its expectation is n/(n+1) times the truth, so it is biased low at every n.
  • Multiplying by (n+1)/n fixes the bias exactly, and the fix is worth knowing because the bias is not an oddity - biased and consistent is the ordinary case.
  • Maximum likelihood will fit a family that does not fit your data and say nothing about it. It optimises the parameter, never the model.

1. The Bernoulli, worked end to end

Setting. n independent observations, each 1 with probability p. k came out 1.

What is assumed: the observations are independent and share the same p. Both are spent in step 1, exactly as in Lesson 129.

  1. One observation. Its probability is px(1−p)1−x, which equals p when x is 1 and 1−p when x is 0.
  2. The whole dataset. By independence, multiply: L(p) = pk(1−p)n−k, since k of the exponents pick up a p and the other n−k pick up a 1−p.
  3. Take the log. l(p) = k log p + (n−k) log(1−p). The product became a sum.
  4. Differentiate. The derivative of log p is 1/p, and of log(1−p) is −1/(1−p). So l′(p) = k/p − (n−k)/(1−p).
  5. Set to zero and clear the fractions. k(1−p) = (n−k)p, so k − kp = np − kp, so k = np.
  6. Solve. p-hat = k/n.
The step that does the real work is step 2, and independence is spent there and nowhere else. Step 5 is worth a second look: the kp terms cancel on both sides, which is why the answer is so clean. And the result is the obvious one - the proportion you observed - which is the point. A recipe that returned something surprising here would be a recipe to distrust.

2. The Poisson, where the recipe works exactly as advertised

Setting. n independent Poisson counts with rate λ, from Lesson 132.

  1. One observation. Its probability is λxe−λ ÷ x!.
  2. The whole dataset. By independence, multiply all n of them.
  3. Take the log. l(λ) = −nλ + (∑xi) log λ − ∑log(xi!). The last term has no λ in it, so it cannot affect the argmax and is dropped.
  4. Differentiate. l′(λ) = −n + (∑xi)/λ.
  5. Set to zero and solve. n = (∑xi)/λ, so λ-hat = (∑xi)/n = x-bar.
The step worth pausing on is step 3, where the factorial term is discarded. It is not negligible - it is enormous - it simply does not contain the parameter, so it shifts the whole curve vertically and moves the peak not at all. That move recurs in every likelihood derivation you will read, and it is why so many of them quote the log-likelihood "up to a constant".

3. And here is the recipe returning a confident wrong answer

Run that on the pages_viewed column. The sum is 116,418 over 20,000 sessions, so λ-hat = 5.8209. The recipe worked, the derivative was zero, the answer is a number.

A Poisson forces its variance to equal its mean, from Lesson 132. This column's variance is 1,130.15 against a mean of 5.8209, a ratio of 194.2. The data is nowhere near Poisson - the overdispersion is bots viewing hundreds of pages - and maximum likelihood said nothing. It returned the best Poisson fit to data that is not Poisson, which is exactly what it promised to do and is not what you wanted. The diagnostic from Lesson 134 catches this in one line; the MLE never will.

4. The uniform, where step three has nothing to do

Setting. n independent draws from Uniform(0, θ), from Lesson 130. Estimate θ.

The density is 1/θ on the interval and zero outside it, so the likelihood of the whole dataset is:

L(θ) = θ−n if θ ≥ max(xi),   and 0 otherwise θ: the unknown upper end of the range. max(xi): the largest value you actually observed. The two cases matter: a θ smaller than something you have already seen would have made that observation impossible, so its likelihood is exactly zero, not merely small.

Now look at what that function does. To the left of the sample maximum it is flat at zero. At the sample maximum it jumps. To the right it is θ−n, which is strictly decreasing - bigger θ spreads the same probability over more room, so every observation becomes less likely.

So the maximum is at the smallest legal θ, which is the sample maximum: θ-hat = max(xi). No differentiation anywhere.

5. And that estimate is always too small

Claim. E[max(xi)] = (n/(n+1)) × θ, so the bias is −θ/(n+1).

What is assumed: n independent draws from Uniform(0, θ).

The shape: get the CDF of the maximum, differentiate for its density, then integrate for its mean.

  1. The maximum is below t exactly when every draw is. So P(max ≤ t) = P(x1 ≤ t) × … × P(xn ≤ t) by independence.
  2. Use the uniform CDF. Each factor is t/θ from Lesson 130, so P(max ≤ t) = (t/θ)n.
  3. Differentiate for the density. n tn−1 ÷ θn, on 0 to θ.
  4. Integrate t times the density. The integral of n tn/θn from 0 to θ is (n/θn) × (θn+1/(n+1)) = nθ/(n+1).
  5. Read the bias. nθ/(n+1) − θ = −θ/(n+1), which is negative for every finite n.
The step that does the real work is step 1, turning a statement about a maximum into a product, and independence is spent exactly there. The result is also obvious once seen: the largest value you drew cannot exceed the true upper end, so the estimate can only ever be too small, and the only question was by how much. Multiplying by (n+1)/n corrects it exactly.

6. Worked: measuring the bias and the fix

The screen_brightness column genuinely is Uniform(0, 100), so the truth is known.

  1. Predict the bias at n = 5. −100/6 = −16.67, so the estimate should average 83.33.
  2. Predict at n = 100. 100 × 100/101 = 99.0099.
  3. Measure at n = 100. Redrawing many samples, the MLE averages 98.9817 against that prediction of 99.0099.
  4. Apply the fix. Multiply by (n+1)/n = 101/100, giving an average of 99.9715.
  5. Compare. The MLE is below 100 at every n, by construction. The debiased version sits on 100.
  6. Watch the bias shrink. At n = 5 it is 16.67, at n = 100 it is 0.99, at n = 1,000 it would be 0.0999. It goes to zero, so the estimator is consistent.

Sanity check. The MLE can never exceed 100, because it is the maximum of numbers that are all below 100. If a measured average had come out above the truth, the simulation would be wrong rather than surprising.

What changes at n = 20,000. The predicted bias is 100/20,001 = 0.005, which is smaller than the column's own rounding, so the MLE and the debiased estimate become indistinguishable in the data. The correction matters when n is small and is invisible when n is large, which is the general shape of every bias correction in this module - including the n−1 in the sample variance.

7. Tying it back: biased and consistent is the ordinary case

Lesson 161 defined consistency and warned that it is a separate promise from unbiasedness. Here is the cleanest example in the course:

  • The uniform MLE is biased at every finite n, by exactly −θ/(n+1).
  • It is consistent, because that bias goes to zero.
  • It is also, at any given n, the estimator that makes the observed data most probable.

All three are true simultaneously, and they are not in tension. Maximum likelihood optimises probability of the data, and nothing in that objective asks the estimator to be centred on the truth. Unbiasedness was never promised, so its absence is not a defect.

Do not conclude that the debiased version is simply better. Multiplying by (n+1)/n removes the bias and increases the variance, and Lesson 161's identity is what decides which wins on MSE. For this estimator the debiased version does win, and it is worth knowing that the question had to be asked rather than assumed.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does differentiating the likelihood fail to find the MLE for a uniform on [0, θ]?
The likelihood jumps at the sample maximum and decreases after it, so the largest value sits at the edge of the legal region where the derivative does not exist. The first option overstates it: the function is perfectly differentiable to the right of the wall, and its derivative there is negative rather than zero, which is exactly why the maximum is pushed back to the boundary. The fourth invents a second peak; the curve is monotone once it starts.
Fitting a Poisson to a column gave λ-hat = 5.82. The column's variance is 1,130. What does that tell you?
5.82 is the correct maximum likelihood estimate; it is the best Poisson fit to data that is not Poisson. The first option confuses a property the model asserts with a step in the calculation. The third is exactly backwards - the mean-equals-variance link is what makes the diagnostic possible. The fourth blames the sample, and 20,000 rows is not the problem: no sample size makes a wrong family right.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You draw 6 independent values from Uniform(0, θ) and the largest is 47.2.

Give the maximum likelihood estimate, its expected value if the truth were θ = 50, and the debiased estimate from your sample. Then say why the MLE can never be an overestimate.

Hint

The first is one number you already have. For the second use n/(n+1) with n = 6. For the third, multiply by the reciprocal of that factor.

Show the worked solution
  1. The MLE. θ-hat = max(xi) = 47.2. No calculus, and nothing to solve.
  2. Its expectation under θ = 50. (6/7) × 50 = 42.857.
  3. The implied bias. 42.857 − 50 = −7.143, which is −50/7 as the formula says.
  4. Debias this sample. Multiply by (n+1)/n = 7/6: 47.2 × 7/6 = 55.07.
  5. Why it cannot overestimate. Every observation is at most θ, so the largest of them is at most θ. The estimate is bounded above by the truth for every possible sample, which is a much stronger statement than "it is biased low on average".

Sanity check. The debiased estimate must exceed the MLE, since it multiplies by something above 1, and 55.07 does. Note that it also exceeds the true 50 on this particular sample - correcting a bias fixes the average across samples and says nothing about any single one, which is Lesson 161's distinction between bias and variance showing up in one number.

Your teacher. Two habits. First, before differentiating a likelihood, look at where its support depends on the parameter - if the legal range of the data moves as the parameter moves, expect a boundary answer. Second, after any fit, run the family's own diagnostic: mean against variance for a Poisson, the one-sigma band for a normal. The fit will never volunteer that the family is wrong.
