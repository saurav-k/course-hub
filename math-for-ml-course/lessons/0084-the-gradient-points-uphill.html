The gradient points uphill, and it is steepest only in the Euclidean sense

working 12 min The sentence every optimiser rests on, and the qualifier almost everyone drops from it.

The one-minute version

  • The rate of change along a direction is a dot product, which the chain rule hands you in one line.
  • Among unit directions, the negative gradient is provably the fastest way down, and its rate is exactly minus the gradient's length.
  • The proof is Cauchy-Schwarz and nothing else.
  • "Unit" was defined by the Euclidean norm. Measure length differently and a different direction wins, by 88.8 degrees on the sensor data.

1. The question

You are standing on a loss surface with seven dials. You may step one unit in any direction you like. Which direction takes you downhill fastest?

"Downhill" needs a number attached before it can be compared, and Lesson 83 gives you the ingredients: one partial derivative per dial, collected into a vector.

2. The directional derivative is a dot product

Walk from x along a direction u at speed one, and watch the function. That is a function of a single variable, so Lesson 80 applies to it, and Lesson 81 tells you how to differentiate it.

Duf(x) = d/dα f(x + αu) at α = 0 = u · ∇f(x) u: the direction you walk, a column vector of length 1. α: how far along you have walked, the only variable once the direction is fixed. ∇f(x): the gradient at the point you started from. u · ∇f(x): their dot product, which is a single number, the rate at which f changes per unit walked.

The chain rule does all the work: the inner function is x + αu, whose derivative in α is u, and the outer derivative is the gradient. Multiply and you have a dot product.

Why the length of u has to be pinnedWithout a constraint the question has no answer. Double u and you double the directional derivative, so you can make it as negative as you like by walking faster rather than by walking somewhere better. Fixing ||u|| = 1 is what turns "which direction" into a question with a winner, and it is also the step where a choice sneaks in.

3. The theorem

Theorem (steepest ascent and descent). Let f be differentiable at x with ∇f(x) not zero. Over all unit vectors in the Euclidean norm, Duf(x) is largest at u = ∇f/||∇f|| with value +||∇f||, and smallest at its negative with value −||∇f||.

What is assumed: differentiability, and that the gradient is not the zero vector. The shape of the argument: one inequality bounds every candidate, and one candidate attains the bound. The step that does the real work is the equality condition in Cauchy-Schwarz, because a bound nobody reaches proves nothing.

  1. Bound every candidate. Cauchy-Schwarz says |u · ∇f| ≤ ||u|| ||∇f||, and ||u|| = 1, so every unit direction gives a value between −||∇f|| and +||∇f||.
  2. Ask when the bound is tight. Cauchy-Schwarz holds with equality exactly when the two vectors are parallel.
  3. Build the candidate. Take u = ∇f/||∇f||, which is parallel and has length 1. Its dot product with ∇f is ||∇f||² ÷ ||∇f|| = ||∇f||.
  4. Do the same downhill. The negative of that vector gives −||∇f||, the other end of the range.
  5. Conclude. A bound that is attained is the extremum, so both claims hold. QED

4. Which also tells you the gradient is perpendicular to the contours

Read the ninety-degree point off that curve. A direction at right angles to the gradient has a directional derivative of zero, which means the function does not change along it. A path along which the function does not change is a contour. So the gradient is perpendicular to the contour through every point where it is not zero.

5. Worked: five directions ranked on real data

Twelve thousand sensor readings, six columns predicting a seventh, at the intercept-only guess from Lesson 83. The gradient has length ||∇f|| = 783.8356.

  1. Compute the length. ||∇f|| = 783.8356, so no unit direction can do better than −783.8356.
  2. Take the negative gradient. Its directional derivative is −783.8356, exactly the bound, as the theorem promises.
  3. Try the pressure axis alone. −777.0013, which is 99.13 per cent of the best.
  4. Try the humidity axis alone. −10.0239, which is 1.28 per cent.
  5. Try equal magnitude on every dial. −365.6725, or 46.65 per cent.
  6. Check the bound by brute force. Fifty thousand random unit directions: the most negative found is −776.5780. None beats −783.8356.

Sanity check. Every entry must lie between −783.8356 and +783.8356, because Cauchy-Schwarz says so. A value outside that range means the direction was not normalised, which is the one arithmetic slip this table can hide.

What changes if the columns are standardised first? The gradient rotates, and pressure stops being nearly optimal on its own. The ranking is a fact about units and Lesson 89 turns it into a condition number.

In words. On unscaled data the negative gradient is barely better than "move the biggest-numbered column", which is a warning about the data rather than a discovery about the method.

6. The qualifier: steepest with respect to what?

Step back to the moment the length of u was pinned. "Length" meant the Euclidean norm, and that was a choice rather than a fact. Boyd and Vandenberghe define the normalised steepest descent direction for any norm:

Δxnsd = argmin { ∇f(x) · v : ||v|| = 1 } v: a candidate direction. ||v|| = 1: the constraint, and the whole point is that this bar can mean different things. argmin: the direction achieving the smallest value, not the value itself. With the Euclidean norm this returns the negative gradient; with another norm it returns something else.
The reframingSteepest descent is not one direction. It is a family indexed by the norm, and ordinary gradient descent is the member you get by choosing the Euclidean one, usually without noticing that you chose. Every optimiser in Module 06 that beats plain gradient descent does so by choosing differently.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why must the direction be constrained to unit length for "which way is steepest" to have an answer?
Double the direction and you double the directional derivative, so without a constraint there is no smallest value to find. The first is false, a dot product is defined for any two vectors. The second is false and easy to check: the gradient's length is 783.8 here. The third reverses the mechanism, since the cosine is bounded whatever you do and it is the length factor that is not.
On the raw sensor regression, descending along pressure alone reaches 99.13 per cent of the best rate and along humidity alone 1.28 per cent. What follows?
Pressure runs in hundreds of kilopascals and the other columns near one, so it dominates the gradient by units alone. The first is the misconception: a rate of descent is not a measure of predictive value, and standardising the columns changes the ranking without changing the data. The third is false and the program checks every partial against the definition. The fourth is a true-sounding claim about a different question, since nothing here bounds the iteration count.

Show the worked solution

  2. Along the first axis. (1, 0) · (−777.0013, −10.0239) = −777.0013.

Your teacher. If the norm dependence still feels like a technicality, ask an agent to draw the unit ball of the L1 norm, a diamond, and mark which of its points reaches furthest against a gradient that is not axis-aligned. The answer is always a corner, which is the same fact that makes L1 regularisation produce sparse solutions in Module 06.

Primary source to go deeper

Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization, section 9.4, is the clearest statement in print that steepest descent is defined relative to a norm, and section 9.4.1 says outright that "the steepest descent method for the Euclidean norm coincides with the gradient descent method" and derives the quadratic-norm direction as −P−1∇f. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, section 4.3, gives the cos-theta minimisation this page's theorem restates.
