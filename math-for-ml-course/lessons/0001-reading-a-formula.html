Module 01 · Foundations · Lesson 01

Every formula is a loop you already know how to write

foundation 10 min The notation is not a second subject. It is a compression of something you do every day.

The one-minute version

  • A sigma is a for loop with a running total, and it has exactly three parts: where the index starts, where it stops, and what gets added.
  • An index is a bound variable. Renaming it changes nothing, which is why two papers writing the same formula with different letters are writing the same formula.
  • A superscript in brackets picks out an example, a subscript picks out a feature, and mixing the two up is the most expensive notation error in the field.
  • A pi is the same loop with a running product, and the fact that products of small numbers collapse is what pushes the whole field into logarithms four lessons from now.

1. The problem is the spelling, not the mathematics

Here is a line from a real algorithm, printed exactly as its textbook prints it.

ĝ ← (1/m) ∇θ Σi L(f(x(i); θ), y(i)) Read as: take the m examples in this batch, run each one through the model, measure how wrong it was, add up those m numbers, divide by m, and ask how that average would change if the parameters moved. m is the batch size, i counts the examples in the batch, x is one input, y is its correct answer, θ is every number the model has learned, f is the model, L measures the mistake, and the ∇ is the only symbol on this line that belongs to a later module.

Most readers who stall on that line do not stall on the calculus. They stall because eleven symbols arrived at once and nobody said which were nouns. This page is about the four doing the structural work: the index, the sigma, the pi, and the two places a number can be labelled.

2. A sigma is a for loop

The sigma has three parts, and every one of them appears in the for loop you would write instead.

So Σi=14 ai is four steps, not one operation. Watching it run once is usually enough to stop it being frightening.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are load-bearing later.

The index is a bound variable. It exists only between the sigma and the end of the expression, so Σi=1n ai and Σk=1n ak are the same number. This is why two papers can write the same formula with different letters and mean the same thing, and it is why the letter itself is never worth arguing about.

An empty sum is zero. If the range is empty the loop body never runs and the accumulator keeps its starting value. The matching fact for a product is that an empty product is one, because a running product starts at 1. Both look like pedantry until lesson 07, where they are the difference between a model with no evidence predicting its prior and predicting nothing at all.

3. The two places a label can sit

This is the one piece of notation that costs real money when it is misread.

A dataset is a table: rows are examples and columns are features. That orientation is fixed for this whole course, so it is worth fixing in your head now. A bracketed superscript picks a row, and a subscript picks a column.

The brackets are doing real work. Without them, x2 is the ordinary square of a number, and there would be no way to tell it apart from "the second example". That is the entire reason for a piece of punctuation most readers skip.

4. A pi is the same loop, multiplying

Everything above holds with one word changed. A pi runs the same loop and keeps a running product instead of a running total, and it starts at 1 rather than 0.

Πi=1n pi  =  p1 × p2 × … × pn Read as: multiply together the n numbers p1 through pn. i is the index, n is where it stops, and pi is the i-th thing being multiplied.

You meet a pi whenever a model assumes its examples are independent, because independent things happening together multiply. Hold one fact about it: those probabilities sit below 1, so the product shrinks at every step, and a few hundred steps reach a number your computer stores as exactly zero. That is lesson 07, and it is why this field is written in logarithms.

5. Two sums in a row

A double sum is a nested loop, and the trap is thinking the two indices move together.

Σi=1m Σj=1d Xi,j2 Read as: for every one of the m rows, walk all d columns, square the entry and add it on. i counts rows, j counts columns, and they are independent: the inner loop runs all the way through for each single step of the outer one.

That expression has m × d terms, not m and not m + d. Tie the indices together and you get the m diagonal entries instead, which is a different and also useful quantity written Xi,i, so the notation tells you which one is meant.

6. Reading it end to end

Back to the opening line, with every symbol now named. Take a batch of 32 support tickets, each described by 45 features.

  1. Find the loop. The sigma is over i, and i counts examples inside the batch. So the loop runs 32 times.
  2. Find the body. Each pass computes L(f(x(i); θ), y(i)): run ticket i through the model, compare with its correct answer, get one number.
  3. Find what is not indexed. θ carries no i. There is one parameter vector shared by all 32 tickets, and that is why the 32 numbers are averaged rather than kept apart.
  4. Read the prefactor. (1/m) Σi is "add up 32 things and divide by 32". It is an average, written the long way.
  5. Count, as a check. The double sum Σi Σj Xi,j2 over this batch has 32 × 45 = 1,440 scalar terms.
Sanity check. The term count is the product of the two loop bounds and nothing else. What changes if the batch doubles to 64: the number of terms doubles, and the average barely moves. That is the whole reason for the 1/m.

7. The same sum, three ways

The program for this lesson computes that double sum three ways over the real ticket data and asserts all three agree. The point is not that NumPy is faster. It is that the notation, the loop and the library call are one object wearing three costumes.

code/0001-reading-a-formula.py · runnable Python, numpy and pandas only · download · data: tickets.csv
def sum_of_squares_by_loop(matrix):
    """The formula read literally: two nested loops and one running total."""
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(matrix.shape[0]):
        for j in range(matrix.shape[1]):
            total += matrix[i, j] ** 2
    return total

by_loop   = sum_of_squares_by_loop(matrix)
by_numpy  = float((matrix ** 2).sum())
by_pandas = float((pd.DataFrame(matrix) ** 2).to_numpy().sum())
assert np.isclose(by_loop, by_numpy)
assert np.isclose(by_loop, by_pandas)

It also renames i and j to k and q and asserts the answer does not move, which is the bound-variable fact checked rather than claimed.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

In x(3), what does the bracketed superscript pick out?
The bracketed superscript walks down the rows, and a row is one example. The second option is the subscript's job, written x3. The third is what a bare superscript means in ordinary algebra, which is precisely why the brackets are there. The fourth names a real thing that this notation never denotes.
A ticket contains none of a model's known tokens, so its score sums over an empty range. What is the sum?
The accumulator starts at 0 and the loop body never runs, so the answer is 0. One is the empty product, and confusing the two is how a log-space score ends up starting in the wrong place. The fourth option is true of naive Bayes and answers a different question, because the prior is a separate term added outside this sum. The first is what a careless implementation does, not what the notation says.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Here is a loss function with a regulariser attached. Take a batch of m = 32 tickets described by d = 45 features.

J(θ) = (1/m) Σi=1m (y(i) − ŷ(i))2  +  λ Σj=1d θj2 Read as: the average squared mistake over the batch, plus a penalty on the size of the parameters. y is the correct answer, ŷ is the model's answer, θj is the j-th parameter, and λ decides how much the penalty counts.

Answer three questions. (a) What does i range over, and what does j? (b) How many squared terms are in each sum? (c) Why does the second sum carry no i?

Hint

Read each sigma's own bounds and nothing else. Neither index escapes its own sum, so the answer to (a) is two different things, not one.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the first sum's bounds. It runs i = 1 to m, and m is the batch size, so i counts examples: tickets 1 through 32.
  2. Read the second sum's bounds. It runs j = 1 to d, and the thing being summed is θj, a parameter. So j counts parameters: 1 through 45.
  3. Count each. 32 squared terms in the first sum, 45 in the second. They are different sums over different things and the counts have no reason to match.
  4. Ask what an index on the second sum would mean. There is one parameter vector, shared by every ticket in the batch. Writing θj(i) would claim each ticket had its own copy.
  5. State the consequence. If the penalty carried an i and were summed over the batch, you would penalise the same 45 numbers 32 times over, so the strength of the regulariser would silently depend on your batch size.

Sanity check. The two sums have different bounds, so any answer in which both counts come out equal has merged the two indices. And the penalty term contains no y at all, which is the giveaway that it is about the model rather than about the data.

Your teacher. This page names no theorem, and that is honest rather than a gap: notation is a convention, not a result, and there is nothing here to prove. If a formula in a later lesson still stops you, ask an agent to "name every symbol in this expression in words, say what each index ranges over, and rewrite it as a Python loop" - that request works on almost anything and it is the habit this page is trying to build.
