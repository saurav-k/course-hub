Module 04 · Eigenvalues, SVD and PCA · Lesson 69

PCA is the SVD of the centred data

frontier 16 min The module's capstone. Module 06 owns the variational route, module 08 owns covariance as a statistical object.

The one-minute version

  • Centre the data, take its SVD, and the right singular vectors are the principal directions. No covariance matrix is ever built.
  • The eigenvalue of each direction is σ2/(n-1), and it is the variance along it, not merely proportional to it.
  • The reconstruction error from keeping M directions is exactly the sum of the eigenvalues you dropped.
  • So keeping the most variance and losing the least are one act, because the total is fixed.
  • PCA never sees a label, so the direction that separates your classes can be the one it discards first.

1. Setting up, and one word about orientation

Throughout this course X is n by d: one row per sample, one column per feature. Some sources write it the other way up, and every formula below changes shape if you do, so check the orientation before copying a formula from elsewhere.

Centre first, by subtracting each column's mean. This is not hygiene, it is the definition: covariance is built from deviations, so XTX computed on uncentred data is not a covariance at all, and its leading direction points at where the cloud is rather than at how it spreads.

S = (1 / (n - 1)) XcT Xc Read as: Xc is the centred data, n is the number of samples, and S is the d by d sample covariance matrix. Dividing by n - 1 rather than n is the sample convention this course uses everywhere.

By lesson 64 this is a Gram matrix, so it is symmetric and positive semidefinite for free, and lesson 62 therefore guarantees it an orthonormal eigenbasis with real, non-negative eigenvalues.

2. The SVD hands PCA over

Result 1 If Xc = UΣVT, then the columns of V are eigenvectors of S, with eigenvalues λi = σi2 / (n - 1).

Proof. Substitute the SVD straight into the Gram matrix.

XcTXc = (UΣVT)T(UΣVT) = VΣT(UTU)ΣVT = V(ΣTΣ)VT Read as: transposing a product reverses it, which brings the two copies of U together in the middle as UTU. That is the identity because U is orthogonal, so it vanishes, and ΣTΣ is the diagonal matrix of squared singular values.

Dividing by n - 1 gives S = V diag(σi2/(n-1)) VT. That is an orthogonal matrix times a diagonal times its transpose, which by lesson 62 is a spectral decomposition of S. Reading it column by column, Svi = (σi2/(n-1))vi.

The step that does the real work UTU collapsing in the middle, the same cancellation as lesson 63's principal-axes proof and for the same reason. Note what the proof did not need: no derivatives, no constrained optimisation, no Lagrange multiplier. That absence is why this page sits before module 05.

3. Two ways to say "best", and why they are one way

Two natural definitions of the best M-dimensional summary. Keep the most variance, or lose the least when you reconstruct. They look like different requests.

Result 2 With B holding the first M columns of V, the average squared reconstruction error is λM+1 + … + λd: exactly the eigenvalues dropped.

Proof. The columns of V are an orthonormal basis, so every centred sample expands as x = ∑j(vjTx)vj. The projection BBTx keeps only the first M terms, so the residual is exactly the tail, and because the vj are orthonormal its squared length is the sum of the squared coefficients. Summing over samples and dividing by n - 1, then exchanging the two sums:

(1/(n-1)) ∑x ∑j>M (vjTx)2 = ∑j>M vjT S vj = ∑j>M λj Read as: exchanging the order of the two sums lets the inner one over samples be recognised as the covariance matrix S. Then each vj is an eigenvector of it by Result 1, so vjTSvj is just λj.

The step that does the real work is that exchange of sums. Before it the expression is about individual samples; after it, it is about one matrix, and the eigenvalues can be substituted in.

Now the two requests collapse into one. The total variance trace(S) is the sum of all the eigenvalues and does not move. So keeping the largest and dropping the smallest maximises what is kept and minimises what is lost in the same act. There is no trade-off, because there is only one quantity.

4. PCA is not a regression line

This is the misconception the module most needs to prevent. Ordinary least squares minimises the vertical residual, treating x as known and y as the thing in error. PCA minimises the perpendicular distance, treating both coordinates as subject to error. Different criteria, different lines, same points.

5. Worked in full

  1. Six samples, two features. (1,2), (2,1), (3,4), (4,3), (5,6), (6,5).
  2. Centre. The mean is (3.5, 3.5), so the centred rows are (-2.5,-1.5), (-1.5,-2.5), (-0.5,0.5), (0.5,-0.5), (1.5,2.5), (2.5,1.5). They sum to (0, 0), which is the check that centring worked.
  3. Covariance. ∑x12 = 17.5, ∑x22 = 17.5, ∑x1x2 = 14.5. Dividing by n - 1 = 5, S = [[3.5, 2.9], [2.9, 3.5]].
  4. Eigenvalues. For a matrix [[a, b], [b, a]] they are always a + b and a - b, so 6.4 and 0.6, with eigenvectors (1, 1)/√2 and (-1, 1)/√2.
  5. Explained variance. trace(S) = 7, so PC1 carries 6.4/7 = 91.43 percent and PC2 carries 8.57 percent.
  6. The same answer through the SVD, per Result 1. The singular values of the centred matrix are 5.656854 and 1.732051. Then σ12/5 = 32/5 = 6.4 and σ22/5 = 3/5 = 0.6. Identical, and S was never formed.
  7. Sanity check. Three of them. 6.4 + 0.6 = 7 = trace(S). The reconstruction error from keeping only PC1 is measured at 0.6, matching λ2, which is Result 2 on six points. And the PC1 scores are -2.828, -2.828, 0, 0, 2.828, 2.828, whose mean is zero, as projections of centred data must have.
  8. What changes if. Skip step 2. On this symmetric toy the uncentred leading direction happens to come out near (0.707, 0.707) again, because the mean lies along the same diagonal. Move one point to (1, 20) and the uncentred direction swings towards the new mean while the centred one does not. Agreement on a symmetric example is a coincidence, not a licence.

6. Choosing M, honestly

Explained variance is a share of input variance. It is not an accuracy on any task, and PCA never saw a label. Choose M by the downstream metric when you have one, and use the curve only when you do not.

7. What is not pinned down, and what PCA cannot see

Promised on lesson 60 and paid off here: if v is a principal direction then so is -v, with the same eigenvalue. Two libraries, two machines or two runs may hand you opposite signs and both are right. Any code that branches on the sign of a loading is testing an arbitrary choice. When two eigenvalues are equal, the entire plane they span is an eigenspace and even the directions are not determined.

PCA is unsupervised, and that is a limitation rather than a feature. It ranks directions by variance, and variance is not importance. The direction that separates two classes can be a small-variance direction that PCA discards first. If you are reducing dimension before a supervised model, check that the components you kept still carry the signal, because nothing in the method arranged for them to.

8. Both routes, at 24 dimensions

code/0069-pca-through-the-svd.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
cov_values, cov_directions = pca_via_covariance(data)   # builds S, then eigh
svd_values, svd_directions = pca_via_svd(data)          # never builds S

assert np.abs(cov_values - svd_values).max() < 1e-12
# directions agree only UP TO SIGN, and -v solves S v = lambda v just as truly
alignment = np.abs(np.einsum("ij,ij->j", cov_directions, svd_directions))
assert np.allclose(alignment, 1.0)

The two routes agree on every eigenvalue to 7.2e-16 and on every direction up to sign. And then the number that makes section 7 concrete: on this dataset the two routes returned the same sign for only 12 of the 24 directions. They agree perfectly on the mathematics and disagree on half the signs, which is exactly why "the signs matched when I tried it" is not something to rely on.

The program also verifies Result 2 directly, projecting and measuring at several values of M. Measured against predicted, the two agree to 1.1e-16 or better every time.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Four samples, two features: (0,0), (2,1), (4,4), (6,7). Centre them, form the covariance matrix, find both eigenvalues, and report the explained-variance ratios.

Hint

Centre first and check the centred rows sum to zero before going further. The covariance divides by n - 1 = 3, not by 4.

Show the worked solution
  1. Centre. The mean is (3, 3), giving (-3,-3), (-1,-2), (1,1), (3,4), which sum to (0, 0).
  2. Sums. ∑x12 = 9+1+1+9 = 20, ∑x22 = 9+4+1+16 = 30, ∑x1x2 = 9+2+1+12 = 24.
  3. Covariance. Divide each by 3: S = [[6.6667, 8], [8, 10]].
  4. Invariants. trace = 16.6667, det = 66.667 - 64 = 2.6667.
  5. Solve. Discriminant 277.778 - 10.667 = 267.111, root 16.3436, so λ = 16.5051 and 0.1616.
  6. Ratios. 99.03 percent and 0.97 percent.

Sanity check. 16.5051 + 0.1616 = 16.6667 = trace(S) and 16.5051 × 0.1616 = 2.667 = det(S). Both invariants reproduced, so the quadratic was solved correctly. A first ratio this close to 100 percent is the signature of four nearly collinear points, which is what the data is: check by plotting them.

Your teacher. If section 4 has not fully landed, ask an agent to fit both lines to the same six points and then swap the roles of the two columns, regressing x on y instead. You get a third line. PCA gives the same answer either way, and that symmetry is the real difference between the two methods.

Primary source to go deeper

Karl Pearson stated the perpendicular-distance criterion in the paper that introduced the method, in 1901: a good fit is obtained by making the sum of the squares of the perpendiculars from the points onto the line a minimum, and he contrasts it with the vertical-residual criterion on the same page. Pearson (1901), Philosophical Magazine 2, 559-572. For the modern treatment, Mathematics for Machine Learning Sections 10.2 to 10.4, noting that it writes the data matrix with samples as columns rather than rows.
