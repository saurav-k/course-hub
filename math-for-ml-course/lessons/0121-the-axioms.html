Module 07 · Probability · Lesson 121

Three rules generate the whole subject, and the hard part is the set they talk about

foundation 10 min The axioms as Hajek states them, and the four consequences that do the work. · Hajek, ECE 313, section 1.2

The one-minute version

  • A sample space is the set of possible outcomes, and an event is a subset of it. Not an outcome, a subset.
  • There are three axioms: probabilities are non-negative, disjoint events add, and the whole space has probability 1.
  • Everything else, including the one people get wrong, is derived in a line or two.
  • The rule people get wrong is inclusion-exclusion: adding two overlapping events double-counts the overlap.
  • A softmax output satisfies all three axioms. Three sigmoid outputs do not, and reading them as a distribution is a real bug.

1. The sample space, and what an event actually is

Write down everything that could happen. That set is the sample space, written Ω, and one element of it is an outcome.

Take the route column of the module's request log. A request goes to one of three places, so Ω = {chat, embed, rerank}.

Now the definition that does the work. An event is a subset of Ω, not a single outcome. "The request went to chat" is the subset {chat}. "The request did not go to chat" is the subset {embed, rerank}. Both are events, and both get a probability. Once events are subsets, "and", "or" and "not" become intersection, union and complement, and set algebra does the rest of your thinking for you.

Two events are mutually exclusive when they share no outcome, so their intersection is empty. A collection of mutually exclusive events that together cover Ω is a partition. The three routes are one.

2. The three axioms

Hajek states them for a probability measure P defined on the events. They are short enough to memorise and they are the entire foundation.

P.1   P(A) ≥ 0
P.2   P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)   whenever A and B are mutually exclusive
P.3   P(Ω) = 1 A, B: events, that is, subsets of the sample space. ∪: union, read as "or". Ω: the sample space, the set of everything that could happen. mutually exclusive: A and B share no outcome, so they cannot both occur. P.2 also holds for a countably infinite list of mutually exclusive events, which is the version the continuous distributions later in this module need.
The reframing P.2 is the only axiom with a condition attached, and that condition is where every mistake lives. Disjoint events add. Overlapping events do not.

3. What follows, in one line each

These are not new assumptions. Each is forced by the three above.

  1. The complement rule. A and its complement are mutually exclusive and together they are Ω, so by P.2 and P.3 their probabilities sum to 1. Therefore P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  2. Probabilities are at most 1. P(A) = 1 − P(not A), and P(not A) ≥ 0 by P.1, so P(A) ≤ 1.
  3. The empty event has probability zero. It is the complement of Ω, so P(∅) = 1 − 1 = 0.
  4. Bigger events are more likely. If A sits inside B, then B splits into A and the rest of B, which are disjoint, so P(B) = P(A) + something non-negative.

4. Inclusion-exclusion, and why it needs proving

P.2 says nothing about events that overlap, and most interesting pairs do. The repair is one theorem.

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) ∩: intersection, read as "and". The subtracted term is the overlap. Adding P(A) and P(B) counts every outcome in the overlap twice, once in each, so it is removed once.

The proof

What is assumed: only the three axioms, and that A and B are events.

The shape of the argument: P.2 only applies to pieces that do not overlap. So cut the union into pieces that do not overlap, add them with P.2, then reassemble.

  1. Cut the union into three disjoint pieces. Every outcome in A ∪ B is in exactly one of: A but not B, B but not A, or both. So P(A ∪ B) = P(A not B) + P(B not A) + P(A and B).
  2. Add and subtract the overlap. Rewrite that as ( P(A not B) + P(A and B) ) + ( P(B not A) + P(A and B) ) − P(A and B). Nothing has changed; one term was added and removed.
  3. Recognise each bracket. The first bracket is P.2 applied to the two disjoint pieces that make up A, so it is P(A). The second is P.2 applied to B, so it is P(B).
  4. Read off the result. P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
The step that does the real work is the first one. Everything after it is arithmetic. The theorem needs proving at all only because P.2 is silent about overlapping events, and the minus sign is the price of that silence.

5. Worked: the axioms on a real distribution

Of the 25,000 requests in the log, 14,930 went to chat, 7,580 to embed and 2,490 to rerank.

  1. Turn counts into probabilities. 14,930 ÷ 25,000 = 0.5972, then 0.3032, then 0.0996.
  2. Check P.1. All three are positive. A count divided by a count cannot be negative.
  3. Check P.3. 0.5972 + 0.3032 + 0.0996 = 1.0000. It has to be, because each was divided by the same total and the totals add back up.
  4. Check P.2 where it applies. Chat and embed are mutually exclusive, so P(chat or embed) should be 0.5972 + 0.3032 = 0.9004. Counting the rows directly gives 0.9004.
  5. Now two events that overlap. P(verified) = 0.70076 and P(cached) = 0.24928, and 4,361 rows are both, so P(both) = 0.17444.
  6. Apply the theorem. 0.70076 + 0.24928 − 0.17444 = 0.77560. Counting the rows where either holds gives 0.77560 as well.
  7. See what the minus sign bought. Without it you get 0.95004, which is too big by 0.17444 - exactly the overlap, counted a second time.

Sanity check. P(verified or cached) must be at least P(verified) = 0.70076, because "verified or cached" contains "verified". It is 0.77560.

What changes if you add a fourth route with no traffic. The sample space grows to four outcomes, every existing probability is unchanged, and the new one is 0. That is allowed: P.1 says non-negative, not positive. An outcome with probability zero is a perfectly ordinary member of a sample space, and forgetting that is how people end up dividing by it later.

6. Where this bites: a softmax is a distribution and three sigmoids are not

A classifier's label set is a sample space, so the axioms apply to a model's output and can be checked.

A softmax passes by construction. Every entry is positive because the exponential is, which is P.1, and the entries sum to 1 because each is divided by their total, which is P.3. The labels are mutually exclusive, so P.2 holds too. A softmax vector genuinely is a probability measure over the labels.

A multi-label head is different. Three sigmoid outputs of 0.9, 0.8 and 0.7 sum to 2.4, so P.3 fails outright. They are three separate Bernoulli distributions on three separate sample spaces, one per tag.

This is the sample-space mistake in its most common form. Before you read a vector as a distribution, ask what the outcomes are and whether exactly one of them happens. If a request can carry two tags at once, the tags are not a sample space and no amount of normalising makes them one.

7. The same checks, at scale

The program checks all three axioms on the route distribution and computes the union both ways, asserting they agree on 25,000 rows.

code/0121-the-axioms.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def union_by_inclusion_exclusion(a, b):
    """P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B). The theorem, spelled out."""
    return float(a.mean() + b.mean() - (a & b).mean())


def union_by_counting(a, b):
    """P(A or B), by counting the rows where either holds. No theorem used."""
    return float((a | b).mean())

The second function never uses the theorem. That is what makes the assertion worth running: one route trusts the axioms and the other trusts the data, and they land on 0.775600 together.

An honest note about the other three axioms. Hajek states three further axioms about which subsets are allowed to be events. They matter only when Ω is uncountably infinite, where it can be impossible to assign a consistent probability to every subset. This is an engineering course and every event it meets is well behaved, so the page names them and moves on.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A multi-label head emits sigmoid scores of 0.9, 0.8 and 0.7 for three tags. Which axiom fails if you read those three as a distribution over the tags?
Axiom 3 is the one that fails: the whole sample space must have probability 1 and these total 2.4. Axiom 1 holds, since all three are positive. Axiom 2 is not even in play, because the tags are not mutually exclusive - a request can carry two, which is the whole reason the head is multi-label. The third option is the trap: each score is individually a fine Bernoulli parameter, and that is exactly why the error survives review.
P(A) is 0.30, P(B) is 0.55, and P(A and B) is 0.22. What is P(A or B)?
Inclusion-exclusion gives 0.30 + 0.55 - 0.22 = 0.63. The second option is the mistake this page exists to prevent: it counts the overlap twice, and 0.85 is too big by exactly 0.22. The third is P(A and B), which answers a different question. The fourth is arithmetic on the two numbers with no probabilistic meaning at all.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

In the request log, 17,519 requests come from a verified user and 6,232 are served from cache. 4,361 requests are both.

Find P(verified or cached) and P(neither). Then name the rule you used for the second one.

Hint

The first is inclusion-exclusion. The second needs no new counting at all - "neither" is the complement of "either".

Show the worked solution
  1. Turn the counts into probabilities. 17,519 ÷ 25,000 = 0.70076, 6,232 ÷ 25,000 = 0.24928, 4,361 ÷ 25,000 = 0.17444.
  2. Apply inclusion-exclusion. 0.70076 + 0.24928 − 0.17444 = 0.77560.
  3. Take the complement. 1 − 0.77560 = 0.22440.
  4. Name the rule. The complement rule, which comes from axioms 2 and 3 together: an event and its complement are disjoint and cover Ω.

Sanity check. Both answers sit between 0 and 1, and the first must be at least 0.70076 because "verified or cached" contains "verified". As a second check, the four disjoint cells 0.17444, 0.52632, 0.07484 and 0.22440 sum to 1.00000.

Your teacher. The habit worth taking from this page is asking "what is Ω here?" before anything else. Most confusion in probability is not about arithmetic, it is about two people silently using different sample spaces. Ask an agent to describe the sample space for a coin flipped three times, then for the number of heads in three flips, and notice that those are different sets with different sizes.
