frontier 9 min Part 1 of 3. The capstone teaches nothing new: it walks one dataset through every block and links out at each step.

The one-minute version

  • One file, sessions.csv, 20,000 rows and four columns, has been sitting underneath this course the whole way.
  • Module 2 already told you its mean is 1.74 times its median, so the average session is not a typical session.
  • Module 10 already told you one column correlates with spend at 0.487 and another at 0.002. A real signal and a null one, in one table.
  • What none of them answered is the question a business actually asks: how much is one more second worth, and how sure are we? The next two pages answer it.

1. The table

The file is sessions.csv, and it holds one row per visit to a shop. Seven columns, of which four carry the argument; the diagram leaves out session_id, device and returning, which this module never uses.

2. What Module 2 already said about it

Module 2 used session_seconds to make its point about centre and shape. The two summaries disagree, and they disagree in a direction that matters.

mean / median = 171.7 / 98.9 = 1.74 Read as: the arithmetic mean of session_seconds divided by its median. A ratio of 1 would mean a symmetric column. A ratio above 1 means a right tail, which is to say a few very large values and no matching very small ones, because the column cannot go below zero.

The quartiles say the same thing in a second way. The middle half of all sessions runs from 48.6 to 200.8 seconds, so the mean of 171.7 does not even sit in the middle of the middle half. Module 2 called this shape lognormal and gave the reason; nothing here re-argues it.

3. What Module 10 already said about it

Module 10 used the same file for correlation, and it picked the columns deliberately. Two of them look equally plausible as predictors of spend, and only one of them is.

Keeping a column that carries nothing is not free, and Part 3 shows exactly what the machinery does when it is handed one.

4. The question the next two pages answer

A correlation of 0.487 says that longer sessions and larger spend move together. It does not say by how much, in the units a person can act on. The question the shop actually has is this one:

The capstone question If a change to the product keeps a visitor for one more minute, how much more do they spend, and how confident should anyone be in that number before spending money on the change?

The first half of that question is Part 2, which fits the number three separate ways and checks that the ways agree. The second half is Part 3, which puts an interval on it and decides whether it is real. Both use machinery you already have; neither adds any.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

The mean session is 171.7 seconds and the median is 98.9 seconds. What does that gap establish?
A gap between the two is evidence about shape, nothing more. The mean is computed correctly and is the right answer to a different question: total revenue divides by the mean, not the median, so a capacity forecast wants it. Nothing here marks any row as an error, and the 884 sessions past 600 seconds are real visits. And the median is not universally correct either; it is correct when you want a typical case.
Screen brightness correlates with spend at 0.002 across 20,000 rows. What follows?
Correlation measures linear association only, so a curved or conditional relationship would sit at nearly zero too, which is why "no straight-line association" is all that is licensed. Proof of independence is a much stronger claim and this number cannot carry it. Nothing needs removing before other columns can be fitted. And 20,000 rows is not too small: Part 3 shows this column produces a confidence interval so narrow that a real effect of any useful size would have been visible.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Here are six rows lifted from sessions.csv, with session_seconds rounded to whole seconds:

73, 93, 115, 203, 663, 688 Read as: the session lengths in seconds of the first six rows of the file, sorted ascending. There are six values, so n is even.

Find the mean and the median, then the ratio of one to the other, and say which of the two a pricing team should quote when it describes a typical visit.

Hint

The list is already sorted, which is the step people forget. With an even count the median is the average of the two middle values, so find positions three and four before doing any arithmetic.

Show the worked solution
  1. Add the six values. 73 + 93 = 166, then 166 + 115 = 281, then 281 + 203 = 484, then 484 + 663 = 1,147, and finally 1,147 + 688 = 1,835.
  2. Divide by the count. 1,835 ÷ 6 = 305.8 seconds, which is the mean.
  3. Take the two middle values. Six values sorted, so positions three and four are 115 and 203.
  4. Average them. (115 + 203) ÷ 2 = 318 ÷ 2 = 159.0 seconds, which is the median.
  5. Take the ratio. 305.8 ÷ 159.0 = 1.92, so these six rows lean right even harder than the full column's 1.74.
  6. Answer the question that was asked. A typical visit is what a person would experience, so quote the median, 159.0 seconds. The mean is dragged up by the two sessions past 600 seconds.

Sanity check. The median must land between the third and fourth sorted values, and 159.0 sits between 115 and 203, so it does. The ratio must exceed 1 whenever the largest values sit further from the middle than the smallest, and here 688 is 529 above the median while 73 is only 86 below it.

Your teacher. Open the dataset yourself before reading Part 2. Load it, compute the mean, the median and the two correlations, and confirm the four numbers on this page. A capstone is worth very little if the reader takes its starting numbers on trust, and this is the last chance to catch a misunderstanding cheaply.
