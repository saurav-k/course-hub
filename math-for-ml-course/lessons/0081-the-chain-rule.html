The chain rule is the only differentiation rule that matters at scale

foundation 12 min Every model in this hub is a composition, and this is how you differentiate one.

The one-minute version

  • The sum, product and quotient rules are fluency. The chain rule is the idea, and this page spends its budget accordingly.
  • A composition's derivative is a product of local derivatives multiplied along a path. A 200-layer network is a 200-link chain and nothing more.
  • The familiar proof has a hole in it, and there is a short proof that does not.
  • Chained through a sigmoid and a cross-entropy, everything cancels and the answer is the prediction error: dL/dz = σ(z) − y.

1. The other rules, briefly, because they are fluency

Three rules cover almost everything you will need to differentiate by hand. They are worth knowing cold and they are not worth a page each.

(f + g)′ = f′ + g′     (fg)′ = f′g + fg′     (f / g)′ = (f′g − fg′) ÷ g² f and g: two functions of the same variable. f′: the derivative of f, from Lesson 80. The sum rule says derivatives add. The product rule says you differentiate one factor at a time and add the results. The quotient rule is the product rule applied to f times one over g, tidied up.

That is the whole of it. The rest of this page is one more rule, and it is the one that makes deep learning possible.

2. Multiplication along a path

Suppose x feeds u, and u feeds y. If u moves three times as fast as x, and y moves twice as fast as u, how fast does y move when x does? Six times. You did not need calculus for that, and the chain rule says the same thing in general.

The reframingEvery layer, every activation and every loss is one link. A network's gradient is not a hard derivative, it is an easy derivative repeated a great many times.

3. The theorem, and the hole in the usual proof

Theorem (the chain rule). If g is differentiable at a, and f is differentiable at g(a), then the composition is differentiable at a and (f ∘ g)′(a) = f′(g(a)) g′(a).

What is assumed: differentiability of each piece, each at the right point. The shape of the argument: restate "differentiable" as "equal to a straight line times a factor that is continuous here", substitute one into the other, and read off the factor. The step that does the real work is the restatement, because it removes every division from the argument.

f(y) − f(b) = φ(y) (y − b), with φ continuous at b and φ(b) = f′(b) This is an equivalent way to say f is differentiable at b. Reading left to right: the change in f equals some factor φ multiplied by the change in the input, and that factor settles on the derivative as y approaches b. Nothing is divided by anything, which is the point.
  1. Write both pieces this way. With b = g(a): f(y) − f(b) = φ(y)(y − b), and g(x) − g(a) = ψ(x)(x − a), with φ continuous at b and ψ continuous at a.
  2. Substitute y = g(x). f(g(x)) − f(g(a)) = φ(g(x)) (g(x) − g(a)) = φ(g(x)) ψ(x) (x − a).
  3. Look at the factor. φ(g(x))ψ(x) is a product of compositions of continuous functions, so it is continuous at a.
  4. Evaluate it there. φ(b) ψ(a) = f′(g(a)) g′(a).
  5. Read the definition backwards. The composition has exactly the form in the box above, with that factor, so it is differentiable at a with that derivative. QED
Why not the proof you were taughtThe familiar argument multiplies and divides by g(x) − g(a) and takes limits. That divides by something which can be zero in every neighbourhood of a, even for a perfectly well behaved g: take g(x) = x²sin(1/x), which is differentiable everywhere and equals zero infinitely often near the origin. The argument above never divides, so it has no gap to patch.

4. The derivative of the sigmoid, by the rules above

σ(z) = (1 + e−z)−1  →  σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) z: the logit. σ(z): the probability it maps to. The remarkable part is the right-hand side: the derivative is expressible using only the function's own value, so once you have computed σ(z) the slope costs one multiplication.
  1. Differentiate the outside. The derivative of u−1 is −u−2, with u = 1 + e−z.
  2. Differentiate the inside. The derivative of 1 + e−z is −e−z.
  3. Multiply, by the chain rule. σ′(z) = −(1 + e−z)−2 × (−e−z) = e−z ÷ (1 + e−z)².
  4. Split the fraction. That is [1 ÷ (1 + e−z)] × [e−z ÷ (1 + e−z)], and the second bracket is 1 − σ(z).

5. Worked: the gradient a classifier actually uses

Now chain the sigmoid into the loss. Binary cross-entropy on one example, as a function of the logit:

L = −[ y log σ(z) + (1 − y) log(1 − σ(z)) ] L: the loss for one example, a single number to be made small. y: the true label, either 0 or 1. z: the model's logit for this example. log: the natural logarithm. Only one of the two bracketed terms survives for any given label, because the other is multiplied by zero.
  1. Differentiate the loss in σ. dL/dσ = −y/σ + (1 − y)/(1 − σ).
  2. Bring in the link from step 4. dσ/dz = σ(1 − σ).
  3. Multiply. dL/dz = [−y/σ + (1 − y)/(1 − σ)] × σ(1 − σ).
  4. Distribute, and watch the denominators go. The first term becomes −y(1 − σ) and the second (1 − y)σ.
  5. Collect. −y + yσ + σ − yσ = σ(z) − y.
  6. Evaluate on one row. A machine with logit z = 2.0 that did fail, so y = 1: dL/dz = 0.880797 − 1 = −0.119203.

Sanity check. The label is 1 and the prediction is below 1, so raising the logit must reduce the loss, so the derivative must be negative. It is. And it must lie in [−1, 1], because it is a difference of two numbers each in [0, 1].

What changes if the label were 0 instead? Then dL/dz = 0.880797, positive and more than seven times larger. The model was confidently wrong, and the gradient says so without anyone adding a special case.

In words. An example the model already gets right contributes almost nothing to the gradient. An example it gets confidently wrong contributes almost a full unit. Training spends its effort where it is wrong, automatically, because the algebra cancelled that way.

6. The trade-off: writing this out by hand does not scale

Two links cancelled beautifully. Try four. The derivative of a modestly nested expression like the square root of x² + ex², plus the cosine of the same thing, runs to two lines of algebra with the same subexpression appearing four times. A network has millions of links and no cancellation waiting at the end.

That is the debt Lesson 86 pays: keep the chain rule, and stop writing the expression down.

    """What the chain rule gives, after the sigmoid and the log cancel."""

Answer from memory before revealing.

What is the largest value the logistic sigmoid's derivative can take?
The derivative is the product of two numbers that sum to 1, so it is largest when both are 0.5, giving 0.25. The second option is the value of the sigmoid itself at zero, not its slope, which is the commonest confusion here. The third is the value the sigmoid approaches for large logits, again the function and not the slope. The fourth describes the exponential, which the sigmoid is built from but does not inherit.
Frameworks fuse the sigmoid and the cross-entropy into one operation. What is the calculus reason?
The chain rule reduces the whole composition to sigma(z) - y, so computing sigma, then the log, then differentiating back through both, computes an intermediate the answer never needed. The first option is a property of the loss and not a calculus reason. The second is false: the gradient vanishes exactly when sigma(z) equals y, which is what you want. The third is a real side effect of fusion and answers a different question, which is what makes it the strongest distractor.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Derive the cross-entropy gradient from scratch, then read one consequence off it.

L(z) = −[ y log σ(z) + (1 − y) log(1 − σ(z)) ] Read as: the loss for one example, written as a function of the logit z alone, with the label y fixed at 0 or 1 for that example.

Find dL/dz, evaluate it at y = 1 and z = 2.0, and then say what the derivative is when the model is exactly right.

Hint

Two links: z feeds σ, and σ feeds L. Differentiate each link on its own, then multiply. Do not substitute numbers until the algebra has finished.

Hint for the cancellation

After multiplying you will have σ(1 − σ) sitting over both a σ and a (1 − σ). Distribute rather than trying to simplify the bracket first.

Show the worked solution
  1. The outer link. dL/dσ = −y/σ + (1 − y)/(1 − σ).
  2. The inner link. dσ/dz = σ(1 − σ).
  3. Multiply and distribute. −y(1 − σ) + (1 − y)σ.
  4. Collect. −y + yσ + σ − yσ = σ(z) − y.
  5. Evaluate. σ(2.0) = 0.880797, so at y = 1 the derivative is 0.880797 − 1 = −0.119203.
  6. The consequence. The derivative is zero exactly when σ(z) = y. For a hard label of 0 or 1 that only happens in the limit as the logit runs off to infinity, so a correctly classified example never stops contributing entirely, it only contributes less and less.

Sanity check. The answer must lie in [−1, 1] for every logit and every label, because it is a difference of two numbers in [0, 1]. If your expression can exceed 1, a denominator survived the cancellation that should not have.

Your teacher. If the cancellation went past you, ask an agent to redo step 3 with y fixed at 1 first and then at 0, so only one term is alive each time. If the Caratheodory proof is the part that did not land, ask it to plot x²sin(1/x) near the origin and count how often it crosses zero.
