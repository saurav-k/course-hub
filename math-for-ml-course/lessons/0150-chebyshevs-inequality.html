Spend one more number and the promise improves fiftyfold

working 11 min It is not a new idea. It is the previous page's inequality applied to a squared deviation.

The one-minute version

  • P(|X - μ| ≥ kσ) is at most 1/k2, for every distribution with a finite variance.
  • The proof is Markov applied to (X - μ)2. Squaring turns a two-sided question into a one-sided one about a non-negative quantity.
  • It is loose on any particular distribution and tight across all of them, because a three-point case attains it.
  • The one-sided version is 1/(1 + k2). Halving the two-sided bound is not valid and can be violated.

1. The mental model: measure the distance in standard deviations

Markov could only ask how far out you were in absolute units. Once you know a standard deviation, you can ask a better question: how far out are you relative to how much this thing normally moves. That is what k standard deviations means, and the answer no longer depends on the units.

P(|X - μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1 ÷ k2 Read as: the probability that X lands at least k standard deviations away from its own mean, on either side, is at most one over k squared. The vertical bars mean size regardless of sign. Nothing here names a distribution, which is the whole point.

At two standard deviations that promises at most a quarter, at three at most a ninth, at ten at most a hundredth. It falls, but only polynomially, and that is the price of a promise that has to hold for every distribution at once.

2. The proof: it is Markov in a costume

Theorem For any random variable X with mean μ and finite variance σ2, and any k > 0, P(|X - μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k2.

Proof. The shape of the argument is a change of variable. Markov needs a non-negative quantity and a one-sided threshold, and the event here is two-sided and about a quantity that can be negative. Squaring fixes both at once.

That squaring is the step doing the real work. The event |X - μ| ≥ kσ happens exactly when (X - μ)2 ≥ (kσ)2, because squaring is order-preserving on non-negative numbers. The two events are the same event, written twice.

P((X - μ)2 ≥ (kσ)2) ≤ E[(X - μ)2] ÷ (kσ)2 Read as: apply Markov's inequality to the squared deviation, which is non-negative as Markov requires, with the threshold (k sigma) squared. The right-hand side is the mean of that squared deviation divided by the threshold.

Now recognise the numerator. E[(X - μ)2] is the definition of the variance, which is σ2. The σ2 cancels against the one in the denominator and only 1/k2 is left.

= σ2 ÷ (k2σ2) = 1 ÷ k2 Read as: the variance in the numerator and the variance inside the squared threshold cancel exactly, which is why the final answer carries no units and no distribution.

Nothing was added beyond Markov and a definition. That is worth holding on to, because it means every criticism of Markov's tightness applies here too, in a milder form.

3. Loose here, tight everywhere

Chebyshev cannot be improved. For any k there is a distribution that attains it exactly: put mass 1/(2k2) at each of ±k and the rest at 0. That variable has mean 0, standard deviation 1, and a tail of exactly 1/k2.

Worth carrying A universal bound is calibrated to its worst case, never to yours. Measuring how loose it is on your data tells you how far your data sits from the adversarial shape, which is useful information about the data rather than a complaint about the theorem.

4. The one-sided form, and the halving that is not allowed

The two-sided bound covers both tails, so halving it for one tail looks obvious. It is wrong, because the mass need not be symmetric: a distribution may put everything on one side. The correct one-sided bound is Cantelli's.

P(X - μ ≥ kσ) ≤ 1 ÷ (1 + k2) Read as: the probability of exceeding the mean by at least k standard deviations, on the upper side only, is at most one over one plus k squared. Compare the denominator with the two-sided bound's: it is larger by exactly one, so the bound is smaller, but not by the factor of two people expect.

5. Worked example: the same queue, one number richer

  1. What you now know. Queue depth averages 20 with a standard deviation of 6. Still no distribution.
  2. Convert the threshold to standard deviations. Reaching 100 means a deviation of 100 - 20 = 80.
  3. Divide by the spread. 80 ÷ 6 = 13.33 standard deviations.
  4. Apply the two-sided bound. 1 ÷ 13.332 = 1 ÷ 177.8 = 0.0056.
  5. Compare with Markov. The mean alone gave 20 ÷ 100 = 0.20, so this is 36 times better.
  6. Notice what you were also given for free. The two-sided bound covers the lower tail too, so it is answering a harder question than Markov did and still winning.
  7. Tighten to one side. Cantelli gives 1 ÷ (1 + 177.8) = 0.0056 as well, to three figures, because at large k the extra 1 barely matters.
  8. What changes if the spread doubles. At σ = 12 the threshold is 6.67 standard deviations out, and the bound loosens to 1/44.4 = 0.0225, four times worse. Bounds are quadratically sensitive to the spread, which is why reducing variance is worth more than it looks.
code/0150-chebyshevs-inequality.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
x = frame["latency_ms"].to_numpy(float)
mu, sd, k = x.mean(), x.std(), 3.0

# The proof, as arithmetic: Markov on the squared deviation gives the same number.
markov_on_squared = ((x - mu) ** 2).mean() / (k * sd) ** 2
assert np.isclose(markov_on_squared, 1 / k ** 2)

print("bound", 1 / k ** 2, "true tail", (np.abs(x - mu) >= k * sd).mean())

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does Chebyshev assume about the distribution?
A finite variance is the whole assumption, and that universality is exactly why the bound is loose on any particular shape. The other three options each name a property the bound does not need, and believing any of them would make you think Chebyshev fails on skewed or bimodal data, when those are precisely the cases it was built to survive.
Can you halve the two-sided bound to get a one-sided one?
A variable that is 1 with probability 0.2 sits two standard deviations above its mean exactly when it is 1, so the true one-sided tail is 0.200 while halving promises 0.125. The fourth option is the dangerous one: halving is not conservative, it is smaller than the truth, which is the wrong direction for a guarantee. Cantelli's 1/(1+k squared) is the valid form.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An inference server's per-request cost averages 0.4 cents with a standard deviation of 0.15 cents. Finance wants a defensible statement about how often a request could cost more than 1 cent, and the cost distribution is unknown.

Find the two-sided bound, then the correct one-sided bound, and say which one you would put in the document and why.

Hint

Express the threshold as a number of standard deviations first. Then notice that finance asked about one direction only, so the two-sided bound is answering a harder question than the one you were asked.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the deviation. 1 - 0.4 = 0.6 cents above the mean.
  2. Convert to standard deviations. 0.6 ÷ 0.15 = 4, so the threshold is 4 standard deviations out.
  3. Two-sided bound. 1 ÷ 42 = 1 ÷ 16 = 0.0625.
  4. What that actually promises. At most 6.25 percent of requests deviate by 0.6 cents in either direction, which includes costing less than a negative amount and is partly wasted.
  5. One-sided bound. Cantelli gives 1 ÷ (1 + 16) = 1 ÷ 17 = 0.0588.
  6. Compare. 5.88 percent against 6.25 percent, a real improvement but nothing like the halving to 3.13 percent that the tempting shortcut would claim.
  7. Which to publish. The one-sided 5.88 percent, because it is both valid and the answer to the question asked. Publishing the halved 3.13 percent would be stating a guarantee that no theorem supports.
  8. What changes if you can assume a normal. Four standard deviations out on a normal is about 0.003 percent, roughly two thousand times smaller. That gap is the value of knowing the shape, and it is also the risk you take on if the shape is wrong.

Sanity check. The one-sided bound must be smaller than the two-sided one and larger than half of it, so it must land between 0.0313 and 0.0625. If yours falls outside that window, the arithmetic slipped, and if it equals exactly half you used the shortcut this page warns against.

Your teacher. Next time you are asked for a guarantee about a metric whose distribution nobody has plotted, compute the Chebyshev bound before reaching for a percentile. If the bound is good enough for the decision, you can make the promise without assuming anything, which is a much stronger position than a percentile from last month's data.
