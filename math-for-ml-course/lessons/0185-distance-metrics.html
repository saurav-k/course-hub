Module 10 · Information, similarity, and dimension · Lesson 185

Similar is a choice you make, not a fact you measure

working 12 min Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (2006), equations 2.44 and 2.50.

The one-minute version

  • Euclidean asks how far apart, cosine asks same direction, Mahalanobis asks how far in units of the data's own spread.
  • Scale decides between the first two. Correlation decides whether you need the third.
  • A metric has three properties. Euclidean and Mahalanobis have them; cosine distance and KL divergence do not.
  • Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance, measured after dividing each principal direction by its standard deviation.
  • On real data the three metrics pick different rows as the most extreme. That is not a rounding difference.

1. The three axioms, and who fails them

Module 03 gave you the axioms a norm must satisfy, which are properties of one vector. A metric is a different object: a function of two points, and it has its own three requirements.

d(x, y) = 0  iff  x = y  ·  d(x, y) = d(y, x)  ·  d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) Read as, in order: the distance between two points is zero exactly when they are the same point, and never otherwise; the distance from x to y equals the distance from y to x; and going directly from x to z is never longer than going via any third point y. The third is the triangle inequality and it is the one that fails most often in practice.

These are not decoration. The triangle inequality is what lets a search index prune: if y is far from x and z is close to y, you can rule z out without measuring it. Break the inequality and the pruning is unsound, so the index silently returns wrong neighbours.

Cosine distance failing is not a theoretical worry. Take a = (1, 0), b = (1, 1) and c = (0, 1). Then cos(a,b) = cos(b,c) = 0.7071 and cos(a,c) = 0, so the two short hops cost 0.2929 each, totalling 0.5858, while the direct route costs 1. Going via b is cheaper than going straight there. The program finds the same failure on real rows of the committed table, with a slack of −0.0498.

And the repair. For two vectors of length one, |x̂ − ŷ|2 = 2 − 2cos, so √(2 − 2cos) is literally the Euclidean distance between the normalised vectors and inherits all three axioms. The step that does the real work is the square root: 1 − cos is the square of a distance, and squaring a metric generally destroys the triangle inequality.

2. Euclidean, and the units problem

Euclidean distance adds up squared differences. That is only meaningful if a unit of one column is comparable to a unit of another, and in a real table it never is.

Two customers described by annual income in rupees and age in years. A is (1,200,000, 30), B is (1,200,050, 65), C is (1,900,000, 31). The distance from A to B is √(502 + 352) = 61.0, and from A to C it is √(700,0002 + 12) = 700,000.0. B wins by four orders of magnitude, and a 35-year age gap contributed 35 out of 700,000.

Standardise, dividing each column by its own standard deviation, and the picture inverts. With standard deviations of 400,000 rupees and 12 years, A becomes (3.0, 2.5), B becomes (3.000125, 5.4167), and C becomes (4.75, 2.5833). Now d(A, B) = 2.9167 and d(A, C) = 1.7520. C is nearer and the ranking has flipped.

Standardising is not a neutral act, and it is worth being clear about what you just asserted: that one standard deviation of income and one standard deviation of age are equally important. That is a modelling claim, not a fact about the data. It is usually a much better claim than "one rupee equals one year", which is what raw Euclidean distance assumes.

3. Mahalanobis, built rather than quoted

Standardising fixes scale. It does not fix redundancy. If two columns are strongly correlated they are largely the same measurement twice, and Euclidean distance counts it twice.

Δ2 = (x − μ)T S−1 (x − μ) Read as: the squared Mahalanobis distance from a point x to the centre μ is the difference vector, transposed, times the inverse of the covariance matrix S, times the difference vector again. When S is the identity, meaning unit variances and no correlations, this collapses to the ordinary squared Euclidean distance.

That formula is opaque until you look at it in the right coordinates. Module 04's spectral theorem says a covariance matrix has an orthonormal set of eigenvectors with positive eigenvalues. Use them as axes.

  1. Decompose. S = ∑i λi uiuiT, with the ui orthonormal.
  2. Invert it. S−1 = ∑i (1/λi) uiuiT. Multiply that candidate by S and every cross term dies, because uiTuj = 0 for i ≠ j, leaving the identity.
  3. Substitute and expand. Δ2 = ∑i (1/λi) (x − μ)Tui · uiT(x − μ). This is the step that does the real work: each of those two factors is a plain number, the projection of the point onto one eigenvector, so the matrix expression becomes a sum of scalars.
  4. Name the projections. Write yi = uiT(x − μ). Then Δ2 = ∑i yi2 / λi.

Read that last line aloud: it is a sum of squares, exactly like Euclidean distance, with each term divided by the variance along that direction. Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance measured in standard deviations along the data's own axes. The requirement that every λi is positive is where positive definiteness earns its keep: a zero eigenvalue is a direction with no spread, the matrix is singular, and the distance is undefined.

Where you have met it already: this quadratic form is the exponent of the multivariate Gaussian, so a contour of constant density is a contour of constant Mahalanobis distance. The ellipses above are the Gaussian's own level sets.

Mahalanobis needs S−1, which needs enough rows to estimate the covariance and a matrix that is not near-singular. In 768 dimensions with 500 samples the estimate is singular and the distance does not exist. Shrinkage estimators are the standard fix; Module 04 covers what conditioning means and why a near-singular inverse amplifies error.

4. Worked: the three disagree on real data

  1. Take three columns of the committed 12,000-row table: tenure in months, monthly spend, and sessions per week.
  2. Read the scales. Standard deviations of 8.796, 208.912 and 3.513. Spend is twenty-four times the spread of tenure, in raw units.
  3. Read the correlations. Tenure and spend correlate at 0.8458. Spend and sessions correlate at 0.0079, which is nothing.
  4. Rank every row three ways by distance from the centre: raw Euclidean, standardised Euclidean, and Mahalanobis.
  5. Compare the top hundreds. Raw and standardised share 54 rows of 100. Raw and Mahalanobis share 30 of 100. Standardised and Mahalanobis share 54.
  6. Sanity check. Raw Euclidean should be dominated by spend, and it is: its single most extreme row is the one with the highest spend, 1522.33, even though that row is unremarkable on the other two columns. The other two metrics both pick a different row, which is extreme on two columns at once.
  7. What changes if you measure spend in thousands rather than units? Raw Euclidean rankings change completely. Standardised and Mahalanobis rankings do not move at all, because both divide by a spread measured in the same units. That invariance is the real argument for them, not the arithmetic.
  8. Read it back. Seventy of the hundred rows an outlier detector flags depend on which metric you picked. Nobody in the review will ask, and the choice is buried in one line of setup.

The program also tests all three axioms on 20,000 random triples of real rows. Euclidean and √(2 − 2cos) hold every time. Cosine distance breaks the triangle inequality, and the program prints the three rows that broke it rather than asserting that some triple must exist.

5. Which one to use

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A query is (1,1,0). Document d1 is (10,10,0) and d2 is (1,1,1). Which document is nearer?
Cosine gives d1 a perfect 1.0000 and d2 only 0.8165; Euclidean puts d2 at 1.0000 away and d1 at 12.7279. The two standard metrics rank them in opposite orders, so the question has no answer until you say which one you meant. The first and third options are each true under one metric and stated as though it were the only one, which is exactly the mistake. The fourth is simply false under both.
Cosine distance, one minus the cosine, is not a metric. Which property does it fail?
It satisfies the first three and fails the fourth, as the worked triple in section 1 shows. The first is wrong because the cosine of two vectors does not depend on their order. The second confuses the cosine, which does range down to minus one, with one minus the cosine, which therefore ranges up to two and is never negative. The third is the interesting near-miss: two different vectors pointing the same way do score zero, so cosine distance is only a metric on directions, not on vectors, and that is a separate objection from the one asked about.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

The covariance matrix of the three columns in section 4 has eigenvalues 12.338, 21.991 and 43,699.414.

Find the Mahalanobis distance of a point lying purely along the largest eigendirection at Euclidean distance 100 from the centre, and of a point lying purely along the smallest, also at Euclidean distance 100. Then say which point each of the two detectors would flag, and which is right.

Hint

Along a single eigendirection the sum in section 3 step 4 has only one term, so the formula collapses to the projection divided by the square root of that eigenvalue.

Show the worked solution
  1. Collapse the sum. Along one eigendirection, Δ = |y| / √λ.
  2. Largest direction. √43,699.414 = 209.045, so Δ = 100 / 209.045 = 0.478.
  3. Smallest direction. √12.338 = 3.513, so Δ = 100 / 3.513 = 28.47.
  4. The ratio. 28.47 / 0.478 = 59.5, which is √(43,699.414 / 12.338). Two points at the same Euclidean distance, sixty times apart in Mahalanobis distance.
  5. What each detector flags. A Mahalanobis detector flags the second and ignores the first. A Euclidean detector cannot separate them: they are the same distance, so it flags both or neither.
  6. Which is right, and on what condition. Mahalanobis is right if the training covariance describes normal behaviour, because 28 standard deviations along a narrow direction is genuinely stranger than half a standard deviation along a wide one.
  7. And when it is not. If the covariance was estimated from data that already contained the outliers, they helped define what "normal spread" means, and the wide direction is wide because of them. Then Mahalanobis has been taught to excuse exactly the points you were looking for.

Sanity check. The two answers must differ by exactly the ratio of the square roots of the two eigenvalues, 59.5. If your ratio came out as 3,542 you divided by the eigenvalues instead of by their square roots, which is the single most common slip here: the formula in section 3 has λ under a squared quantity, so the square root is already applied when you take the final square root of Δ2.

Your teacher. If section 3 is still opaque, ask an agent to generate two hundred points from a correlated two-dimensional Gaussian, plot them, then plot the same points after multiplying by the inverse square root of the covariance matrix. The ellipse becomes a circle, and Mahalanobis distance in the first picture is plainly the ordinary distance in the second.

Primary source to go deeper

Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, section 2.3. The Mahalanobis distance and the multivariate Gaussian are one object there, which is the right way to meet it: the distance is not a tool bolted onto the distribution, it is the distribution's exponent.
