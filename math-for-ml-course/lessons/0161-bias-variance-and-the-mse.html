Unbiased is not the same as better, and one number says which

working 12 min Bias, variance, the identity that reconciles them, and why a biased estimator can win. · code/0161

The one-minute version

  • Bias is where the cloud of estimates is centred. Variance is how wide the cloud is.
  • MSE = Bias2 + Variance. An identity, not an approximation, and it is three lines to prove.
  • Neither bias nor variance alone ranks two estimators. MSE does, and it can prefer a biased one.
  • That preference is exactly what a regulariser buys, and this page measures it: shrinking a sample mean towards a fixed guess cuts MSE to 0.854 of the unbiased estimator's.
  • Consistent means it converges on the truth as n grows. Biased and consistent is the ordinary case, not an oddity.

1. Two ways to be wrong, and they are independent of each other

Run an estimator on many samples and look at the cloud of answers. Two things can be wrong with it, and fixing one does nothing for the other.

This page is about an estimator of a parameter - one fixed number you are trying to recover. There is a second, similarly-named result about the error of a predictor of an outcome, and it is a later page in this module. The two identities look alike for a real reason and they are not the same statement. Keep them apart until that page tells you how they relate.

2. The two definitions

Bias(θ-hat) = E[θ-hat] − θ     Var(θ-hat) = E[(θ-hat − E[θ-hat])2] θ (theta): the true parameter, a fixed number you do not know. θ-hat: your estimator, a random variable because it is a function of the sample. E[·]: the average across repeated samples, from Module 08. Bias compares the estimator's centre to the truth; Var compares the estimator to its own centre and never mentions the truth at all.

Read the second definition again: the truth does not appear in it, which is why an estimator can have zero variance and be useless.

3. The identity that reconciles them

Claim. MSE(θ-hat) = Bias(θ-hat)2 + Var(θ-hat), where MSE is E[(θ-hat − θ)2].

What is assumed: only that the expectation exists, and that θ is a fixed constant rather than random. Nothing about the shape of the distribution, and no approximation anywhere.

The shape: add and subtract the estimator's own mean, expand the square, and show the cross term vanishes.

  1. Insert the estimator's mean. Write θ-hat − θ as (θ-hat − E[θ-hat]) + (E[θ-hat] − θ). Nothing has changed; one quantity was added and removed.
  2. Name the two pieces. The first bracket is the deviation from its own centre. The second bracket is the bias, and it is a constant - it does not depend on which sample you drew.
  3. Square and take the expectation. E[(A + B)2] = E[A2] + 2E[AB] + E[B2]. The first term is the variance by definition, and the third is the bias squared, since B is constant.
  4. Kill the cross term. B is constant so it comes out of the expectation: 2E[AB] = 2B × E[A]. And E[A] = E[θ-hat − E[θ-hat]] = 0, because the average deviation from the average is zero.
  5. Read it off. MSE = Var + Bias2.
An honest boundary. The identity is exact and assumes nothing beyond the second moment existing. What it does not do is tell you which term to prefer: MSE encodes one particular answer, that errors cost quadratically, and a different loss gives a different trade. A page that treats MSE as the definition of "good" has smuggled that in. Relatedly, there is a floor on how small an unbiased estimator's variance can be - the Cramer-Rao bound - and naming it is as far as this course goes.
The step that does the real work is step 4, and it works for one reason: the bias is a constant while the deviation averages to zero. That is why the identity is exact rather than approximate, and why there is no covariance term to worry about. Everything else is expanding a square.

4. Four estimators of the same thing, ranked

Estimate the mean session length from samples of 12, and compare four estimators over 60,000 repeats. The truth is μ = 171.75.

Measured MSE matched Bias2 + Var in every row: 4,861.65 against 4,861.57 for the mean. The identity is exact; the gaps are simulation noise.

5. The n−1 you have been dividing by, finally explained

Lesson 22 divides the sample variance by n−1, saying one number was already spent estimating the mean. Here is that as arithmetic.

E[s2] = σ2 with n−1,    but    E[σ-hat2] = ((n−1)/n) × σ2 with n s2: the sample variance dividing by n−1. σ-hat2: the same sum of squares divided by n, which is what maximum likelihood returns in Lesson 164. The factor (n−1)/n is below 1, so the n version is biased low, and the bias vanishes as n grows.

Measured with σ2 = 9.0: at n = 3 the MLE averages 6.008 against a predicted 6.000, and at n = 100, 8.910 against 8.910. The n−1 version averages 9.012 and 9.000 - unbiased at every size.

So the maximum likelihood estimator of a variance is biased, and everyone uses it anyway. That is not a scandal, it is this page's point arriving early: at n = 100 the bias is one per cent and the choice between the two is dominated by variance, not bias. Lesson 164 derives where the n comes from, and Lesson 163 shows a case where the bias does not shrink so politely.

6. Consistency is a different promise

An estimator is consistent if it converges in probability to the truth as n grows. That is a statement about the limit, independent of bias at any finite n.

  • Biased and consistent. The MLE variance above: wrong on average at every n, and the wrongness goes to zero. The ordinary case.
  • Unbiased and inconsistent. "Use only the first observation" from section 4: centred on the truth at every n and never improving.
  • Neither. The constant 12. It has no variance and never approaches the truth.

7. Buying variance with bias, measured

Shrink the sample mean towards a fixed guess g = 10 with weight w: the estimator is (1−w) x-bar + w g. At w = 0 it is the sample mean; at w = 1 it ignores the data.

8. Worked: reading the shrinkage table

  1. Start unbiased. At w = 0 the bias is 0.083, essentially zero, the variance is 4,861.6, and the MSE is 4,861.6.
  2. Lean 10 per cent on the guess. The bias becomes −16.10, so bias squared is 259.2 - a real cost. But the variance falls to 3,937.9, and the MSE drops to 4,197.1.
  3. Lean 20 per cent. Bias squared climbs to 1,042.2 and variance falls to 3,111.5. MSE is 4,153.6, the lowest point, at 0.854 times the unbiased estimator's.
  4. Lean 30 per cent. Bias squared 2,349.0 now outruns the variance saving, and MSE rises again to 4,731.2.
  5. Lean all the way. At w = 1 the variance is exactly 0 and the MSE is 26,162.7, five times worse than doing nothing.
  6. Read the shape. Variance falls roughly linearly in the weight while bias squared grows quadratically, so there is always some shrinkage that helps, and always a point past which it hurts.

Sanity check. At w = 1 the estimator ignores the data, so its variance must be exactly 0 and its MSE must be the squared distance from the guess to the truth: (171.75 − 10)2 = 26,163. It is. If that row had shown any variance at all, the estimator was still reading the sample.

What changes if the guess is better. The guess of 10 was badly wrong, 162 away from the truth, and shrinkage still helped. Move the guess to 150 and the bias cost at every w collapses while the variance saving is unchanged, so the optimal w moves right and the gain grows. A prior that is roughly right is worth a great deal; a prior that is badly wrong is still worth a little, and that is why ridge regression's default of shrinking towards zero is a defensible starting point even when zero is not where the coefficients live.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Estimator A is unbiased with variance 4,862. Estimator B has bias −32 and variance 3,111. Which has the lower MSE?
B's MSE is 4,135 against A's 4,862, so the biased estimator wins. The first and third options are the misconception this page exists to remove, and the third is the more seductive because it states something true about B and then draws the wrong conclusion. The fourth is wrong precisely because squaring the bias puts both onto the same scale, which is what the identity is for.
An estimator uses only the first row of every sample and ignores the rest. Which description fits it?
One row is a fair draw, so the estimator is centred on the truth, but its variance is the population variance no matter how much data you collect, so it never converges. The third option gets the bias right and then assumes consistency follows, which is exactly the conflation this page separates. The first describes the MLE variance instead.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You estimate a parameter whose true value is 100. Estimator A returns values averaging 100 with a standard deviation of 30. Estimator B returns values averaging 94 with a standard deviation of 12.

Find the MSE of each and say which you would ship. Then find the largest bias B could have and still beat A.

Hint

Variance is the standard deviation squared. For the last part, set B's MSE equal to A's and solve for the bias.

Show the worked solution
  1. Estimator A. Bias 0, variance 302 = 900. MSE = 0 + 900 = 900.
  2. Estimator B. Bias 94 − 100 = −6, so bias squared is 36. Variance 122 = 144. MSE = 36 + 144 = 180.
  3. Ship B. Its MSE is a fifth of A's, and it is the biased one.
  4. Set them equal. bias2 + 144 = 900, so bias2 = 756.
  5. Take the root. √756 = 27.5. B could be off by up to 27.5 in either direction and still beat A on MSE.

Sanity check. B's allowable bias, 27.5, is close to A's whole standard deviation of 30, and that is the right order of magnitude: A's scatter is so wide that a fairly large systematic offset is cheaper than it. If your answer had come out below 6, the bias and variance were the wrong way round in the identity.

Your teacher. Whenever someone defends a method with "it is unbiased", ask what its variance is. Unbiasedness is one term of two and it is the cheaper one to achieve - the constant-12 estimator in section 4 has zero variance and the single-observation one is perfectly unbiased, and both are useless. Ask an agent for two estimators with the same MSE but opposite bias-variance splits, and decide which you would rather explain to a stakeholder.
