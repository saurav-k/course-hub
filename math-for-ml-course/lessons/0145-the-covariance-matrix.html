One matrix that is literally the shape of your data cloud

working 14 min Its eigenvectors are the directions the cloud is long in. That sentence is the whole of PCA.

The one-minute version

  • The covariance matrix holds every pairwise covariance at once: variances down the diagonal, covariances everywhere else.
  • On centred data it is one matrix product, because each entry is a dot product and a matrix product is a table of dot products.
  • It is positive semi-definite, and the one-line proof is also the intuition: the quadratic form is the variance of the projected data.
  • Its eigenvectors are the cloud's axes and its eigenvalues are the spread along them. Diagonalising it is what PCA does.

1. The mental model: a table of dot products

With d columns there are d variances and every pair has a covariance. Arranging them in a grid, with the same column order down the side and across the top, gives a d by d matrix. Entry i,j is the covariance of column i with column j.

Two things follow immediately. Entry i,i is the covariance of a column with itself, which is its variance, so the diagonal is the variances. And covariance does not care which variable you name first, so the matrix is symmetric.

2. The mechanism: one matrix product

Σ = E[(x⃗ - μ⃗)(x⃗ - μ⃗)T] Read as: take the vector of deviations from the mean, multiply it by its own transpose to get a d by d matrix rather than a single number, and average that matrix entry by entry. The capital sigma is the standard name for a covariance matrix and has nothing to do with summation here.

For a sample, write the centred data as a matrix and the whole thing is one product.

S = (XcT Xc) ÷ (n - 1) Read as: Xc is the data matrix with every column's mean subtracted, so it has one row per observation and one column per variable. Multiplying its transpose by itself produces a table whose i,j entry is the dot product of centred column i with centred column j. Dividing by n minus one turns each of those dot products into a covariance.

That single line is the previous lesson's third form, done for every pair simultaneously. A matrix product is a table of dot products, and covariance is a scaled dot product, so the covariance matrix is what you get when those two facts meet.

3. Positive semi-definiteness, and the proof that is also the intuition

Theorem For any random vector x with a covariance matrix Σ, and any fixed direction z, the quadratic form zTΣz is never negative. Σ is positive semi-definite.

Proof. The claim looks like a fact about matrices. It is a fact about variance wearing matrix notation, and the proof is to notice that.

Fix a direction z and project the data onto it: the projection of one observation is the single number zTx. That is a random variable, so it has a variance, and being a linear combination its variance is computable directly.

Var(zTx⃗) = zT Σ z Read as: the variance of the data projected onto direction z equals the quadratic form of the covariance matrix in that direction. It follows from expanding the definition of variance for the combination and collecting the pairwise covariances, which is the same expansion the previous lesson did for two variables.

That line is the step doing the real work. Everything after it is one sentence: a variance is an average of squares, so it cannot be negative, so the left side is at least zero, so the right side is too. That holds for every z, which is the definition of positive semi-definite.

Semi-definite, not definite. A direction with zero spread gives exactly zero, and that happens whenever one column is an exact linear combination of others, or whenever you have fewer observations than columns. Then Σ is singular and Σ-1 does not exist, which is why every Gaussian model on wide data needs shrinkage or a pseudo-inverse rather than the textbook formula.

4. The geometry: eigenvectors are the axes

Σ is symmetric, so the spectral theorem from Module 04 applies: it has real eigenvalues and a full set of orthogonal eigenvectors. Being positive semi-definite makes every eigenvalue non-negative, since an eigenvalue is a quadratic form in its own eigenvector's direction.

Those eigenvectors are the cloud's own axes. The set of points at a fixed Mahalanobis distance is an ellipse, its axes point along the eigenvectors, and its half-lengths are proportional to the square roots of the eigenvalues.

{ x⃗ : (x⃗ - μ⃗)T Σ-1 (x⃗ - μ⃗) = c2 } Read as: the set of all points whose scaled squared distance from the centre equals a fixed value c squared. In two dimensions that set is an ellipse, in three an ellipsoid, and in d a shape nobody can picture but which behaves identically. Half-length along eigenvector i is c times the square root of eigenvalue i.
The hand-off Finding the direction of greatest variance means maximising zTΣz over unit vectors z, and the answer is the top eigenvector of Σ. PCA is diagonalising the covariance matrix. Module 04 derives it through the SVD of the centred data matrix, which is the same computation done more stably, and this is the object it is computing with.

5. Worked example: total variance, and the trap in splitting it

  1. Read the diagonal. The five variances are 85,481.4, 6,267.0, 6.1, 395.4 and 26,719.6.
  2. Add them. The trace is 118,869.6, and that is all the variance the five columns hold between them.
  3. Take the eigenvalues. They come out as 88,595.1, 26,259.9, 3,665.0, 345.3 and 4.2.
  4. Check they sum to the trace. They total 118,869.6. Rotating to the eigenvector basis moved variance between directions and created none.
  5. Read the split. The first direction carries 88,595.1 ÷ 118,869.6 = 74.5 percent.
  6. Now standardise and redo it. On the correlation matrix, which is the covariance matrix of the standardised columns, the first component carries only 36.5 percent.
  7. Sanity check. The answer had to change, because prompt tokens has a raw variance of 85,481 against queue depth's 6.1, and the unstandardised split is mostly reporting that one column is measured in bigger units.
  8. What changes if. Had all five columns shared a unit, the two answers would agree closely and either would do. They do not, so the correlation version is the honest one here, and the choice has to be made deliberately rather than inherited from whichever function you called.
code/0145-the-covariance-matrix.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
cols = ["prompt_tokens", "output_tokens", "queue_depth", "gpu_util", "latency_ms"]
X = frame[cols].to_numpy(float)
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)

sigma = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)          # one product, every pair at once
assert np.allclose(sigma, np.cov(X, rowvar=False, ddof=1))

# The proof, as arithmetic: z^T Sigma z IS the variance of the projection.
z = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=5)
assert np.isclose(z @ sigma @ z, np.var(Xc @ z, ddof=1))

values = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(sigma))[::-1]
print("trace     ", np.trace(sigma))
print("eigen sum ", values.sum())
print("explained ", (values / values.sum()).round(4))

The full program runs that projection check over a thousand random directions, and prints the covariance and correlation splits side by side.

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why can a covariance matrix never have a negative eigenvalue?
An eigenvalue is the quadratic form in its own eigenvector's direction, and that form is the variance of the data projected there, which cannot be negative. The first option is false: symmetric matrices routinely have negative eigenvalues. The second gives a necessary but not sufficient condition, and it is the strongest distractor because it is true and sounds like a reason. The fourth confuses estimation with the property.
PCA gives 74.5 percent on the first component, but 36.5 percent after standardising. Which is right?
They answer different questions and neither is a default. Columns sharing a unit make the covariance version defensible; mixed units make it a report about measurement scale rather than about the data. The first two options each assume a default exists, and the third invents a precondition PCA does not have.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Two features have this covariance matrix:

S = [[4, 2], [2, 1]] Read as: a 2 by 2 covariance matrix. The first feature has variance 4, the second has variance 1, and the covariance between them is 2.

Find the eigenvalues, the share of variance on the first component, and then say what is unusual about this particular matrix and what it means for a model that needs its inverse.

Hint

The trace gives you the eigenvalue sum before you solve anything, and the determinant gives you their product. Two numbers whose sum and product you know are quick to find.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the trace. 4 + 1 = 5, so the two eigenvalues sum to 5.
  2. Take the determinant. (4 × 1) - (2 × 2) = 4 - 4 = 0, so their product is 0.
  3. Solve. A product of zero forces one eigenvalue to be zero, and the sum then forces the other to be 5. The eigenvalues are 5 and 0.
  4. Share on the first component. 5 ÷ 5 = 100 percent.
  5. Say what is unusual. The second eigenvalue is exactly zero, so the cloud has no spread at all in that direction. It is not an ellipse, it is a line segment.
  6. Find why. A determinant of zero means the second column is a multiple of the first: here feature one is exactly twice feature two, so the second feature carries no information the first does not.
  7. What it means for an inverse. A zero eigenvalue makes S singular, so S-1 does not exist and any formula needing it, including the multivariate Gaussian density, is undefined.
  8. What changes if. Move the covariance from 2 to 1.9 and the determinant becomes 4 - 3.61 = 0.39, the eigenvalues become about 4.92 and 0.08, and the inverse exists but is enormous. Near-singular is not safe, it is just quieter than singular.

Sanity check. The eigenvalues must sum to the trace, 5, and both must be non-negative because this is a covariance matrix. If you get a negative one, the matrix you were handed was not a covariance matrix, and if they do not sum to the trace, the arithmetic slipped.

Your teacher. Take your own feature table, compute its covariance matrix and its correlation matrix, and run the eigenvalues of both. If the two explained-variance splits disagree sharply, your columns are in mixed units and somebody downstream is about to report whichever one their library returned by default.
