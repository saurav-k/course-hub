What a matrix multiply costs, and why the order changes the bill

working 8 min Associativity promises the same answer. It promises nothing at all about the price.

The one-minute version

  • An m by p times a p by n product costs about 2mnp floating-point operations, and that count is derived, not looked up.
  • The count predicts how cost scales, not how long something takes. Doubling a dimension multiplies the work by eight.
  • Because multiplication is associative, (AB)C and A(BC) give the same matrix at different prices. On a real adapter that gap is 2,560 times.
  • At a 2,048-token sequence, the quadratic attention terms are 2.7% of a Transformer block's matmul work. The reputation is misplaced until the sequence gets long.

Counting the operations

A floating-point operation, or flop, is one addition, subtraction, multiplication or division on real numbers. Counting them gives a crude estimate of cost that is worth having because it says how the cost scales.

Start with the inner product of two vectors of length n. That is n multiplications and n − 1 additions, so 2n − 1 flops, which Boyd and Vandenberghe simplify to 2n. Dropping a constant is fair here: no flop count predicts a running time to better than a factor of ten.

Everything else follows by counting how many inner products there are.

Ax: about 2mn flops  ·  AB: about 2mnp flops Read as: for Ax, A is m by n, so the answer has m entries and each is an inner product of length n, costing about 2n. For AB with A of shape m by p and B of shape p by n, the answer has mn entries and each is an inner product of length p. The order of the computation is mnp, the product of all three dimensions.

Two adjustments matter in practice. If a matrix is sparse, only the non-zero entries cost anything, so Ax costs between one and two flops per non-zero. And a symmetric product like XTX only needs half its entries computed, because the other half is a mirror, which saves about half the work without changing the order.

At small sizes the measurement does not match the count, and that is the count being honest rather than wrong. Going from 128 to 256 multiplied the flops by 8 and the measured time by only 1.4, because at that size the work is dominated by setting the computation up. Flop counts describe the arithmetic, not the machine.

Associativity has a price tag

Lesson 05 established that (AB)C and A(BC) are the same matrix. Their costs are not the same number.

With A of shape m by n, B of shape n by p and C of shape p by q, the first grouping costs about 2mnp + 2mpq flops and the second about 2npq + 2mnq. Those are different expressions in the same four numbers, and which is smaller depends entirely on the shapes.

The case where this matters most in current practice is a low-rank adapter. A frozen weight is corrected by a product BA with B of shape d by r and A of shape r by d, where r is tiny. For one token x the two groupings are these.

Where a Transformer block's work actually goes

Vaswani and colleagues give the per-layer complexity of self-attention as order n2·d, where n is the sequence length and d the model width. That quadratic term is famous, and it is easy to conclude from its fame that attention is where the time goes. At ordinary sequence lengths it is not.

Working it out for GPT-3's shapes, a model width of 12,288 with a feed-forward layer four times that and a 2,048-token sequence, counting only matrix products at 2mnp each, in the forward pass, with dense attention:

These percentages are derived here, not measured, and the assumptions do real work: forward pass only, dense attention, two flops per multiply-accumulate, and no count for softmax, normalisation, activations or biases, none of which are matrix products. GPT-3 itself alternates dense and locally banded sparse attention, so its true attention share is lower than 2.7 per cent, not higher.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. n is the sequence length, d the model width, dff = 4d the feed-forward width. Every count uses 2mnp for an m by p times p by n product.
  2. Fix the numbers. n = 2048, d = 12288, so dff = 49152.
  3. Count the projections. Each is (n by d) times (d by d), so 2nd2, and there are four of them: 4 × 2 × 2048 × 122882 = 2.474 Tflop.
  4. Count the attention. The scores are (n by d) times (d by n), so 2n2d, and applying them to the values costs the same again: 2 × 2 × 20482 × 12288 = 0.206 Tflop.
  5. Count the feed-forward. Up is 2nddff and down is the same, giving 4.949 Tflop.
  6. Add and divide. The total is 7.628 Tflop, and 0.206 / 7.628 = 2.7%.
  7. Find the crossover. The attention terms total 4n2d and the rest totals 24nd2. Setting them equal gives 4n2d = 24nd2, so n = 6d = 73,728 tokens.
  8. Say what would change. At a 128,000-token context the attention terms are the majority, which is exactly why long-context models are where attention optimisations pay and short-context ones are not.
code/0065-what-a-matmul-costs.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def flops_matmul(m: int, p: int, n: int) -> int:
    """About 2mnp: mn output entries, each an inner product of length p."""
    return 2 * m * n * p

d, n = 12_288, 2_048
attention = 2 * flops_matmul(n, d, n)
rest = 4 * flops_matmul(n, d, d) + 2 * flops_matmul(n, d, 4 * d)
print(f"{100 * attention / (attention + rest):.1f}%")     # 2.7%

d_lora, r = 4096, 4
print(flops_matmul(d_lora, r, d_lora) + flops_matmul(1, d_lora, d_lora))  # 167,772,160
print(flops_matmul(1, d_lora, r) + flops_matmul(1, r, d_lora))            #      65,536

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Multiplying an m by p matrix by a p by n matrix costs about how many flops?
There are mn entries in the answer and each is an inner product of length p costing about 2p, so the total is about 2mnp. The first is linear in the sizes and describes no matrix operation at all. The third over-counts by a whole factor of p. The fourth is the shape of an addition cost, which really is linear, but this is a product.
An adapter can compute x(BA) or (xB)A. What differs between them?
Associativity guarantees an identical answer, and the flop counts differ by 2,560 times at these shapes because the first route builds a full square matrix on the way. The first inverts the truth exactly. The second contradicts associativity, which is a theorem and not a convention. The third is the belief this page exists to remove.
Practice

A block has d = 12,288, dff = 4d, and a sequence of n = 2,048 tokens. Count matrix products only, at 2mnp flops each, forward pass, dense attention.

  1. Cost of the four (n by d) times (d by d) projections.
  2. Cost of the two attention products, each 2n2d.
  3. Cost of the two feed-forward products.
  4. What share of the block is attention, and at what sequence length does that share reach one half?
Hint

For part 4, do not search for the crossover numerically. Write the attention total and the non-attention total as expressions in n and d, set them equal, and cancel.

Solution
  1. 4 × 2 × 2048 × 12288 × 12288 = 2.474e12 flops.
  2. Each is 2 × 2048 × 2048 × 12288 = 1.031e11, so 2.06e11 together.
  3. Each is 2 × 2048 × 12288 × 49152 = 2.474e12, so 4.949e12 together.
  4. The total is 7.628e12, so attention is 2.06e11 / 7.628e12 = 2.7%. The attention terms total 4n2d and the rest totals 24nd2 (eight from the projections, sixteen from the feed-forward). Equal when 4n2d = 24nd2, so n = 6d = 73,728 tokens.

Sanity check: your three costs should add to about 7.6 Tflop, and the projections and the feed-forward pair should come out to the same number, because 4d2 and 2 × 4d × d are the same quantity.

Your teacher. If the ordering result feels like a trick, ask an agent to time both groupings for a shape you choose, then to print the largest intermediate array each route allocates. The flop count and the peak memory tell the same story, and seeing the memory is often what makes it click.

Primary source to go deeper

Boyd and Vandenberghe, Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra, section 10.1, under "Complexity of matrix multiplication". It derives 2mnp, gives the symmetric saving for a Gram matrix, and works the triple-product comparison in general rather than for one example.
