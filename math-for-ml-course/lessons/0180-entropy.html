Entropy is the average of your own surprise

working 12 min Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (Bell System Technical Journal, 1948). · the paper

The one-minute version

  • An unlikely event tells you more than a likely one, and -log p says how much more.
  • Entropy is the average of that number. Nothing else. Every quantity in this module is entropy rearranged.
  • In base 2 it counts yes/no questions, and it is the floor on how short any code can be.
  • A certainty scores zero, the uniform distribution scores log n, everything real sits between.
  • Shannon did not choose -log p: three requirements leave exactly one function standing.

1. Surprise, before entropy

A weather service tells you it rained in Manchester in November: you shrug. It tells you it snowed in Chennai: you sit up. The same number of words arrived and the second carried far more, so whatever measures information must grow as the probability shrinks.

Two more demands settle the form. A certainty must carry none, because you already knew. And two independent pieces of news must add, so learning that two separate coins both came up heads costs exactly twice what one costs.

I(x) = −log2 p(x) Read as: the surprise, or self-information, of an outcome x is minus the base-two logarithm of the probability p(x) that it happens. The minus sign is there because a probability is at most 1, so its logarithm is at most 0, and we want a positive answer.

All three demands hold. As p falls to zero the logarithm falls without limit. At p = 1 it is zero. And because independent probabilities multiply while logarithms turn products into sums, independent news adds. That last property is the whole reason a logarithm appears at all.

In base two the unit is the bit, a word Shannon credits to J. W. Tukey. Probability one half costs one bit, one in eight costs three. This course teaches in bits and quotes training losses in nats, base e, because that is what frameworks print. One nat is 1.4427 bits.

2. Entropy is that average

A distribution has many outcomes, each with its own surprise and its own probability. Average them, weighted by how often each happens, and you have scored the whole distribution.

H(p) = E[−log2 p(x)] = −∑i pi log2 pi Read as: the entropy H of a distribution p is the expected value, written E, of the surprise. Written out, it is the sum over every possible outcome i of that outcome's probability pi multiplied by its surprise. The sum runs over the whole alphabet of outcomes, and the probabilities add to one.

An outcome with probability zero should contribute nothing, but 0 × log 0 is undefined. Read it as zero. That is licensed rather than convenient: as x falls to zero, x log x falls to zero too.

3. What the number is, operationally

The definition says how to compute entropy, not what the answer means. Shannon's own frame does, because he was designing telegraphy. You send one symbol at a time down a wire and you invent the code. Common symbols get short codes and rare ones long, because the average length is what you pay. The entropy of the source is the shortest average length any code can achieve, and the code that achieves it gives each symbol a length equal to its own surprise.

That is what the number is for. Entropy is not a metaphor about disorder borrowed from thermodynamics: it is a length, in bits, that a real encoder can hit and cannot beat.

4. Why minus log, and nothing else

An engineer is right to be suspicious of a formula that arrives fully formed. Shannon was too, so he did it the other way round: he wrote down what a measure of uncertainty must satisfy, then asked what survives.

His three requirements, in section 6 of the 1948 paper, are that H is continuous in the probabilities; that for equally likely outcomes it increases with how many there are; and that a choice broken into two successive choices gives the weighted sum of the two.

The reframing Shannon's Theorem 2 says that those three requirements leave exactly one function standing: H = −K ∑ p log p, for some positive constant K that only fixes the unit. Entropy is not a definition somebody chose. It is what you are forced into once you say what you want.

The shape of the argument, since the full proof is in his Appendix 2 and this course does not reproduce it. Write A(n) for the entropy of n equally likely outcomes. Apply the grouping requirement to a choice among sm options broken into m successive choices among s, and it forces A(sm) = m A(s). A function on the whole numbers satisfying that and increasing in n has to be K log n. Continuity carries the rest. The step that does the real work is turning the grouping axiom into that functional equation; the rest is squeezing.

Shannon is disarming about how much this matters: the theorem "and the assumptions required for its proof, are in no way necessary for the present theory... The real justification of these definitions, however, will reside in their implications." The uniqueness theorem is reassurance. The reason to care about entropy is the ten pages after it.

5. The two properties you will use

Entropy is zero exactly when the outcome is certain. Every term is zero or negative, so the sum is zero only when every term is. A term is zero when pi is 0 or 1, and the probabilities add to one, so all the mass must sit on one outcome.

Entropy is largest for the uniform distribution. The maximum-entropy bound. Compare p against the uniform ui = 1/n:

  1. Write the constant as a sum. Because the probabilities add to one, log2 n = ∑i pi log2 n. This is the step that does the real work: it turns a comparison of two numbers into one sum over outcomes.
  2. Subtract. log2 n − H(p) = ∑i pi log2 n + ∑i pi log2 pi.
  3. Collect the logarithms. That is ∑i pi log2(n pi), which is ∑i pi log2(pi / ui).
  4. Name it. That last expression is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from p to the uniform distribution, which lesson 182 proves is never negative and is zero only when the two distributions agree. So log2 n − H(p) ≥ 0.

Leaning on a later result is deliberate and safe: lesson 182 proves that divergence is non-negative without using this bound, so nothing is circular. It also says what the gap means, which is the better reason to write the proof this way. The distance from your distribution to the flat one is exactly how many bits you were given for free.

Everything above is about a discrete distribution. The continuous analogue, differential entropy, is not the limit of this quantity: the derivation drops a term that diverges, so the continuous version can be negative. A Gaussian's is ½(1 + ln 2πσ2), which is below zero whenever σ2 < 1/(2πe). If you meet a negative entropy in a paper on variational inference, this is why, and nothing has gone wrong.

6. Worked: Shannon's noisy channel

Shannon's own example from page 21, and the shortest demonstration that entropy measures something the obvious arithmetic misses.

A source sends 1,000 binary symbols per second, zeros and ones equally likely, so it produces 1,000 bits per second. Noise flips one symbol in a hundred. What rate actually gets through? The instinct is 990: subtract the errors. Shannon's own words are that this "is not satisfactory".

  1. Ask what the receiver still does not know. Not how many symbols were wrong but which ones. That is the entropy of the error pattern, one symbol at a time.
  2. Compute the first term. −0.01 log2 0.01 = 0.01 × 6.6439 = 0.0664 bits.
  3. Compute the second. −0.99 log2 0.99 = 0.99 × 0.0145 = 0.0144 bits.
  4. Add. H(0.01) = 0.0808 bits per symbol. This is the equivocation: what is still unknown about the input after seeing the output.
  5. Scale to the second. 1,000 × 0.0808 = 80.8, so the rate is 1000 − 81 = 919 bits per second, which is the figure Shannon prints.
  6. Sanity check. At a flip rate of one half, H(0.5) = 1 bit per symbol, the equivocation eats the whole thousand, and the rate is exactly zero. It must be: the output is then independent of the input.
  7. What changes if the error rate drops tenfold, to 0.001? H(0.001) = 0.0189 bits and the rate becomes 989. Ten times fewer errors bought back only nine tenths of the loss, because −p log p does not shrink in proportion to p.
  8. Derived against quoted. The 919 is Shannon's. The 0.08079 in step 4 is this page recomputing it. They agree, which is the only way to know a transcription was faithful.

A second number worth carrying: a dataset split 90/10 between two classes has entropy 0.4690 bits against a balanced set's 1 bit. More than half the label entropy is gone before any model has seen a feature, which is why accuracy on an imbalanced problem flatters so badly.

7. The same arithmetic at a scale you cannot check by hand

Eight numbers can be done on paper. Twenty thousand cannot, and the point of the code is that the definition does not change when the data does.

code/0180-entropy.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def entropy_from_definition(probabilities):
    """H(p) = -sum_i p_i log2(p_i), one term at a time."""
    total = 0.0
    for p in probabilities:
        if p > 0.0:                      # the 0 log 0 = 0 convention
            total += p * np.log2(p)
    return -total if total != 0.0 else 0.0

shares = signals["plan"].value_counts(normalize=True).sort_index()
slow = entropy_from_definition(shares.to_numpy())
fast = float(-(shares[shares > 0] * np.log2(shares[shares > 0])).sum())
assert abs(slow - fast) < 1e-12          # the loop and the vector agree

On the committed 12,000-row table, plan scores 1.4416 bits against a ceiling of log2 3 = 1.5850, and churned scores 0.8902 bits.

It also checks section 4's third requirement on real data. Ask "which plan?" in one go, or ask "basic, or one of the other two?" and then resolve the rest: 0.999944 + 0.6572 × 0.891111 = 1.441564, against 1.441564 for H(plan) computed directly. They agree to twelve decimal places, so the grouping axiom is a fact about the data and not only a line in a proof.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A dataset has two equally likely classes. You add a third class, also equally likely. What happens to the entropy?
Three equally likely outcomes cost log2 3 = 1.585 bits. The first option confuses balance with count: balance says entropy is at its maximum for that many outcomes, and the maximum itself grows with the count. The second is the disorder metaphor doing damage, since more options is more uncertainty, not less. The fourth counts the classes instead of taking their logarithm, which would only be right at four.
Which of these distributions over four outcomes has the highest entropy?
The uniform distribution attains log2 4 = 2 bits, the maximum available over four outcomes. The second scores 0.2419 bits: it is almost a certainty. The third scores exactly 1 bit, because two of its outcomes never occur, so it is really a two-outcome distribution and its own ceiling is 1. The fourth scores 1.3346 bits, higher than the second and still short, because it is spread but not evenly.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A fraud model's training set contains 998 legitimate and 2 fraudulent transactions per thousand.

Find the entropy of the label column in bits. Then a colleague reports that a baseline model "explains 99.8 per cent of the labels": say what their number measures and what yours does.

Hint

Both terms matter, and it is not the one you expect that dominates. The rare class has a tiny probability and an enormous surprise. Work out which factor wins before deciding the answer is near zero.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the common class. −0.998 log2 0.998 = 0.998 × 0.00289 = 0.00288 bits.
  2. Take the rare one. −0.002 log2 0.002 = 0.002 × 8.9658 = 0.01793 bits.
  3. Add. 0.0188 bits. The rare class supplies 86 per cent of it, on 0.2 per cent of the rows.
  4. Answer the colleague. Their 99.8 per cent is the accuracy of a model that always says "legitimate": it measures how often a constant is right. Your 0.0188 bits measures how much uncertainty there ever was to remove. A model that removes none of it still scores 99.8 per cent, which is why the metric and the loss disagree here, and why the training loop optimises the second.

Sanity check. The answer must lie between 0 and 1 bit, because there are two classes and one bit is the ceiling for two. Anything above 1 means the two surprises were summed instead of averaged by their probabilities.

Your teacher. If the code reading did not land, ask an agent to build a Huffman code for 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125 and then for 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, printing the average code length beside the entropy for each. The first matches exactly and the second does not quite. Asking why is the fastest route into what the floor really claims.
