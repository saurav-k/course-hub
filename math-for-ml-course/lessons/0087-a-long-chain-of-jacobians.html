A long chain of Jacobians: what backprop costs, and how the signal decays

frontier 13 min Why reverse mode won, what you pay for it, and why depth used to be unusable.

The one-minute version

  • Forward mode costs one sweep per input. Reverse mode costs one per output. Machine learning has millions of inputs and one loss, so the choice is not close.
  • You pay for it in memory: every intermediate a backward rule needs must stay alive.
  • Backpropagation is neither symbolic nor numerical differentiation, and confusing it with either gives a wrong model of what it costs.
  • A gradient reaching back k steps carries a product of k Jacobians, and products shrink or blow up geometrically.

1. Two modes, and why only one of them scales here

The chain rule is a product of Jacobians and matrix multiplication is associative, so you may bracket the product however you like. Bracketing from the input end is forward mode; from the output end is reverse mode. Same answer, wildly different cost.

cost of the m by n Jacobian:   forward   n · c · ops(f)    reverse   m · c · ops(f) n: the number of inputs, for us the number of parameters. m: the number of outputs, for us one, because a loss is a single number. ops(f): how many operations it takes to evaluate the function once. c: a constant, guaranteed below 6 and usually between 2 and 3. The whole content is which of n and m multiplies the cost.

The rule generalises: reverse mode wins when outputs are few and inputs many, and forward mode wins the other way round. Training a network is the extreme case of the first.

2. What you pay instead

Reverse mode has to run the function forwards before it can walk backwards, and the backward rules need the intermediate values. So every activation stays alive until its gradient has been used.

3. What it is not

Two confusions, both of which give a wrong mental model of the cost.

It is not symbolic differentiation. Symbolic methods build an expression for the derivative, which suffers what the literature calls expression swell, and they need the model written as a closed form, which rules out branching and loops. Backpropagation carries numbers, not expressions.

It is not finite differences. Those are approximate, they inherit the round-off floor from Lesson 80, and they need one function evaluation per parameter, which is the forward-mode cost with worse accuracy.

The reframingAutomatic differentiation is exact like symbolic differentiation and numeric like finite differences, and it is neither. It applies symbolic rules while keeping only values, which is why it costs a small constant factor and gets the right answer.

4. The decay, and the theorem behind it

Now the failure mode. A gradient reaching back from step t to step k is multiplied by a product of t − k Jacobians. Products of matrices behave like products of numbers: they shrink towards nothing or grow without bound, and only a knife edge does neither.

Theorem (sufficient condition for vanishing). Consider xk+1 = σ(Wxk) with |σ′| bounded by γ. If the largest singular value of W satisfies λ1 < 1/γ, the contribution of step k to the gradient at step t decays exponentially in t − k.

What is assumed: a bound on the activation's slope, and a bound on the weight matrix. The shape of the argument: bound one factor, then multiply the bound by itself. The step that does the real work is submultiplicativity of the operator norm, which is what lets a bound on one Jacobian become a bound on a product of them.

  1. Write one link's Jacobian. dxk+1/dxk = diag(σ′(Wxk)) W.
  2. Bound its norm. The operator norm of a product is at most the product of the norms, and a diagonal matrix's operator norm is its largest absolute entry, so the bound is γ × λ1. Call it η.
  3. Use the hypothesis. λ1 < 1/γ gives η < 1. This is the only place the assumption is spent.
  4. Multiply the bounds. By induction on t − k, the product of that many Jacobians has norm at most ηt−k.
  5. Read the consequence. η < 1, so η raised to a growing power tends to zero exponentially fast. QED
The converse says less than people quoteRunning the argument backwards gives that λ1 > 1/γ is necessary for the gradient to explode, not sufficient. The bound controls the largest growth any direction can see; a typical direction does worse. Measured on thirty independent random Jacobians at spectral radius exactly 1.00, a unit gradient still arrives with norm 0.063. "Spectral radius above one means exploding gradients" is the slogan, and it is not the theorem.

5. Worked: why the field abandoned sigmoid recurrent units

η = γ × λ1 ,   vanishing is guaranteed when η < 1 , that is λ1 < 1/γ γ: the largest the activation's slope can be, a property of the function alone. λ1: the largest singular value of the recurrent weight matrix, a property of the weights. η: their product, the per-layer factor. 1/γ: the threshold the weights have to beat, and the whole point is that the activation sets it.
  1. Take tanh. Its derivative is at most 1, so γ = 1 and the threshold is λ1 < 1.
  2. Take the logistic sigmoid. Its derivative is at most 0.25, from Lesson 81, so γ = 1/4 and the threshold is λ1 < 4.
  3. Compare the two thresholds. A sigmoid network vanishes for any recurrent matrix with largest singular value below 4, and almost every sensible initialisation is below 4.
  4. Put a depth on it. At η = 0.9 over thirty steps: 0.930 = 0.0424, a 24-fold shrink. Over a hundred steps: 2.66 × 10−5.
  5. Do the other side. At η = 1.1 over thirty steps: 1.130 = 17.45. Over a hundred: 13,781.

Sanity check. 0.930 must sit between 0.940 = 0.0148 and 0.920 = 0.122, and 0.0424 does. An answer above 0.9 or below 0.001 is an exponent error rather than a surprising result.

What changes if you keep the sigmoid but scale the recurrent matrix up so that λ1 = 8? The sufficient condition no longer applies, so vanishing is not guaranteed. It also does not follow that gradients explode, because that direction of the theorem is only necessary. What you get is a network whose behaviour depends on the data, which is worse than either failure.

In words. The activation function sets the threshold and the weights decide which side of it you are on. That is why the fix was architectural rather than a matter of tuning: no learning rate rescues a factor applied thirty times.

The bound is on operation counts and the measurement is of wall-clock on vectorised hardware, and quoting one as if it were the other is the mistake to avoid.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

For a scalar loss over n parameters, how many reverse-mode sweeps does the full gradient need?
Reverse mode costs one sweep per output and a loss is one number. The first is the forward-mode count and is the trap the whole page is built around. The second confuses a sweep with a phase: the forward pass is not a reverse-mode sweep. The fourth confuses the cost of one sweep, which does grow with depth, with the number of sweeps, which does not.
A recurrent network uses the logistic sigmoid. Vanishing is guaranteed when the largest singular value is below what?
The threshold is one over gamma, and gamma is 0.25 for the sigmoid, so the threshold is 4. The second is the tanh answer, where gamma is 1. The third quotes gamma itself rather than its reciprocal, which is the arithmetic slip this question exists to catch. The fourth is false and the whole point is that the condition holds far too easily.

A thirty-layer network has a per-layer Jacobian whose largest singular value is 0.9.

bound on the product = ηt − k Read as: the bound on the gradient reaching back from step t to step k, where η is the per-layer factor and t − k is how many layers it crossed.

Compute the shrink factor across all thirty layers. Repeat for 1.1. Then name the mitigation each case motivates, without describing how it works.

Hint

One power each. And before you reach for a calculator, decide whether each answer should be bigger or smaller than 1, so you can tell immediately if the arithmetic slipped.

Show the worked solution
  1. The shrinking case. 0.930 = 0.0424, so the signal arrives at about 4 per cent of its size, a shrink of roughly 24 times.
  2. The growing case. 1.130 = 17.45, so it arrives about 17 times larger.
  3. The mitigations. Vanishing motivates residual connections and gated recurrent cells, which give the gradient a path that does not pass through every factor. Exploding motivates gradient clipping, which bounds the step regardless of the gradient's size.
  4. Where they live. Clipping is Module 06's. Residuals and gates belong to the architecture courses in this hub, not to a calculus module.

Sanity check. The two answers are not reciprocals, because 0.9 and 1.1 are not reciprocals: 0.0424 × 17.45 = 0.74, not 1. Getting exactly 1 means 1/0.9 was used in place of 1.1.
