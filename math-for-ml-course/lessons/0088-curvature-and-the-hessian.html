Curvature is the second derivative, and the Hessian holds it for every direction at once

frontier 13 min The gradient makes a promise. This page is about whether it will be kept.

The one-minute version

  • The gradient predicts a straight line. The function is not one, and the gap is curvature.
  • In many dimensions there is a second derivative for every direction, and the Hessian holds all of them.
  • It is symmetric, but only under a hypothesis, and there is a function that breaks it.
  • The second-derivative test has four branches, not three, and the fourth is the one every summary omits.

1. The gradient's promise, and three ways it can be broken

Stand at a point, read the gradient, and it tells you the loss falls at a certain rate. That rate is a statement about an infinitesimal step. Take a real one and three things can happen.

2. Collect the second derivatives

Hij = ∂²f / ∂xi∂xj f: a loss, taking a vector of parameters and returning one number. xi, xj: two of the parameters, possibly the same one. ∂²f/∂xi∂xj: differentiate with respect to xj, then differentiate the result with respect to xi. H: the Hessian, a square matrix with one entry per pair of parameters.

Equivalently, the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient: take the gradient from Lesson 83, treat it as a vector-valued function, and apply Lesson 85.

3. Symmetry, and the hypothesis that earns it

Theorem (Schwarz). If both mixed second partials exist near a point and are continuous at it, they are equal there. So the Hessian is symmetric wherever the second partials are continuous.

What is assumed: existence near the point and continuity at it. Both are used. The shape of the argument: build one quantity that can be evaluated two ways, apply the mean value theorem twice in each, and let the rectangle shrink. The step that does the real work is the second application, because that is what converts a difference of first partials into a mixed second partial.

  1. Build the second difference. D(h, k) = f(p+h, q+k) − f(p+h, q) − f(p, q+k) + f(p, q), the alternating sum over the corners of a small rectangle.
  2. Read it one way. Group the terms as φ(p+h) − φ(p) where φ(x) = f(x, q+k) − f(x, q). One mean value theorem gives D = h[fx(p+sh, q+k) − fx(p+sh, q)] for some s in (0, 1).
  3. Apply it again, in the other argument. D = hk fyx(p+sh, q+tk) for some t in (0, 1).
  4. Read it the other way. Group instead as ψ(q+k) − ψ(q) and run the identical argument: D = hk fxy(p+s′h, q+t′k).
  5. Divide and shrink. Divide both by hk and let (h, k) go to zero. Both interior points converge on (p, q), and continuity of the mixed partials there forces the two limits to agree. QED
The hypothesis is not decorationDrop continuity and the conclusion fails. For f(x, y) = xy(x² − y²)/(x² + y²) away from the origin and 0 at it, both mixed partials exist at the origin and they are −1 and +1. The Hessian is not symmetric there. Most functions in deep learning satisfy the hypothesis almost everywhere, which is a reason to state it once rather than a reason to skip it.

4. Curvature in a chosen direction

The Hessian holds every second derivative, and a particular direction picks one out.

second derivative along a unit d = dT H d d: a unit direction, the same object Lesson 84 walked along. H: the Hessian at the point. dTHd: a single number, the rate at which the slope changes as you move along d. This is the quadratic form from Module 04, and here it has a meaning: curvature.

Because H is symmetric it has an orthonormal eigenbasis, from Module 04. Along an eigenvector the answer is just that eigenvector's eigenvalue, and every other direction gives a weighted average. So the largest and smallest eigenvalues bracket the curvature in every direction, and there is nothing outside that range to worry about.

5. The second-derivative test, all four branches

Theorem. Let a be a critical point of a twice continuously differentiable f, and let H be the Hessian there with eigenvalues l1 to ln. All positive gives a strict local minimum; all negative a strict local maximum; mixed signs a saddle; and a zero eigenvalue with the rest sharing a sign gives no answer at all.

The shape of the argument: put the second-order Taylor expansion at a critical point, where the first-order term vanishes, then diagonalise. The step that does the real work is the eigenbasis, because it turns one quadratic form into a sum of independent one-dimensional problems.

  1. Expand at the critical point. The gradient is zero there, so f(a+h) = f(a) + ½hTHh plus a remainder smaller than ||h||².
  2. Diagonalise. Write h in the eigenbasis as a sum of civi. Then hTHh = ∑ lici², a sum of independent terms.
  3. All positive. That sum is at least l1||h||² and l1 > 0, so the difference is positive for every small enough non-zero h, dominating the remainder. A strict local minimum.
  4. All negative. Apply the previous branch to −f.
  5. Mixed signs. Move along an eigenvector with a positive eigenvalue and f rises; along one with a negative eigenvalue and f falls. A minimum on one cross-section and a maximum on another is what a saddle is.
  6. A zero eigenvalue. Along its eigenvector the quadratic term contributes nothing, so the sign is decided entirely by the remainder, about which the theorem says nothing. QED
Both outcomes really do occurf = x² + y⁴ has a minimum at the origin. f = x² − y⁴ has a saddle. Both have Hessian diag(2, 0) there, so no test that looks only at the Hessian can tell them apart. That is why the fourth branch exists and why it is not a technicality.

6. Worked: the sensor regression, and how badly conditioned it is

H = (2/n) XTX X: the design matrix, one row per reading and one column per parameter, per this course's convention. n: the number of readings, 12,000. H: the Hessian of the mean squared error. For a squared-error loss it does not depend on the parameters at all, which is exactly what makes a quadratic the reference surface everything else is compared to.
  1. Check symmetry. XTX is symmetric by construction, and the numerical mixed partials agree to 0.00e+00 on every pair tested.
  2. Take the eigenvalues. 5.6081 × 10−3, 0.72374, 0.72996, 5.2127, 14.314, 116.28 and 1.9843 × 105.
  3. Apply the test. Every one is positive, so the critical point is a strict local minimum, and since the loss is quadratic it is the global one.
  4. Check the bracket. Random unit directions give dTHd values that all land between the smallest and largest eigenvalues, and each matches a second central difference along that ray.
  5. Divide. The condition number is 1.9843 × 105 ÷ 5.6081 × 10−3 = 35,382,029.

Sanity check. Every eigenvalue of (2/n)XTX must be non-negative, because hTXTXh = ||Xh||² and a squared length cannot be negative. A negative eigenvalue means the matrix was formed wrongly, not that the surface is strange. They must also sum to the trace, which is one line to compute independently.

What changes if a column is duplicated? X loses rank, the smallest eigenvalue becomes exactly zero, and the verdict moves from branch one to branch four. The loss then has a flat valley of equally good answers rather than one, which is the calculus statement of what collinearity does. On this table vibration_x and vibration_y correlate at 0.987, which is why the smallest eigenvalue is already so close to the floor.

In words. A single number, the condition number, says how far from a bowl this surface is, and Lesson 89 turns that number into a limit on how big a step you may take.

Is that eigenvalue zero? It is a numerical question tooWriting the test in code, my first version treated an eigenvalue as zero when it fell below 10−9 times the largest. On this Hessian that threshold is larger than the true smallest eigenvalue, so a genuinely positive definite matrix was classified "inconclusive". The tolerance has to be tied to machine epsilon rather than to a fraction of the spectrum: at eps × n × λmax = 3.08 × 10−10, the smallest eigenvalue clears it by a factor of 18 million and the verdict is right. On a badly conditioned Hessian, "is this zero" is a question about your arithmetic as well as about the mathematics.

7. And a warning about pictures of loss surfaces

A two-dimensional plot of a loss surface is not evidence unless it says how it was normalised. Rectified networks are scale-invariant in their weights: multiply one layer up and the next down and the function is unchanged, but a unit-sized perturbation now means something completely different. So a network with large weights looks smooth and one with small weights looks jagged, for no reason connected to the training. Comparing two such pictures requires filter normalisation first, and a paper that does not mention it has shown you a picture of its own weight scale.

What that geometry is actually like in high dimensions, and why a zero gradient there is almost never a minimum, is Module 06's. This page owns only the test that makes that argument sayable.

def classify(eigenvalues):
    """The second-derivative test, including the branch people forget.

    The tolerance is the delicate part. An eigenvalue counts as zero only
    below what the solver itself can resolve. A looser rule calls a badly
    conditioned but genuinely positive definite Hessian singular.
    """
    scale = max(float(np.abs(eigenvalues).max()), 1.0)
    tol = np.finfo(float).eps * len(eigenvalues) * scale
    zero, positive, negative = np.abs(eigenvalues) <= tol, eigenvalues > tol, eigenvalues < -tol
    if zero.any() and not (positive.any() and negative.any()):
        return "inconclusive - a zero eigenvalue and no sign disagreement"

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

At a critical point the Hessian has eigenvalues +3, +1 and -2. What kind of point is it?
One direction curves up and another curves down, which is the definition of a saddle. The first and second each look at one eigenvalue and ignore the others, which is the whole error the test exists to prevent. The fourth misremembers the inconclusive branch, which needs a zero eigenvalue rather than unequal ones, and no test would ever require the eigenvalues to be equal.
When is the multidimensional second-derivative test inconclusive?
A zero eigenvalue leaves the quadratic term silent along that direction, so the remainder decides and the theorem does not cover it. The first is exactly the conclusive saddle case, which makes it the strongest distractor. The third is a genuine problem with a different cause and a different remedy, namely the continuity hypothesis in Schwarz. The fourth is backwards: the test gains a branch in higher dimensions, it does not stop working.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Two features, five readings, a squared-error loss with the columns centred.

H = 2 XTX Read as: twice the matrix of column dot products. X has one row per reading and one column per feature, and XTX is therefore two by two with the sum of squares of each column on its diagonal.

Write H, find its eigenvalues, classify the critical point, and compute the condition number.

Hint

For a symmetric two by two you need no solver: the eigenvalues are the roots of l² − (trace)l + (determinant) = 0.

Hint for the classification

You do not need the eigenvalues to know the sign. Ask what hTXTXh equals for any h, and whether that can ever be negative.

Show the worked solution
  1. Form the matrix. H = 2XTX is symmetric with the doubled sums of squares on the diagonal and the doubled cross-product off it.
  2. Solve the quadratic. The two roots of l² − (trace)l + (determinant) = 0.
  3. Classify without computing. hTXTXh = ||Xh||² ≥ 0 for every h, so no eigenvalue can be negative, and if X has full column rank none is zero either. Every eigenvalue positive, so a strict local minimum.
  4. Note why the verdict is global. The loss is quadratic, so the Hessian is the same everywhere and there is only one critical point.
  5. Divide. The condition number is the larger root over the smaller. On the full seven-parameter sensor problem this is 198,430 ÷ 0.0056081 = 35,382,029.

Sanity check. The two eigenvalues must sum to the trace of H. Compute the trace independently as twice the sum of the two columns' squared lengths and compare. If they do not sum to it, the quadratic was solved wrongly rather than the matrix being unusual.

Your teacher. If the fourth branch still feels like a technicality, ask an agent to plot x² + y⁴ and x² − y⁴ near the origin side by side and then to print both Hessians there. Seeing two different surfaces with the same matrix is what makes the branch stick. If the condition number is the sticking point, ask it to draw the contours of a quadratic with eigenvalues 1 and 100.
