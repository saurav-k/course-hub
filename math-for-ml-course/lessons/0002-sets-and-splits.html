A split is a partition, and leakage is a non-empty intersection

foundation 11 min Set notation is not abstract. It is the vocabulary of every split you have ever made.

The one-minute version

  • A set is unordered and has no duplicates, which is why a vocabulary is a set and a token stream is not.
  • Train, validation and test are a partition: they cover everything and overlap nowhere. That one word is the entire contract.
  • Leakage is that contract broken, and it is usually broken by a statistic crossing the line rather than by a row.
  • Intersection over union, the headline metric of object detection, is two set operations and a division.
  • The two results this page proves - inclusion-exclusion and De Morgan - are the ones you use without noticing.

1. What a set is, and what it is not

A set is a collection with two properties: it does not remember order, and it does not keep duplicates. Both matter in practice more than they sound.

Take one ticket reading login login error page login. As a sequence it has five tokens; as a set it is {login, error, page} and has three members. Neither is wrong, and they answer different questions: word frequencies need the sequence, and "does this mention login at all" needs the set. Reaching for the wrong one is a quiet bug.

Four pieces of notation carry almost everything.

x ∈ A    A ⊆ B    ∅    |A| Read as: x is a member of A; every member of A is also a member of B; the empty set, which has no members at all; and the number of members in A, called its cardinality.

And set-builder notation, which reads as a filter and nothing more exotic. {x ∈ A : condition} is "the members of A that satisfy the condition" - the same thing your WHERE clause does.

2. The four operations, and the one that double-counts

3. Inclusion-exclusion, proved

That last observation is a theorem, and it is worth proving because the proof is the idea.

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B| Read as: the number of things in either set equals the number in the first, plus the number in the second, minus the number counted twice because they are in both.

Proof. Every member of A ∪ B lies in exactly one of three pieces: in A alone, in B alone, or in both. The three pieces do not overlap, so their sizes add. Now count |A| + |B| and ask what each piece contributed: the first piece was counted once, the second once, and the third twice, because it sits inside A and inside B. Subtracting |A ∩ B| removes exactly one of those two copies.

The step that does the work The three pieces being disjoint is what lets their sizes be added at all. Everything else is bookkeeping. Whenever you meet a counting argument that goes wrong, this is almost always the step that was skipped.

4. De Morgan, proved

The other result you use without noticing. It is what tells you how to negate a filter.

(A ∪ B)c = Ac ∩ Bc     (A ∩ B)c = Ac ∪ Bc Read as: not in either one means outside both; and not in both means outside at least one. The superscript c means the complement, everything not in that set.

Proof of the first. Suppose x is in (A ∪ B)c. Then x is not in A ∪ B, so it is in neither A nor B, so it is in Ac and in Bc, which is to say in Ac ∩ Bc. Every step reverses, so the two sets contain each other and are equal. The step that does the work is "not in a union means in neither", which is the definition of union read backwards.

The practical form: the negation of status == 'open' OR priority == 'high' is status != 'open' AND priority != 'high'. Changing the OR to an AND is not optional, and a filter negated without it silently keeps rows you meant to drop.

5. A split is a partition, and leakage breaks it

Train, validation and test are a partition of the dataset: every row is in exactly one, and together they are everything. Both halves of that are promises, and the second one is the promise people break.

scikit-learn's own definition is worth keeping: leakage happens when "information that would not be available at prediction time is used when building the model". Notice what it does not say. It says nothing about rows.

The dataset for this course is split per row, and the row sets really are disjoint. It is still leaky, and badly: 510 of the 527 customers in the test split also appear in training. A model can learn the customer instead of the label and the score will not tell you. Finding this is the practice problem at the foot of the page.

6. Intersection over union, which is the definition read aloud

Object detection scores a predicted box against a true box with a quantity built from exactly the two operations above.

ao = area(Bp ∩ Bgt) / area(Bp ∪ Bgt) Read as: the area the two boxes share, divided by the area they cover between them. Bp is the predicted box and Bgt the ground-truth box. The PASCAL VOC challenge calls a detection correct when this exceeds 0.5.
  1. Find the overlap in each direction. Across: 70 to 150, which is 80. Down: 60 to 200, which is 140.
  2. Multiply for the intersection. 80 × 140 = 11,200.
  3. Apply inclusion-exclusion for the union. 14,000 + 17,000 − 11,200 = 19,800.
  4. Divide. 11,200 ÷ 19,800 = 0.566, which clears the 0.5 bar, so this counts as a detection.
Sanity check. IoU always lands between 0 and 1, and these boxes visibly share more than half their area, so 0.57 is believable where 0.9 would not be. What changes if the prediction shifts 30 pixels right: the shared area shrinks, the union grows, and the score falls through 0.5 into a miss.

7. Both theorems, checked on real data

The program for this lesson does not illustrate the two proofs. It tests them, on the customer sets of the actual dataset, and then finds the leakage.

code/0002-sets-and-splits.py · runnable Python, numpy and pandas only · download · data: tickets.csv
train = set(frame.loc[frame.row_split == "train", "customer_id"])
test  = set(frame.loc[frame.row_split == "test",  "customer_id"])

assert len(train | test) == len(train) + len(test) - len(train & test)   # inclusion-exclusion

universe = set(frame["customer_id"])
assert universe - (train | test) == (universe - train) & (universe - test)   # De Morgan
assert universe - (train & test) == (universe - train) | (universe - test)   # and the other one

both = train & test
print(f"customers in BOTH: {len(both)} of {len(test)} test customers")   # 510 of 527

It then splits by customer instead of by row and asserts the overlap is empty, which is the fix stated as a test rather than as advice.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A predicted box and a true box share 11,200 square pixels. What do you divide by to get IoU?
The denominator is the union, which is exactly the area covered by either box. The first option gives a recall-like quantity and is the most common wrong version, because it rewards a prediction for being enormous. The second gives a precision-like one. The fourth is the trap this page's first theorem exists to close: adding the two areas counts the shared region twice, and the union subtracts one copy back.
A statistic is fitted on all rows, then the rows are split. The row sets are still disjoint. What went wrong?
Leakage is about information, not rows, and a mean or a scale fitted on everything carries knowledge of the test rows into training. The first option is the misconception the whole page is aimed at. The third is simply false: no row moved anywhere. The fourth is also false, and the counts are exactly what you asked for, which is part of why this is hard to spot.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

(a) A predicted box spans (0, 0) to (100, 100) and the true box spans (50, 50) to (150, 150). Compute IoU and say whether PASCAL VOC counts it as a detection.

(b) This course's ticket data is split by row, and the row sets are disjoint. Why is an evaluation on it still optimistic, and what is the fix?

Hint

For (b), ask what a model could memorise instead of the label that would still score well, then ask whether the split separated that thing.

Show the worked solution
  1. Find the overlap for (a). Across: from 50 to 100, which is 50. Down: the same, 50. So the intersection is 50 × 50 = 2,500.
  2. Take the two areas. Each box is 100 by 100, so 10,000 each.
  3. Apply inclusion-exclusion. Union = 10,000 + 10,000 − 2,500 = 17,500.
  4. Divide. 2,500 ÷ 17,500 = 0.143. That is well under 0.5, so it is a miss, even though the two boxes are the same size and clearly overlap.
  5. Name the unit for (b). The partition is over tickets. But several tickets belong to one customer, and customers write in a recognisable way. The model can learn the customer rather than the urgency.
  6. Say it as sets. train ∩ test is empty as sets of tickets, and has 510 members as sets of customers. Both statements are true at once, and only the second one predicts the evaluation.
  7. State the fix. Partition by the leakage unit: choose which customers are held out, then take all of their tickets. The program for this lesson does exactly that and asserts the resulting overlap is empty.

Sanity check. In (a), IoU must land in [0, 1], and two equal boxes offset by half their width should score well below a half - if you got a number above 0.5, you divided by one box rather than by the union. In (b), if your answer does not name a unit other than the row, it has not found the bug.

Your teacher. The two proofs on this page are short on purpose: they are the kind you should be able to reconstruct, not memorise. If either one slipped past, ask an agent to "prove inclusion-exclusion for two sets by splitting the union into disjoint pieces, and then show me a case where the pieces are not disjoint and the argument fails" - watching it break is what makes the disjointness step stick.
