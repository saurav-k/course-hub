Adaptive learning rates: one step size per parameter

working 13 min Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2015, and Reddi, Kale & Kumar, ICLR 2018. · arXiv:1412.6980

The one-minute version

  • These methods give every parameter its own step size, computed from how large that parameter's own recent gradients have been.
  • AdaGrad divides by the square root of a running sum, which only grows, so its effective rate can only fall. RMSProp swaps the sum for a moving average, which can rise again.
  • Adam is RMSProp plus momentum plus bias correction. Without the correction the first step is mis-scaled by a factor of a thousand.
  • Adam's published convergence proof is wrong, and there is a convex problem on which it converges to the worst point in the feasible set.

1. The problem they were built for

AdaGrad's authors put it exactly: the adaptation lets you "find needles in haystacks in the form of very predictive but rarely seen features". Think of a bag-of-words model where one term appears in one document in ten thousand. That weight sees a gradient once every ten thousand steps, and a step size tuned for the common features serves it terribly.

So: scale each parameter's step by how much action that parameter has seen.

2. AdaGrad, and the flaw that is structural

rj ← rj + gj2,    θj ← θj − ( η / √rj ) gj j: the index over parameters, so every line here is per-parameter. gj: the j-th entry of the current gradient. rj: a running sum of that parameter's squared gradients, starting at zero. √rj: its square root, which is roughly the accumulated size of the gradients this parameter has seen. η/√rj: the effective step size for this parameter alone.

Rare parameters have small r and get big steps. Busy ones get small steps. That is the design, and it works well on sparse problems.

Now the flaw, which needs no assumptions at all. Every gj2 is non-negative, so rj never decreases, so √rj never decreases, so η/√rj never increases. On every problem, for every parameter, forever. A long run stalls, and nothing in the algorithm can raise the rate again once it has fallen.

3. RMSProp: replace the sum with an average

MeanSquare(w, t) = 0.9 × MeanSquare(w, t−1) + 0.1 × g2 MeanSquare(w, t): a running average of the squared gradient for weight w at step t. 0.9 and 0.1: the decay and its complement, so the average forgets at a fixed rate rather than accumulating forever. g2: the current squared gradient. The step then divides by the square root of this, exactly as AdaGrad did.

An average can go down as well as up, so the effective rate can recover when a parameter becomes active again. That single change is the whole of RMSProp.

RMSProp has no paper. Its primary source is slide 29 of lecture 6e of Hinton's Coursera course, which states the rule above and attributes it "(Tijmen Tieleman, unpublished)". A method used in millions of training runs is cited to a lecture slide. That is the honest citation, and inventing a paper reference for it would be the dishonest one.

4. Adam, and why bias correction is not a detail

Adam keeps two moving averages: m of the gradient, which is momentum, and v of its square, which is RMSProp. Both start at zero, and that is the problem the correction solves.

  1. Unroll the second moment. vt = (1 − β2) ∑i=1..t β2t−i gi2.
  2. Take expectations, assuming for the moment the true second moment is steady at E[g2].
  3. Sum the geometric series. ∑i=1..t β2t−i = (1 − β2t)/(1 − β2).
  4. Collect. E[vt] = E[g2] × (1 − β2t). The estimate is too small by exactly that factor, and dividing by it removes the bias exactly. That is the step that does the real work, and everything else is bookkeeping.

The assumption in step 2 is not free: the source calls the leftover ζ, the residual when the true second moment drifts. Naming it is part of stating the result honestly.

The paper's defaults are α = 0.001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 10-8. The step is α times the corrected first moment divided by the square root of the corrected second, which the authors read as a trust region: the step magnitude is approximately bounded by α, so α sets a scale in parameter space rather than a rate. That is Lesson 102's bounded-step idea arriving a second time, and it is why α is easier to set than an SGD learning rate.

5. Worked: Adam walking to the worst point in the set

Reddi, Kale and Kumar's counterexample is small enough to state in three lines and it settles the question.

  1. The feasible set. All x between −1 and +1.
  2. The cost at step t. ft(x) = Cx when t is 1 more than a multiple of 3, and −x otherwise, with C > 2.
  3. Find the optimum. Over any block of three steps the total is (C − 2)x, which is positive-coefficient and therefore minimised at x = −1.
  4. Set C = 3, so β2 = 1/(1 + C2) = 0.1, and β1 = 0 as the paper does.
  5. Run it. x reaches +0.0045 by step 3, +0.674 by step 300, and +1.000 by step 3,000.
  6. Read the answer. Adam converges to +1, which is the worst point in the feasible set, while the optimum is −1.

Why, in one sentence. The informative gradient C arrives once every three steps and is divided by a second-moment estimate that it itself dominates, so it is scaled down by roughly C; the two misleading gradients of −1 are not scaled down, and they win the average.

Sanity check. Plain SGD and AdaGrad both solve this problem without difficulty, which is what makes it a statement about the exponential moving average rather than about stochastic optimization in general.

What changes with a bigger ε? Nothing that helps. The paper proves that for any constant ε there is a problem of this shape where Adam has non-zero average regret, so the usual "increase epsilon" reflex does not repair it.

What it means. Adam remains an excellent practical default and this course recommends it. It is not a guaranteed one, and the difference matters when a run misbehaves: "Adam is proven to converge" is a sentence that cannot be used to rule anything out.

Two more costs, in the same breath as the technique. Adaptive methods can generalise worse than SGD even when they reach a lower training loss (Wilson et al., 2017). And adding an L2 term to the loss is not weight decay once the optimizer is adaptive (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), which is what the W in AdamW fixes and why every modern language-model recipe uses AdamW rather than Adam. Lesson 110 returns to that second one where the equivalence is derived.
code/0106-adaptive-learning-rates.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
    C = 3.0
    beta2 = 1.0 / (1.0 + C * C)
    x, first, second = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for step in range(1, 2_000_001):
        g = C if step % 3 == 1 else -1.0
        first = 0.0 * first + 1.0 * g          # beta1 = 0, as the paper sets it
        second = beta2 * second + (1 - beta2) * g * g
        x -= (0.1 / np.sqrt(step)) * first / (np.sqrt(second) + 1e-16)
        x = min(1.0, max(-1.0, x))             # project onto the feasible set

What does Adam's bias correction fix?
Both moving averages are initialised at zero, so early on they understate the true moments, and dividing by (1 − βt) removes exactly that. The first uses "bias" in a completely different sense. The third is a real Adam problem, which is why it is the strongest distractor here, but it is what AdamW fixes and it has nothing to do with initialisation. The fourth is what the moving average itself already handles.
What did Reddi, Kale and Kumar show about Adam in 2018?
They built a one-dimensional convex problem where Adam converges to +1 while the optimum is −1, and identified the error in the original convergence proof. The first is Wilson et al., a different and also real result, which makes it the strongest distractor on this page. The second is explicitly ruled out: they show no constant epsilon rescues it. The fourth describes AdaGrad's rate decay misremembered as a memory cost.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A parameter's gradient is 0.1 for a hundred steps and then 10.0 once. Adam runs with its defaults:

α = 0.001,   β1 = 0.9,   β2 = 0.999,   ε = 10-8 Read as: the four hyperparameters of Algorithm 1. α is the step scale, β1 decays the gradient average, β2 decays the squared-gradient average, and ε keeps the division safe when the second moment is near zero.

Find the update magnitude at t = 1, and then explain why the spike at t = 101 does not produce a step a hundred times larger.

Hint

Compute the corrected first and second moments separately at t = 1 and look at their ratio, not at either one on its own. Almost everything about Adam's behaviour is in that ratio.

Hint for the second half

Ask which of the two averages the spike moves more, given that one forgets over roughly ten steps and the other over roughly a thousand.

Show the worked solution
  1. First moment at t = 1. m = 0.9(0) + 0.1(0.1) = 0.01.
  2. Second moment at t = 1. v = 0.999(0) + 0.001(0.01) = 1 × 10-5.
  3. Correct them. m-hat = 0.01 ÷ (1 − 0.9) = 0.1. v-hat = 10-5 ÷ (1 − 0.999) = 0.01.
  4. Take the step. 0.001 × 0.1 ÷ (√0.01 + 10-8) = 0.001 × 0.1 ÷ 0.1 = 0.001, which is exactly α.
  5. Compare without correction. 0.001 × 0.01 ÷ √(10-5) = 0.001 × 0.01 ÷ 0.00316 = 0.00316, three times larger and in the wrong proportion.
  6. Now the spike. β1 = 0.9 averages over roughly ten steps, so m moves a long way. β2 = 0.999 averages over roughly a thousand, so v barely moves.
  7. Read the ratio. The numerator rises sharply and the denominator does not, so the step rises, but it is bounded by construction: the ratio of a first moment to the square root of a second moment cannot exceed roughly 1 in size.
  8. Name the property. That bound is the trust region: the step stays within a small multiple of α whatever the gradient does, which is why α can be set once and left.

Sanity check. Every update magnitude you compute must stay within roughly a factor of ten of α = 0.001. If any of them comes out near 10.0, you have used the raw gradient somewhere instead of the moment ratio, and you have lost the exact property that makes Adam's step size easy to choose.
