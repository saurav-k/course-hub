KL divergence measures the excess, and it has a direction

working 12 min Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (2006), section 1.6.1, after Kullback and Leibler (1951).

The one-minute version

  • Take what you pay and subtract what you must pay. What is left is your model's error, in bits.
  • It is never negative, and zero only when the two distributions agree exactly. That is a theorem, proved here.
  • It is not symmetric, so it is not a distance, and three other properties of a distance also fail.
  • Which direction you minimise decides what your fitted model looks like: one blurs across modes, the other picks one.
  • One outcome your model rules out makes it infinite. This is why nothing ever outputs an exact zero.

1. The subtraction

Lesson 181 ended with a decomposition: what you pay is what you must pay, plus something non-negative. Give that something a name and study it on its own.

  1. Write what you pay. H(p, q) = −∑ pi log2 qi.
  2. Write what you must pay. H(p) = −∑ pi log2 pi.
  3. Subtract. ∑ pi log2 pi − ∑ pi log2 qi.
  4. Collect the logarithms. ∑ pi log2(pi / qi).
DKL(p ‖ q) = ∑i pi log2 (pi / qi) Read as: the Kullback-Leibler divergence from p to q is the sum, over every outcome i, of how often that outcome really happens times the logarithm of the ratio between reality's probability and the model's. The double bar is conventional and it is not a division: it is there to remind you the two arguments are not interchangeable.

Deriving it by subtraction rather than presenting it as a definition matters. The reader never has to accept a new formula, and the meaning is already fixed: it is the number of extra bits per symbol you pay for believing q when reality is p.

2. It is never negative, and that is a theorem

The claim is Gibbs' inequality. Assume only that p and q are probability distributions over the same finite set of outcomes, so both are non-negative and both sum to one.

The shape of the argument is two steps. Flip the sign so the sum looks like an average of a logarithm. Then use the fact that a logarithm curves the wrong way for the average to survive the move, which is Jensen's inequality, and the constraint that q sums to one finishes it.

Now the proof itself, in nats because the base only scales the answer.

  1. Flip the sign. −DKL(p ‖ q) = ∑i pi ln (qi / pi), summing only over outcomes with pi > 0, which the 0 log 0 = 0 convention allows.
  2. Read it as an average. The pi are weights adding to one, so this is the average of ln(qi/pi) under p.
  3. Apply Jensen. Because ln is concave, the average of the logarithm is at most the logarithm of the average: ∑ pi ln(qi/pi) ≤ ln (∑ pi · qi/pi). This is the step that does the real work. Everything before it is rearranging and everything after it is arithmetic.
  4. Cancel. Inside that logarithm the pi cancel, leaving ∑ qi, which is at most 1.
  5. Finish. ln of something at most 1 is at most 0. So −DKL ≤ 0, which is DKL ≥ 0.

The equality case. The logarithm is strictly concave, so step 3 is an equality only when the quantity inside is the same for every outcome that p gives weight to: qi/pi = c. Summing over that support gives c times 1 on the left and at most 1 on the right, and step 5 is an equality only when ∑ qi = 1 over the same support. Together those force c = 1, so qi = pi everywhere. Zero divergence means identical distributions and nothing weaker.

That proof assumes a finite alphabet. With continuous distributions the same argument runs with integrals and reaches the same conclusion, but "equality" weakens to "equal almost everywhere", and this course does not build the measure theory that makes that phrase precise. The engineering consequence is unchanged: a divergence of zero means you cannot tell the two apart from data.

3. Four reasons it is not a distance

Everyone calls it a distance and it is not one. Four separate failures, each with its own consequence.

It is not symmetric. Swapping the arguments changes the number, as section 4 measures.

It fails the triangle inequality. There is no guarantee that going via a third distribution costs at least as much as going directly, so you cannot reason about chains of divergences the way you reason about chains of distances.

It can be infinite. If q assigns zero to an outcome that p gives positive probability, one term is pi log(pi/0) and the whole sum diverges. A distance between two things that both exist is never infinite.

The units are wrong. It is measured in bits. Nothing about it is a length, and it does not scale like one.

Lesson 185 states the three axioms a metric must satisfy and files these failures against them properly. What matters here is that the word "distance" invites you to expect symmetry, and expecting symmetry is what causes the mistake in section 4.

The symmetric repair, in one line: the Jensen-Shannon divergence averages the two directions against the midpoint distribution, and its square root is a genuine metric. It is bounded too, which is often the more useful property.

4. The direction is a modelling decision

Here is where the asymmetry stops being a curiosity. Suppose reality p has two separated modes and your model q can only be a single Gaussian. You must fit badly. The direction of the KL decides how you fit badly.

Minimising the forward divergence, DKL(p ‖ q), weights the sum by p. Wherever reality has mass, a small q is punished hard, because log(p/q) blows up. The fit is forced to cover everything, so it spreads across both modes and puts most of its density in the valley between them, where reality has almost none. This is mean-seeking.

Minimising the reverse divergence, DKL(q ‖ p), weights by q. Now the model chooses where to be scored. It can avoid the penalty entirely by placing no mass where reality has none, so it retreats onto one mode and ignores the other. This is mode-seeking.

Both behaviours are wanted somewhere. Maximum likelihood is forward KL against the empirical distribution, which is why a badly specified model hedges rather than commits. Variational inference minimises reverse KL, which is why it is known for underestimating posterior variance. And the penalty term in a preference-tuned language model is a KL back to the reference policy, holding the tuned model near where it started: see DPO in LLM Papers.

5. Worked: two directions and one infinity

  1. Take two distributions over three outcomes. p = 0.9, 0.05, 0.05 and q = 0.34, 0.33, 0.33.
  2. Compute the floor. H(p) = 0.5690 bits. Reality is nearly certain, so it is cheap.
  3. Compute the bill. H(p, q) = −(0.9 log2 0.34 + 0.05 log2 0.33 + 0.05 log2 0.33) = 1.5607 bits.
  4. Subtract. DKL(p ‖ q) = 1.5607 − 0.5690 = 0.9917 bits. Believing a nearly flat model when reality is nearly certain costs about one extra bit per symbol.
  5. Swap and repeat. DKL(q ‖ p) = 1.3193 bits. Same two distributions, 33 per cent more expensive in the other direction.
  6. Sanity check. Both must be non-negative, and both must be zero when the arguments match. DKL(p ‖ p) = 0.0000 exactly. If either had come out negative, one of the two vectors does not sum to one.
  7. What changes if the model rules out the third outcome, q = 0.35, 0.35, 0? Then p3 = 0.05 > 0 and q3 = 0, so DKL(p ‖ q) is infinite, while DKL(q ‖ p) is still finite. A single ruled-out outcome destroys one direction and leaves the other intact, which is asymmetry at its most violent.
  8. Read it back. That infinity is not a pathology of the formula. It is the formula telling you the truth: no code built from q can encode an outcome q says is impossible, so the expected cost really is unbounded.

6. Testing the theorem instead of trusting it

Non-negativity is proved in section 2. A proof can still be misread, so the program tries to break it.

smallest = float("inf")
for _ in range(10000):
    a = rng.dirichlet(np.full(k, 0.7))     # two random distributions
    b = rng.dirichlet(np.full(k, 0.7))
    value = kl_from_definition(a, b)
    assert value >= -1e-12, f"KL went negative: {value}"
    smallest = min(smallest, value)
# smallest KL seen over 10,000 random pairs: 0.006997 bits, and none was negative

It also runs the section 4 experiment properly, grid-searching the mean and width of a single Gaussian against a two-mode target under each direction. Forward KL settles on a mean of 0.000 and a width of 3.250; reverse KL settles on a mean of +3.000 and a width of 0.800, which is exactly the width of the mode it chose. No optimiser is involved, so there is nothing to distrust: the program evaluates 19,095 candidates and reports the best.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You are told that KL(p || q) = 0.99 bits. What is KL(q || p)?
Nothing constrains the reverse direction beyond it also being non-negative. The second option is the "KL is a distance" belief stated directly. The third confuses the argument order with the sign flip inside the proof, and would break non-negativity, which is a theorem. The fourth notices correctly that the ratio inverts but forgets that the weights change too, and it is the weights that do most of the work.
You fit one Gaussian to a two-mode target by minimising KL(q || p). What does the fit look like?
Reverse KL weights by the model, so the model avoids being scored where reality is empty and retreats onto a single mode. The first option is exactly what the other direction does, which makes it the strongest distractor: it is a true statement about KL(p || q). The second describes a moment-matching fit, which is what forward KL approaches but not what either direction is defined as. The fourth is a minimax criterion, a different objective altogether.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Two outcomes. Reality is p = 0.5, 0.5. Your model is q = 0.99, 0.01.

Find both divergences in bits. Then set q = 1.0, 0.0 and find them again. Finally, say in one sentence why a language model must never output an exact zero.

Hint

In the first part one of the two terms comes out negative. That is allowed: only the total is constrained to be non-negative, and seeing a negative term is a good check that you have the ratio the right way up.

Show the worked solution
  1. Forward, first term. 0.5 log2(0.5/0.99) = 0.5 × (−0.9856) = −0.4928.
  2. Forward, second term. 0.5 log2(0.5/0.01) = 0.5 × 5.6439 = 2.8219.
  3. Add. DKL(p ‖ q) = 2.3291 bits, despite one term being negative.
  4. Reverse. 0.99 log2(0.99/0.5) + 0.01 log2(0.01/0.5) = 0.9757 − 0.0564 = 0.9193 bits. A factor of 2.53 between the two directions.
  5. Now the zero. With q = 1.0, 0.0: the second outcome has p = 0.5 > 0 and q = 0, so the forward divergence is infinite.
  6. And the other way. DKL(q ‖ p) = 1 × log2(1/0.5) = 1 bit, perfectly finite, because the outcome q rules out is one q never asks about.
  7. The sentence. A token the model assigns exactly zero makes the forward divergence, and therefore the cross-entropy loss, infinite the instant that token occurs.

Sanity check. Both answers in the first part must be positive even though individual terms are not. If a total came out negative, check that both vectors sum to exactly 1: that constraint is the only thing standing between you and a negative divergence, and it is precisely what step 4 of the proof uses.

Your teacher. If the mean-seeking and mode-seeking split did not land, ask an agent to plot a fixed two-mode target and then sweep a single Gaussian's mean from −4 to +4 at a fixed width, printing both divergences at each step. Watching which curve has its minimum in the middle and which has two minima at the sides makes the asymmetry a thing you saw rather than a thing you were told.
