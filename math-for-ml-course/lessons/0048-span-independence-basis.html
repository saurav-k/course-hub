Span, independence, and basis: how much space a set of vectors covers

working 9 min A set of vectors reaches exactly its linear combinations, and adding a vector you could already reach adds nothing.

The one-minute version

  • The span of a set of vectors is everything you can build from them by scaling and adding.
  • A x = b has a solution exactly when b lies in the span of the columns of A, which turns lesson 47's three outcomes into a picture.
  • A set is linearly dependent when one member is already a combination of the others, and such a member enlarges nothing.
  • An independent set of n-vectors has at most n members, so any table with more features than samples has redundant columns before you look at it.

Span: the set of everything reachable

Take one non-zero vector and scale it by every real number. You sweep out a line through the origin, and nothing else. Take a second vector that is not on that line, and combinations of the two sweep out a plane. Take a third off that plane, and in three dimensions you now reach everything.

That progression is the whole idea. The span grows by one dimension each time you add a vector that was not already reachable, and does not grow at all when you add one that was.

The column space, and why lesson 47 had three answers

Lesson 44 said that Ax is a linear combination of the columns of A, weighted by the entries of x. Put that beside the definition of span and the whole of lesson 47 falls out.

The reframing Ax = b has a solution exactly when b lies in the span of the columns of A. That span has a name: the column space. "No solution" means b was outside it, and the whole of lesson 51 is about what to do then.

Dependence, and a ceiling on how many can be independent

A set is linearly dependent when at least one member can be written as a combination of the others, and linearly independent when none can. The definition is about the set, not about any one vector: dependence is a property of the company they keep.

There is a hard ceiling, and Boyd and Vandenberghe call it the independence-dimension inequality: a linearly independent collection of n-vectors has at most n members. Equivalently, any collection of n + 1 or more n-vectors is dependent. Three vectors in the plane always have a dependency, no matter which three.

Basis, and why coordinates are a choice

A basis is a set of n independent n-vectors, which by the inequality above is as many as you can have. Every vector is then reachable, and reachable in exactly one way.

The expansion in a basis is unique

What is assumed. a1 to ak are linearly independent, and a vector x has been written as a combination of them in two ways, with coefficients βi and γi.

The shape of the argument. Subtract the two expressions. What is left is a combination of independent vectors that equals zero, and independence says the only such combination is the one with every coefficient zero.

The step that does the real work is that subtraction, because it converts a statement about two representations into a statement about one combination being zero.

0 = x − x = (β1 − γ1)a1 + … + (βk − γk)ak Read as: 0 is the zero vector. Each βi is the coefficient on ai in the first expansion and each γi the coefficient on the same vector in the second. Subtracting the two expansions collects each vector's two coefficients into one difference.

The vectors are independent, so no combination of them equals zero unless every coefficient is zero. Therefore βi − γi = 0 for every i, which is to say the two expansions were the same expansion all along. ■

Uniqueness is what makes coordinates meaningful. The list of coefficients in a chosen basis is the vector's address, and it changes when the basis changes even though the point does not move. That is what every learned representation in machine learning is doing: choosing a basis in which the addresses are more useful.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. a1 = (2, 3, 1) and a2 = (1, 4, 2), two vectors in three-dimensional space, taken from the opening of Strang's textbook.
  2. Build combinations. a1 + a2 = (3, 7, 3) and 2a1 + a2 = (5, 10, 4).
  3. Ask what they span. Neither is a multiple of the other, so they are independent and their span is a plane through the origin. It is not all of three-dimensional space.
  4. Test a vector that is in it. Is (5, 10, 4) reachable? Step 2 already built it with coefficients (2, 1), so yes.
  5. Test one that is not. Is (1, 1, 1) reachable? Solving for coefficients gives (0.3684, 0.0526), and those rebuild (0.7895, 1.3158, 0.4737), not (1, 1, 1). Not in the span.
  6. Check that answer a second way. The rank of the two columns is 2. Appending (5, 10, 4) leaves it at 2, so nothing was added. Appending (1, 1, 1) raises it to 3, so that vector reached somewhere new. Both tests agree on both vectors.
  7. Complete a basis. Adding (0, 0, 1) gives three independent vectors, so every point in three-dimensional space now has exactly one address. The point (7, 11, 5) has coordinates (3.4, 0.2, 1.2) in that basis, which two different solvers agree on.
  8. Say what would change. Adding a fourth vector cannot make the basis better. By the independence-dimension inequality the four would be dependent, and 5,000 random attempts to find four independent vectors in three dimensions produced not one.

Sanity check. In step 7 the coordinates must rebuild the original point exactly: 3.4a1 + 0.2a2 + 1.2(0, 0, 1) should give (7, 11, 5) and not merely something close. If it does not, the three vectors were not independent and the "basis" was not one.

What it means. On the sensor table, the eight columns have rank 8, so every sensor is genuinely new. Appending vibration_x a second time in different units gives nine columns and still rank 8: the ninth was already in the span of the first eight, so it enlarged nothing. Lesson 49 is what that costs.

code/0048-span-independence-basis.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

def in_span(columns, b):
    """b is reachable exactly when appending it adds no rank."""
    return np.linalg.matrix_rank(columns) == np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.column_stack([columns, b]))

a1, a2 = np.array([2., 3., 1.]), np.array([1., 4., 2.])
A = np.column_stack([a1, a2])
print(in_span(A, 2 * a1 + a2))          # True
print(in_span(A, np.array([1., 1., 1.])))   # False

The full program checks the rank test against the solve test on both vectors, draws 5,000 sets of four vectors in three dimensions and confirms none is independent, and repeats the span question on the sensor columns.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You have five vectors in three-dimensional space. What can you say for certain?
An independent set of three-dimensional vectors has at most three members, so five of them always contain a dependency. The first is not forced: all five could lie in one plane and span only that. The second says nothing about length, which nothing here constrains. The fourth needs exactly three independent vectors, and five is too many to be a basis.
Ax = b has a solution exactly when which condition holds?
A x is a combination of the columns, so the reachable set is exactly their span, and b has to be in it. The second names a space that usually lives in a different dimension from b when A is not square. The third and fourth are facts about shape, and neither settles solvability on its own: a square matrix with dependent columns can easily miss b.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Take a1 = (2, 3, 1) and a2 = (1, 4, 2).

  1. Compute a1 + a2 and 2a1 + a2.
  2. Is (5, 10, 4) in their span? Give the coefficients or show that none exist.
  3. Is (1, 1, 1) in their span? Answer by naming what the span is before computing anything.
  4. How many further independent vectors could you add to the set before it must become dependent?
Hint

Part 2 needs no algebra at all if you look carefully at what you computed in part 1.

Hint for part 3

Two independent vectors in three dimensions span a plane through the origin, and a plane is a thin thing to land on by accident. To settle it, try to solve for the coefficients and see whether the third equation agrees with the first two.

Show the worked solution
  1. Add them. a1 + a2 = (2+1, 3+4, 1+2) = (3, 7, 3).
  2. Scale and add. 2a1 = (4, 6, 2), so 2a1 + a2 = (5, 10, 4).
  3. Read part 2 off part 1. (5, 10, 4) is exactly what step 2 built, so it is in the span, with coefficients (2, 1).
  4. Name the span. The two vectors are independent, so their span is a plane through the origin in three-dimensional space, not the whole space.
  5. Try to solve for (1, 1, 1). From the first two rows: 2c1 + c2 = 1 and 3c1 + 4c2 = 1. Solving gives c1 = 0.6 and c2 = −0.2.
  6. Test the third row. It needs c1 + 2c2 = 1, and 0.6 + 2(−0.2) = 0.2, not 1. So (1, 1, 1) is not in the span.
  7. Count the room left. One. A third independent vector makes a basis of three-dimensional space, and any fourth vector is then forced to be dependent.

Sanity check. Step 5 solved two equations for two unknowns, which always has an answer when the two are independent, so getting coefficients there proves nothing on its own. The test is step 6: the third equation is the one that has to agree, and it is the reason the answer is no.

Your teacher. If the difference between "I found coefficients" and "the vector is in the span" is still slippery, ask an agent to give you several three-dimensional targets and to check each one both ways, by least squares and by the rank test. The cases where a least-squares answer exists but does not reproduce the target are exactly the cases outside the span, and seeing four or five of them makes the distinction stick.
