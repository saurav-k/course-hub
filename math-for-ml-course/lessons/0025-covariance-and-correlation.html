Covariance and correlation, and why the units do not matter

foundation 11 min Two columns, one number, and a derivation rather than a definition to memorise.

The one-minute version

  • Covariance averages the product of the two deviations: when one column is above its mean, is the other?
  • It carries both columns' units multiplied together, so its size means nothing until you are told them. Seconds to minutes changes it by a factor of 60.
  • Divide by both standard deviations and the units cancel. That cancellation is the whole derivation of the correlation coefficient.
  • Correlation measures linear association only. A perfect parabola scores zero, measured.

1. One question, asked row by row

Take two columns measured on the same rows. For each row, ask two things: is the first column above its own mean, and is the second above its own? Multiply the two answers as signed distances.

If the two tend to be above together and below together, every product is positive and the average is positive. If one is high when the other is low, the products are negative. If there is no pattern, the positives and negatives cancel and the average sits near zero. That average is the covariance, and it was built before it was named.

cov(x, y) = (1/n) ∑i (xi − x-bar)(yi − y-bar) n: the number of rows, the same for both columns because they are measured on the same rows. xi, yi: the two values in row i. x-bar, y-bar: each column's own mean. (xi − x-bar): the deviation, how far this row sits above or below its column's centre, keeping its sign. The product: positive when the two deviations share a sign and negative when they do not. The average: one number summarising all n agreements and disagreements.

2. The problem: covariance has units

Look at what the product is measured in. A deviation in session_seconds is in seconds; a deviation in spend is in pounds. Their product is in second-pounds, a unit that means nothing to anyone, and the average of them inherits it.

So a covariance cannot be read on its own, and two covariances cannot be compared unless they happen to share both units. On sessions.csv:

A covariance of 0.4 is not "weak" and a covariance of 4,000 is not "strong". Nothing in the number tells you which, because the scale came from the units. The first three rows above are the same data three times. Anyone who reads a covariance as a strength has read a units choice.

Notice the fourth row too: adding a constant to either column changes nothing at all, because a shift moves the mean by the same amount and leaves every deviation exactly where it was.

3. The fix, and why it is a derivation

We want a number that survives a change of units. The units of the covariance are the units of x times the units of y. The standard deviation of x is in the units of x, and the standard deviation of y is in the units of y. So dividing by both cancels exactly what we want cancelled.

r = cov(x, y) / ( sx sy ) r: the correlation coefficient, a plain number with no units at all. cov(x, y): the covariance from section 1, carrying both columns' units. sx: the standard deviation of the first column, in that column's units. sy: the same for the second. The division: the units on top are exactly the units on the bottom, so they cancel and what is left is a pure ratio.
Stated proof Correlation is unit-free. Rescale a column by a positive constant a. Every deviation scales by a, so the covariance scales by a. That column's standard deviation also scales by a. In the ratio the two factors of a cancel, so r is unchanged.

The step that does the work is that the deviation, not the value, enters both the numerator and the denominator: that is why a shift changes nothing and a scale cancels. The boundary: the constant must be positive. A negative one flips the sign of the covariance and therefore of r, which is correct behaviour rather than a failure of the argument, because reversing a column really does reverse the direction of the association.

The measured table in section 2 is that proof as a fact: the covariance column moves by exactly the product of the two scale factors each time, and the correlation column does not move at all.

4. What the two ends mean

The coefficient lies between −1 and +1. Both ends mean the same geometric thing: every point sits exactly on one straight line, rising for +1 and falling for −1. The program checks it directly, correlating a column with an exact linear function of itself and getting 1.00000000.

5. Two columns, two pictures, one scale

6. Rank correlation, in two sentences

Replace every value by its rank within its column and apply the same formula, and you have measured monotone association instead of linear: whether the two move together in the same direction, regardless of whether the relationship is a straight line. That buys robustness to outliers and to the skew of Lesson 24, and it costs you the interpretation of the slope.

7. Worked: six pairs, by hand, then rescaled

x = (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12)     y = (1, 3, 2, 5, 4, 6) Read as: six rows, two columns. Row 1 has x = 2 and y = 1, row 2 has x = 4 and y = 3, and so on. The columns are two lists of numbers measured on the same six rows.
  1. Means. x: 42 ÷ 6 = 7. y: 21 ÷ 6 = 3.5.
  2. Deviations for x. −5, −3, −1, 1, 3, 5.
  3. Deviations for y. −2.5, −0.5, −1.5, 1.5, 0.5, 2.5.
  4. Products. 12.5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5, 12.5, summing to 31.
  5. Covariance. 31 ÷ 6 = 5.1667.
  6. Standard deviations. For x, squared deviations sum to 70, so sx = √(70/6) = 3.4157. For y they sum to 17.5, so sy = √(17.5/6) = 1.7078.
  7. Correlation. 5.1667 ÷ (3.4157 × 1.7078) = 5.1667 ÷ 5.8333 = 0.8857.
  8. Now multiply every x by 100 and redo it. Every x-deviation multiplies by 100, so the covariance becomes 516.67 and sx becomes 341.57. The ratio is 516.67 ÷ (341.57 × 1.7078) = 0.8857, unchanged.

Sanity check. The correlation must lie between −1 and +1, and 0.8857 does. It should also be high but not 1, because y rises with x overall while wobbling: rows 2 and 3 go down when x goes up. If you got exactly 1, a deviation lost its sign.

What changes if you multiply x by −100 instead? The covariance flips sign to −516.67 and sx stays positive at 341.57, so r becomes −0.8857. That is the boundary the stated proof named, and it is the right answer: reversing a column really does reverse the direction.

What it means. The covariance moved by a factor of 100 and told you nothing new. The correlation did not move at all, and that is the entire reason it is the number people quote.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two columns have a correlation of 0. What does that establish?
Correlation measures linear association only, and the program scores a perfect parabola at 0.000000. The first is the strongest distractor because independence really does imply zero correlation - it is a true statement about a stronger property, answering a different question, and the implication does not run backwards. The second would give an undefined correlation, not a zero one, since a constant column has zero standard deviation. The fourth is the same error as the first without the vocabulary.
You convert a column from seconds to minutes. What changes?
The measured table shows it: the covariance moves from 2222.69 to 37.04 and the correlation stays at 0.486811 to six decimal places. The second has it backwards, and standardised is exactly the word for why it does not move. The third confuses changing units with changing data, when no row's actual duration changed. The fourth calls a division a shift, and shifts are the operation that changes neither.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Six paired readings:

x = (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12)     y = (1, 3, 2, 5, 4, 6) Read as: six rows with two measurements each. The pairing matters: row 3 has x = 6 and y = 2, and reordering either column on its own would change the answer.

Compute the covariance and the correlation. Then multiply every x by 100 and report both again.

Hint

Build the two deviation columns first and keep them beside each other. Every quantity on this page is made from those two columns, so once you have them the rest is multiplication and addition.

Hint for the rescale

Do not recompute from scratch. Ask what happens to each of the three ingredients - the covariance, sx and sy - when every x is multiplied by 100, and then look at where those factors end up in the ratio.

Show the worked solution
  1. Means. x-bar = 42 ÷ 6 = 7. y-bar = 21 ÷ 6 = 3.5.
  2. Deviations. x: −5, −3, −1, 1, 3, 5. y: −2.5, −0.5, −1.5, 1.5, 0.5, 2.5.
  3. Products, row by row. 12.5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5, 1.5, 12.5. Sum = 31.
  4. Covariance. 31 ÷ 6 = 5.1667.
  5. The two spreads. Squared x-deviations sum to 70, so sx = √(70/6) = 3.4157. Squared y-deviations sum to 17.5, so sy = √(17.5/6) = 1.7078.
  6. Correlation. 5.1667 ÷ 5.8333 = 0.8857.
  7. After multiplying x by 100. Covariance = 516.67, exactly 100 times larger. sx = 341.57, also exactly 100 times larger. sy unchanged.
  8. The ratio. 516.67 ÷ (341.57 × 1.7078) = 0.8857. The factor of 100 appears once on top and once underneath, and cancels.

Sanity check. The correlation must lie in [−1, +1] and must be identical before and after the rescale. If it moved, you rescaled the covariance and forgot to rescale sx alongside it, which is the one slip this problem is built to catch. Check also that your covariance moved by exactly 100 and not by 10,000: only one of the two columns was rescaled, so only one factor enters.

Your teacher. If the unit-cancellation is the part to keep, take any two columns at work, compute the covariance, change one column's units, and compute it again. Watching a number you were about to quote move by a factor of sixty is more persuasive than the table. If you want the parabola result in your hands, ask an agent: "correlate t with t squared for t from -10 to 10 and tell me the answer", and then ask it why.
