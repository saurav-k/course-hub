Early stopping is the regularizer you did not write

working 11 min The capacity argument is Goodfellow et al. chapter 7, section 7.8. · Deep Learning, ch. 7

The one-minute version

  • τ steps at rate η can only travel so far from where you started. That is a constraint region, and the previous page proved a constraint region is a penalty.
  • So early stopping regularises, and the mechanism is the step budget rather than anything added to the loss.
  • Early stopping and L2 are the same kind of regularizer, both shrinking in the Hessian eigenbasis. Measured on real data, no single L2 coefficient reproduces it.
  • The tidy formula τη ≈ 1/α needs a regime that real well-conditioned problems never occupy. Its conditions are the result.

1. The observation, before the explanation

Train long enough on a model with capacity to spare and two curves separate. The training loss falls and keeps falling. The validation loss falls, reaches a minimum, and turns up. Stopping at the turn is early stopping, and it is the single most used regularizer in machine learning.

2. Why a step budget is a constraint

Here is the mechanism, and it needs no assumptions beyond a bounded gradient.

  1. Write the total displacement. After τ steps, θτ − θ0 is the sum of τ individual steps, each of which is −η times a gradient.
  2. Take the norm and apply the triangle inequality. The norm of a sum is at most the sum of the norms.
  3. Bound each term. If every gradient has norm at most G, each step has norm at most ηG.
  4. Collect. ‖θτ − θ0‖ ≤ τηG.
  5. Read what that is. The reachable set is a ball of radius τηG around the initialisation. That is a constraint region, and Lesson 110 proved a constraint region is a penalty.

So τη is a measure of effective capacity: a small budget confines you near the start, and a large one lets you reach further. Nothing was added to the objective, and the model was regularised anyway.

3. Is it the same regularizer as L2, or the same kind?

This is where most accounts overclaim, so here is the exact statement and then the measurement.

Under a quadratic approximation with Hessian H, plain gradient descent from θ0 = 0 shrinks the component along eigenvector i by a factor 1 − (1 − ηλi)τ. The previous page showed L2 shrinks the same component by λi/(λi + α). Both are shrinkage in the same basis, and both spend their budget on low-curvature directions. That much is real and it is the mechanism.

They coincide exactly when (1 − ηλi)τ = α/(λi + α), for every i at once. Taking logs and expanding, using log(1 + x) ≈ x on both sides, gives the familiar

τ ≈ 1/(ηα),    equivalently    α ≈ 1/(τη) τ: the number of steps taken before stopping. η: the learning rate. α: the weight-decay coefficient this is claimed to be equivalent to. ≈: an approximation, and the conditions it needs are stated below rather than assumed.
That approximation needs ηλi << 1 and λi/α << 1, for every i. The second condition says the eigenvalues must be small relative to α, which is the opposite of the natural misreading. It is easy to get backwards, and getting it backwards is not a small error: with the condition inverted the approximation is wrong by a factor of 145.

4. Measured: the regime is unreachable

On the credit data's least-squares Hessian, the eigenvalues run from 0.078 to 2.078, and η = 1/λmax = 0.481. Now watch the two demands fight.

The condition needs α to dominate every eigenvalue, but α is predicted as 1/(τη), which shrinks as τ grows. Every row fails, and the first row already fails: the regime the approximation needs would require stopping before the first step.

And the per-direction check confirms it. At τ = 20 the L2 coefficient that would reproduce early stopping in each eigendirection separately ranges from 6.8 × 10-2 down to essentially zero. No single scalar α reproduces this early-stopped model at all.

The honest statement Early stopping and L2 are the same kind of regularizer: both shrink in the Hessian eigenbasis, both spend their budget on the directions the data does not constrain. They are not the same regularizer, and on a well-conditioned problem no L2 coefficient reproduces a given stopping step. The mechanism transfers; the conversion does not.

Nothing here weakens the page. Section 2's bound holds unconditionally and is why early stopping regularises at all. Only the tidy reciprocal needs a regime real problems rarely occupy, and a page that states its conditions has already told you that.

5. Worked: patience, and the trap in it

A validation loss by epoch, and three patience settings applied to it.

0.90, 0.72, 0.61, 0.55, 0.52, 0.53, 0.51, 0.54, 0.55, 0.56, 0.58 Read as: eleven epochs of validation loss, one number each. It falls to 0.52 at epoch 5, bumps up once, reaches its true minimum of 0.51 at epoch 7, and rises from there. The bump at epoch 6 is the whole point of the exercise.
  1. Track two things only. The best value so far, and how many epochs since it improved.
  2. Epoch 5 sets 0.52. Best so far, counter resets to zero.
  3. Epoch 6 gives 0.53. No improvement, counter is 1.
  4. Epoch 7 gives 0.51. A new best, so the counter resets to zero again.
  5. Epochs 8 and 9 fail twice. Counter reaches 2.
  6. Patience 2 fires at epoch 9, and restores epoch 7, two epochs back.
  7. Patience 5 never fires within this data: the counter only reaches 4 by epoch 11.
  8. Patience 1 fires at epoch 6, on the single bad step, and restores epoch 5 at 0.52 - missing the genuinely better epoch 7.

Sanity check. The restored epoch is never later than the stopping epoch, and when the run fires the gap between them is exactly the patience value. If your gap is smaller, you reset the counter on an epoch that merely tied rather than improved.

What changes with a noisier curve? Every bump becomes a candidate trigger, so the patience needed to survive it rises. Patience is buying tolerance for noise, and its right value depends on how noisy your validation estimate is, which depends on how much validation data you set aside.

What it means. Patience 1 cost 0.01 of validation loss for nothing. Patience is itself a hyperparameter fitted on the validation set, which is the cost the usual account leaves out.

6. What it costs

Three costs the usual account omits. It spends validation data, which is data not used for fitting. The stopping point is itself a parameter fitted on that data, so a validation loss reported at the stopping epoch is optimistic. And it couples to the learning-rate schedule from Lesson 103: the capacity measure is the product τη, so halving one is not the same as halving the other once η is no longer constant.

Against those, it is the cheapest regularizer available. It needs one evaluation loop, no extra hyperparameter in the objective, and it adapts to the problem without a search. That is why it is in essentially every training script.

code/0111-early-stopping.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def patience_run(losses, patience):
    """Return (stop_epoch, restored_epoch), stop None if it never fires."""
    best, best_epoch, waited = np.inf, None, 0
    for epoch, value in enumerate(losses, start=1):
        if value < best:
            best, best_epoch, waited = value, epoch, 0
        else:
            waited += 1
            if waited >= patience:
                return epoch, best_epoch
    return None, best_epoch

Note that it returns two epochs rather than one. The program also verifies section 3's shrinkage formula two ways, by running gradient descent and projecting the result into the eigenbasis and by evaluating the closed form, agreeing to 8 × 10-15. Then it prints the per-direction table that shows no single α can match.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Early stopping behaves like L2 regularization because
τ steps at rate η can reach only so far, which is a constraint region, and the previous lesson showed a constraint region is a penalty. The first two both invent a penalty term that early stopping never adds, which is the whole point of calling it the regularizer you did not write. The third describes a selection rule nobody runs and which would need every checkpoint's norm computed.
When early stopping fires after the patience window, which weights do you keep?
You restore the checkpoint that set the best validation value, which is patience epochs behind where you stopped. The first is the off-by-patience bug and the commonest one in hand-rolled loops. The third is weight averaging, a real but different technique. The fourth is the subtle one: with a noisy curve the best epoch and the epoch before the first uptick are often the same and are not guaranteed to be, as the worked example shows at epochs 5 and 7.

Hint

Keep exactly two counters: the best value so far, and the number of epochs since it last changed. Reset the second only when the first genuinely improves, and not for an epoch that merely ties.

Show the worked solution
  1. Epochs 1 to 5 all improve. The best is 0.52 at epoch 5, counter 0.
  2. Epoch 6 is 0.53. No improvement, counter 1.
  3. Epoch 7 is 0.51. A new best, so counter resets to 0 and this checkpoint is saved.
  4. Epoch 8 is 0.54, epoch 9 is 0.55. Counter reaches 2.
  5. Patience 2: stops at epoch 9, restores epoch 7 at 0.51.
  6. Patience 5: the counter runs 1, 2, 3, 4 across epochs 8 to 11 and never reaches 5, so it never fires in this data. The best is still epoch 7.
  7. Patience 1: epoch 6's single bad step trips it immediately. It stops at epoch 6 and restores epoch 5 at 0.52.
  8. The cost. 0.01 of validation loss, lost by refusing to tolerate one noisy epoch. Epoch 7 was a genuine improvement and patience 1 never saw it.

Sanity check. The restored epoch must never be later than the stopping epoch, and whenever the run fires the gap between them must equal the patience exactly. Check patience 2: stopped at 9, restored 7, gap 2. If your gap is smaller you have reset the counter on an epoch that did not improve, and if it is larger you have failed to reset on one that did.

Your teacher. If the off-by-patience bug is the part to keep, look at your own training code: if it saves weights only at the end of the loop, it is keeping the wrong model every time early stopping fires. If the section 4 measurement bothered you, it should: it is the second place in this module where a widely quoted approximation turned out to need conditions that real problems do not meet, and checking the conditions is the habit rather than the result.
