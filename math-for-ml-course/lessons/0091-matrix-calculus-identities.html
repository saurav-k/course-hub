Six matrix-calculus identities, and the layout convention that breaks them

frontier 13 min The last page of the module, and the one that makes every derivation in Modules 06 and 09 short.

The one-minute version

  • Differentiating a loss index by index takes a page. In matrix form it takes three lines and two identities.
  • Two textbooks give different shapes for the same derivative and neither has a typo. They are in different layout conventions.
  • This course holds one rule everywhere: a gradient has the shape of the thing you differentiate by. Five of the six identities are just that rule.
  • The payoff is the normal equations, which Module 03 already reached by dropping a perpendicular. Same equation, disjoint routes, and on the sensor table they agree to fourteen decimals.

1. Why bother writing it in matrix form

Take the squared error of a linear model. Written with index sums it is ∑i(yi − ∑jXijθj)², and differentiating it means chasing two indices, renaming a summation variable, and collecting terms. Written as ||y − Xθ||² it is one expression, and the whole derivative is two lookups.

The catch is that a lookup table is only safe if you know what shape the answer should be. That is what the rest of this page is about, and it is why the layout question comes before the identities rather than after them.

2. Two textbooks, two shapes, no typo

The house rule, held everywhere in this course A gradient has the shape of the thing you differentiate by. Differentiate a scalar by a column and you get a column; differentiate it by an n × m matrix and you get an n × m matrix. Lesson 83 declared this rule. This is the page where it earns its keep.

3. The six identities, each with its derivation

Throughout, x is a column in Rn, a a constant column, A a constant matrix, and the house rule applies.

  1. ∂(aTx)/∂x = a. Since aTx = ∑kakxk, differentiating in xi keeps the single term with k = i and gives ai. Stacking over i gives a.
  2. ∂(xTAx)/∂x = (A + AT)x, and 2Ax when A is symmetric. Here xTAx = ∑j,kAjkxjxk. Differentiating in xi, the terms with j = i give ∑kAikxk and those with k = i give ∑jAjixj. The single term with j = k = i is Aiixi², whose derivative 2Aiixi is exactly the sum of the two contributions, so nothing is double counted. Stacking gives Ax + ATx.
  3. ∂(||x||²)/∂x = 2x. Identity 2 with A = I.
  4. ∂(Ax)/∂x = A. This one is a Jacobian, not a gradient, because the output is a vector. Entry (i, j) is ∂(∑kAikxk)/∂xj = Aij, so the Jacobian is A itself, shape m × n, exactly as Lesson 85's shape rule says.
  5. ∂(||y − Xb||²)/∂b = −2XT(y − Xb). Expand to yTy − 2yTXb + bTXTXb. The first term is constant. The second is −2(XTy)Tb, so identity 1 gives −2XTy. The third has the symmetric XTX, so identity 2 gives 2XTXb. Sum and factor.
  6. ∂ tr(AX)/∂X = AT. Since tr(AX) = ∑i,kAikXki, differentiating in Xpq keeps the single term with k = p and i = q, giving Aqp. So entry (p, q) of the derivative is Aqp, which is AT.

4. The payoff, in three lines

L(θ) = ||y − Xθ||² y: the targets, a column with one entry per sample, length n. X: the design matrix, n rows by p columns, one row per sample and one column per parameter, per this course's convention. θ: the parameters, a column of length p. L: one number, the total squared error.
  1. Expand. L = yTy − 2yTXθ + θTXTXθ.
  2. Differentiate term by term. The first is constant. Identity 1 turns the second into −2XTy. Identity 2, with the symmetric XTX, turns the third into 2XTXθ.
  3. Set to zero. −2XTy + 2XTXθ = 0, that is XTXθ = XTy.
  4. Check it is a minimum. The Hessian is 2XTX by identity 2, and hTXTXh = ||Xh||² > 0 for every non-zero h when X has full column rank. So the loss is strictly convex and the single critical point is the global minimum, by Lesson 88's second theorem.
  5. Solve on the real table. Seven parameters on 12,000 sensor readings: intercept 35.96331766, vibration_x 0.37622718, vibration_y 0.44567939, current_amp 0.75434178, humidity_pct 0.30747471, dust_index 0.37424901 and pressure_kpa 0.01949510.
  6. Solve it again the other way and compare. The two routes agree to between 9.60 × 10−15 and 4.70 × 10−12 on every coefficient.

5. The same equation, without a derivative anywhere

Sanity check. XTX must be symmetric, and its determinant must be non-zero for the solve to be unique. Both hold here. And the residual really is perpendicular to every column: max |XTr| = 1.219 × 10−8, which is the geometric fact the calculus route never mentions and never needed.

What changes if you switch to numerator layout at step 2? Every gradient becomes a row, both identities transpose, and step 3 reads θTXTX = yTX, which is the same equation transposed. That is Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong's equation 9.12a exactly. Nothing about the answer changes and only the shape of every intermediate does, which is the page's whole argument about conventions made once, on a result the reader already trusts.

6. A lookup table is only safe if the shapes are checked

The program behind this page checks all six identities against a finite-difference derivative before using any of them, and they agree to between 1.098 × 10−10 and 2.453 × 10−9. That is the habit worth taking away, because the transposed-gradient bug does not raise an error: it produces a matrix of the wrong shape that a broadcasting library will often multiply anyway.

Where the rest of ridge belongs is worth saying plainly. The geometry of L2 regularisation, the ball and the contour, is Module 06's. The statistics, L2 as a Gaussian prior under MAP, is Module 09's. This page owns only the differentiation whose by-product happens to be the ridge estimator.

code/0091-matrix-calculus-identities.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
# Every identity is checked against a central difference before it is trusted,
# and the shape is printed beside the gap. The transposed-gradient bug does not
# raise: it makes a wrongly shaped array that a broadcasting library will often
# happily multiply anyway, so the shape is part of the check and not a comment.
def report(name: str, analytic: np.ndarray, numeric: np.ndarray) -> None:
    gap = float(np.abs(analytic - numeric).max())
    shape = "x".join(str(d) for d in analytic.shape)
    print(f"  {name:<34} shape {shape:<8} largest gap {gap:.3e}")

report(
    "5. d(||y - Xb||^2)/db",
    -2.0 * x_mat.T @ (y_vec - x_mat @ b),
    numeric_gradient(lambda v: float((y_vec - x_mat @ v) @ (y_vec - x_mat @ v)), b.copy()),
)

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two textbooks give different shapes for ∂(aTx)/∂x. What follows?
Numerator layout makes a scalar-by-vector derivative a row and denominator layout makes it a column, and each is internally consistent. The first is the trap and is what most readers conclude, which is why the page puts two named books on the two branches. The third confuses the operand with the layout: a is a column in both books and the answers still differ. The fourth is advice rather than an explanation, and it is precisely how the bug survives into production.
Module 03 derives XTXθ = XTy from perpendicularity and this page derives it from a gradient. What is their relationship?
Two disjoint arguments landing on the identical equation is what the figure and the fourteen-decimal agreement show. The first is false: both routes need full column rank, one for a unique projection and one for a positive definite Hessian. The second is simply false, and centring changes only the intercept. The fourth is a true-sounding claim about a different question, since it is the projection argument that generalises, holding in any inner-product space.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Add an L2 penalty to the squared error and differentiate the whole thing.

L(θ) = ||y − Xθ||² + λ||θ||² λ: a fixed non-negative number chosen before fitting, not a parameter. ||θ||²: the squared length of the coefficient vector, which is θTIθ. Everything else is as in section 4.

Differentiate with respect to θ, set it to zero, solve, and then say in one sentence what λ does to the Hessian.

Hint

Exactly two identities, plus the observation that ||θ||² = θTIθ so identity 2 applies to it with a symmetric matrix. No new machinery.

Hint for the last part

You do not need eigenvalues to answer it. Ask what adding a multiple of the identity does to every eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix, then ask which eigenvalue changes most as a proportion of itself.

Show the worked solution
  1. Differentiate the fit term. Identity 5 gives −2XTy + 2XTXθ.
  2. Differentiate the penalty. Identity 3 gives 2λθ.
  3. Set to zero and collect. (XTX + λI)θ = XTy, so θ = (XTX + λI)−1XTy.
  4. Read the Hessian. It is 2(XTX + λI), so every eigenvalue rises by exactly 2λ.
  5. Say which one that helps. The smallest, because the same absolute rise is the largest relative rise there. On the sensor table κ falls from 35,382,029 at λ = 0 to 118,658 at λ = 10,000 while ||θ|| falls from 35.98 to 0.39.

Sanity check. At λ = 0 the answer must collapse to the ordinary normal equations, and it does. As λ grows without bound θ must tend to zero, and it does. Either limit failing means a sign is wrong somewhere in step 3.

A slip in a source this course otherwise leans onDeisenroth, Faisal and Ong define the negative log-likelihood in equation 9.10b with the factor 1/(2σ²) in front, and then remark that its Hessian is XTX. Differentiating their own expression twice gives XTX/σ², so the factor has been dropped. It changes nothing about the argument, because positive definiteness is unaffected by a positive scale, and it changes everything about a step size, which is 1/λmax and therefore scales with σ². Read a Hessian back off the loss you actually wrote down.
Your teacher. The habit this page is really teaching is the shape check. Ask an agent to differentiate a loss you use, then to print the shape of every intermediate beside the shape it should have, and to do the whole derivation again in the other layout convention so you can see that only the shapes move.
