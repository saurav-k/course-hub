A matrix is a function: rotate, scale, shear, and collapse

working 10 min A weight matrix is not a table of numbers that happens to be square. It is a function on space, and that is why depth composes.

The one-minute version

  • y = A x is a map from one space to another, and every matrix is one.
  • Column j is the image of the j-th basis vector. That single rule is how you find the matrix for any transformation you can describe.
  • Composing two maps is multiplying their matrices, in the order they are applied, right to left.
  • The determinant is the factor by which area is scaled, and a determinant of zero means the map flattened space and nothing can undo it.

The rule that finds every matrix

Read y = Ax as input to output. Feeding in the first basis vector, the one with a 1 in position one and zeros elsewhere, picks out exactly the first column of A and nothing else. The same happens for every position.

Aej = column j of A Read as: ej is the j-th standard basis vector, a list with a single 1 in position j and zeros everywhere else. Multiplying A by it selects one column, because the column reading of lesson 44 weights the columns by the entries of the input and every weight here is zero but one.

So to build the matrix for a transformation you can describe in words, apply it to each basis vector and write down where each one lands. Rotating (1, 0) clockwise by 90 degrees gives (0, −1) and rotating (0, 1) gives (1, 0), so the matrix is [[0, 1], [−1, 0]]. No algebra was needed.

Four maps, and what each preserves

A rotation turns everything about the origin by a fixed angle. A dilation stretches along the axes by separate factors. A shear slides one axis along another. A collapse flattens the whole plane onto a line, and it is the interesting one.

The determinant is the area factor

Every matrix above carried a determinant, and the number was not decoration. In two dimensions the determinant of [[a, b], [c, d]] is ad − bc, and its size is the factor by which the map multiplies area.

Why the determinant is the area of the image of the unit square

What is assumed. A is a 2 by 2 matrix with columns u = (a, c) and v = (b, d). The unit square has corners at the origin, e1, e1 + e2 and e2.

The shape of the argument. The map sends the corners of the unit square to the origin, u, u + v and v, so the image is the parallelogram spanned by the two columns. Then compute that parallelogram's area directly and find it is |ad − bc|.

The step that does the real work is the second one, and it is a rectangle minus four offcuts. Enclose the parallelogram in the axis-aligned rectangle of width a + b and height c + d. Removing the two triangles of area ac/2 and bd/2 and the two rectangles of area bc each leaves

(a + b)(c + d) − 2·(ac/2) − 2·(bd/2) − 2bc = ac + ad + bc + bd − ac − bd − 2bc = ad − bc Read as: a and c are the two entries of the first column, b and d the two entries of the second. The first bracket is the area of the enclosing rectangle, and the three subtractions remove the parts of it that are outside the parallelogram. Everything cancels except ad − bc.

The sign records whether the map turned the plane over, which is why the area is the size of the determinant rather than the determinant itself. ■

What the general case adds. In n dimensions the same statement holds with volume in place of area, and the proof is by induction rather than by cutting up a rectangle. This course proves the case you can see and takes the general one from it.

The 2 by 2 formula generalises to the cofactor expansion, which is the definition of the determinant and is not a method for computing one. It costs about n! multiplications: 6 terms at n = 3, 3.6 million at n = 10, and about 2.4 × 1018 at n = 20. Every library computes a determinant from a factorisation instead. Cramer's rule and the adjugate inverse have the same problem and the same status: worth having seen, never worth running. The formula sheet carries all three.

Composition is multiplication, and the order is the order

Applying S and then R to a vector means computing R(Sx), which by associativity is (RS)x. So the matrix of "first S, then R" is the product RS, written right to left in the order they are applied.

This is where lesson 44's non-commutativity stops being an algebraic curiosity. Rotating by 30 degrees and then stretching is a different transformation from stretching and then rotating, and the two matrices differ because the transformations do.

A worked example, in eight steps

  1. Name the objects. R rotates counter-clockwise by 30 degrees, S is the dilation with factors 2 and 0.5, and v = (1, 1) is the point being moved.
  2. Write the matrices. R = [[0.8660, −0.5], [0.5, 0.8660]] and S = [[2, 0], [0, 0.5]].
  3. Check the columns rule. Re1 = (0.8660, 0.5), which is the first column, and Re2 = (−0.5, 0.8660), which is the second.
  4. Apply S first, then R. Sv = (2, 0.5), and rotating that gives (0.8660×2 − 0.5×0.5, 0.5×2 + 0.8660×0.5) = (1.4821, 1.4330).
  5. Apply R first, then S. Rv = (0.3660, 1.3660), and stretching that gives (0.7321, 0.6830).
  6. Compare. Two different points from the same two transformations and the same starting vector. The matrices RS and SR are different matrices.
  7. Check the areas. det R = 0.86602 + 0.52 = 1, and det S = 2 × 0.5 = 1. Both preserve area, and the product of the determinants is 1 whichever order you compose them in.
  8. Say what would change. Replacing S with the dilation (2, 0) makes its determinant zero. The map then sends every point onto the horizontal axis, and applying all 12,000 sensor points leaves exactly two distinct directions among them: the two rays of a single line.

Sanity check. Step 4 and step 5 must disagree, because the two matrices disagree; if they had matched, one of the two products was computed in the wrong order. And a rotation must leave every length alone, so ||Rv|| should equal ||v|| = √2 = 1.4142, which it does.

What it means. A neural network layer applies a weight matrix and then bends the result. The matrix part is exactly one of these maps, in several hundred dimensions rather than two, and stacking layers composes them. Depth works because composition of linear maps is another linear map, and the non-linearity between them is what stops the whole stack collapsing into a single matrix.

code/0046-a-matrix-is-a-function.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

def shoelace(polygon):
    x, y = polygon[:, 0], polygon[:, 1]
    return 0.5 * (x @ np.roll(y, -1) - y @ np.roll(x, -1))

UNIT_SQUARE = np.array([[0., 0.], [1., 0.], [1., 1.], [0., 1.]])
A = np.array([[1.0, 1.5], [0.0, 1.0]])              # a shear

print(A @ np.array([1., 0.]), A[:, 0])              # the columns rule
print(abs(np.linalg.det(A)), abs(shoelace(UNIT_SQUARE @ A.T)))   # 1.0  1.0

The full program checks the columns rule and the area claim for all four maps, applies each to the 12,000 standardised vibration readings, and compares a cofactor expansion against the library determinant at sizes 2, 3 and 4.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

How do you find the matrix of a geometric transformation you can describe?
Column j is the image of the j-th basis vector, so applying the transformation to each basis vector reads the matrix straight off. The second is not enough information: one input and one output do not determine a map in two dimensions. The third is a different matrix entirely. The fourth would work and is a much longer route, which is why nobody uses it.
The matrix [[1, 2], [2, 4]] acts on the plane. What does it do?
The second column is twice the first, so every image is a multiple of (1, 2): the determinant is zero and the whole plane lands on one line. The first would need orthonormal columns, which these are not. The second describes 2I, a different matrix. The fourth describes the identity, whose determinant is 1 rather than 0.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Let R rotate counter-clockwise by 30 degrees and let S be the dilation with factors 2 and 0.5.

  1. Write both matrices, using cos 30 = 0.8660 and sin 30 = 0.5.
  2. Apply R to x = (3, 1) and check that the length is unchanged.
  3. Apply S then R to (1, 1), and then R then S to (1, 1). Are the two results equal?
  4. Which of the two preserves area, and how can you tell from the matrix without moving anything?
Hint

For part 3 the trap is the order. "Apply S first, then R" is the matrix product RS, written right to left. Doing it as two separate matrix-vector products in the right order avoids the question entirely.

Hint for part 4

You are looking for one number per matrix, and this page named it. You do not need to transform a single point.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write them. R = [[0.8660, −0.5], [0.5, 0.8660]], S = [[2, 0], [0, 0.5]].
  2. Rotate (3, 1). First entry 0.8660×3 − 0.5×1 = 2.0981. Second entry 0.5×3 + 0.8660×1 = 2.3660. So Rx = (2.0981, 2.3660).
  3. Check the length. ||x|| = √(9 + 1) = 3.1623. ||Rx|| = √(4.4021 + 5.5980) = √10.0001 = 3.1623. Unchanged, as a rotation must leave it.
  4. S then R. S(1, 1) = (2, 0.5). Rotating: (0.8660×2 − 0.5×0.5, 0.5×2 + 0.8660×0.5) = (1.4821, 1.4330).
  5. R then S. R(1, 1) = (0.3660, 1.3660). Stretching: (2×0.3660, 0.5×1.3660) = (0.7321, 0.6830).
  6. Compare. Not equal, and not close. Composition does not commute, so the order of the two operations is part of the transformation.
  7. Take the determinants. det R = 0.8660×0.8660 − (−0.5)×0.5 = 0.7500 + 0.2500 = 1. det S = 2×0.5 − 0 = 1. Both preserve area.

Sanity check. Part 3 must give two different answers, because part 7 shows the two matrices are different. And part 2's two lengths must agree to every digit you carried, because a rotation cannot change a length at all: if they differ by more than rounding, the sine and cosine have been swapped somewhere.

Your teacher. If the columns rule has not become automatic, ask an agent to give you five transformations in words, one at a time, and to check the matrix you write for each. Reflections and projections onto a line are the two worth practising, because both look harder than they are once you only have to ask where two arrows go.

Primary source to go deeper

Boyd and Vandenberghe, Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra, section 7.1. It gives the matrices for scaling, dilation, rotation, reflection and projection onto a line in one place, and states the find-the-matrix-by-its-columns rule in the same two sentences this page uses.
