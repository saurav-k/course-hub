Every penalty term you have ever added was a prior

working 13 min MAP as MLE plus a log-prior, ridge as a Gaussian, the lasso as a Laplace, and the limit that carries. · code/0165

The one-minute version

  • Take the log of Bayes' theorem. The evidence has no parameter in it, so it drops out of the argmax.
  • What is left is MAP = argmax [ log-likelihood + log-prior ]. MLE plus one extra term, and nothing else.
  • A Gaussian prior on each weight contributes −w2/(2τ2), which is ridge, with λ = σ2/τ2.
  • A Laplace prior contributes −|w|/b, which is the lasso. Same derivation, one distribution swapped.
  • The honest limit: these are posterior modes. Ridge is also the posterior mean; the lasso is not, and its sparsity belongs to the mode alone.

1. Take the log of Bayes' theorem

You have typed weight_decay=0.01 without being able to say what it assumes. This page says. Lesson 125 gave posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ evidence; take logs:

log p(θ | data) = log L(θ) + log p(θ) − log p(data) θ: the parameter, or the whole vector of weights. L(θ): the likelihood from Lesson 162. p(θ): the prior, what you believed before the data. p(data): the evidence - and the crucial thing about it is that θ does not appear in it.

The evidence contains no θ, so it is a constant for the argmax. Drop it.

θ-hatMAP = argmax [ log L(θ) + log p(θ) ] Read it as the MLE objective with one term added. Set the prior to a flat one - the same density everywhere - and its log is a constant too, which drops out and leaves the MLE exactly. MLE is MAP with no opinion.
The reframing MLE listens only to the data. MAP starts from what you believed and lets the data move it. How hard the data has to push is the strength of the prior, and that strength is the regularisation parameter you have been tuning by cross-validation.

2. A Gaussian prior is ridge

Setting. Gaussian noise, so the log-likelihood is Lesson 164's. Put an independent Normal(0, τ2) prior on each weight: coefficients near zero, with τ saying how near.

  1. Write the prior's log for one weight. The Gaussian density is a constant times exp(−w2/(2τ2)), so its log is −w2/(2τ2) plus a constant.
  2. Sum over the weights. The prior is independent across weights, so the logs add: −(1/(2τ2)) ∑wj2.
  3. Write the log-likelihood. From Lesson 164 it is a constant minus (1/(2σ2)) ∑(yi − predictioni)2.
  4. Add them and flip the sign to minimise. Minimise (1/(2σ2)) ∑residual2 + (1/(2τ2)) ∑wj2.
  5. Multiply through by 2σ2, which cannot move the argmin because it is positive. Minimise ∑residual2 + (σ2/τ2) ∑wj2.
  6. Read off the penalty. That is ridge regression, with λ = σ2/τ2.
The step that does the real work is step 5, and it is where λ acquires a meaning rather than being a knob. It is a ratio of two variances: the noise in the data over the spread you allow the weights. Noisy data or a tight prior both raise it. A cross-validated λ of 9 is a statement that you believe the noise variance is nine times the weight variance, whether or not anyone said so out loud.

3. Measured: the prior earning its keep

The features.csv file has 30 predictors of which only five are real, with true coefficients 4.0, −2.5, 1.5, −1.0 and 0.6, and the other 25 exactly zero. Fit on 60 rows, so the problem is nearly as wide as it is tall and the prior has real work to do.

  1. Start with almost no prior, τ2 = 106 so λ = 0.000. The coefficients come out 4.028, −2.019, 1.420, −1.797 and 0.331, and the largest coefficient on a pure-noise column is 0.840. Total distance from the truth: 2.414.
  2. Tighten to τ2 = 1, so λ = 9. Coefficients shrink to 3.360, −1.582, 1.086, −1.283, 0.178; the worst noise coefficient falls to 0.641; the distance improves to 2.029.
  3. Tighten to τ2 = 0.25, λ = 36. The noise coefficient falls again to 0.519, and the distance worsens to 2.720.
  4. Tighten hard, τ2 = 0.05, λ = 180. Noise down to 0.300 and distance up to 4.013.
  5. Read the trade. Suppressing the fake predictors always helps and shrinking the real ones always hurts, and the same knob does both. The best setting is where the two rates cross.

Sanity check. The noise coefficients must shrink monotonically as λ grows, since the penalty acts on all weights alike and the data supports these ones least. They do: 0.840, 0.641, 0.519, 0.300. If a noise coefficient had grown, the fit did not converge.

4. The objective, evaluated directly

The two terms really do trade against each other, and you can watch it. Evaluating log-likelihood and log-prior separately at three prior strengths:

  • τ2 = 100, λ = 0.09: log L = −135.558, log prior = −96.797, sum −232.356.
  • τ2 = 1, λ = 9: log L = −137.847, log prior = −37.112, sum −174.959.
  • τ2 = 0.25, λ = 36: log L = −148.188, log prior = −25.862, sum −174.050.

A tighter prior always costs likelihood and always buys prior. MAP maximises the sum, and λ is the exchange rate between the two.

5. Swap the prior and you get the lasso

Nothing in section 2 was special to the Gaussian. Replace it with a Laplace prior, density (1/(2b)) exp(−|w|/b).

  1. Take its log. −|w|/b plus a constant.
  2. Sum over the weights and add to the log-likelihood, exactly as before.
  3. Flip the sign and scale. Minimise ∑residual2 + λ ∑|wj|, with λ = 2σ2/b. Ridge's constant in section 2 carried no 2 and this one does, under the same convention, and that is not a slip: the Gaussian log-prior −w2/(2τ2) brings its own one-half which cancels the 2, while the Laplace log-prior −|w|/b brings none, so here the 2 survives.
  4. Recognise it. That is the lasso. One distribution swapped, one derivation reused.

Measured on the same 30 predictors: at λ = 1 the lasso keeps 28 of 30 coefficients nonzero and all 5 real ones, with distance 2.219. At λ = 20, 18 nonzero and 4 real, distance 1.207. At λ = 60, only 5 nonzero and 4 real, distance 1.395. At λ = 150, 3 nonzero and distance 2.583.

The reframing The lasso sets coefficients to exactly zero and ridge never does. That is not a difference in the algorithms, it is a difference in the two priors' shape at the origin, and it was decided the moment you chose the penalty.

6. Two trade-offs, and the second one is an honest limit

First, a strong prior buys variance reduction with bias. That is Lesson 161's identity showing up as something you tune rather than something you observe, and section 3's error curve is that identity plotted.

Second, and this is the limit the result carries: these are posterior modes, not posterior means. Ridge happens to be both, because a Gaussian likelihood times a Gaussian prior is Gaussian, and a Gaussian is symmetric about its peak. The lasso is the mode only, and the difference is not a detail. The Laplace-prior posterior is an absolutely continuous distribution over the weights, so the probability that any single weight is exactly zero is zero, so the posterior mean contains no exact zeros at all. The sparsity belongs to the mode, which is one particular summary of the posterior that the lasso happens to report.

So "regularisation is just Bayesian inference" is too strong. The correct sentence is that these penalties are the log-densities of particular priors, and the estimator is a particular summary of the resulting posterior.

7. Worked: reading a λ as a belief

Someone hands you a ridge model with λ = 9, fitted on data whose residual variance is 9.0.

  1. Recall the identity. λ = σ2/τ2 from section 2.
  2. Substitute. 9 = 9.0/τ2.
  3. Solve. τ2 = 1.0, so τ = 1.0.
  4. State the belief in words. Each coefficient was assumed drawn from a Normal(0, 1), so before seeing any data you were 95 per cent sure every coefficient lay between −1.96 and +1.96.
  5. Check it against the truth. The true coefficients here are 4.0, −2.5, 1.5, −1.0 and 0.6. Two of the five lie outside that interval, so the prior was genuinely wrong about them - and it still improved the total error, because it was right about the 25 zeros.
  6. Read the lesson. A prior does not have to be correct to help. It has to be less wrong than having no opinion at all, which on 60 rows and 30 predictors is a low bar.

Sanity check. λ and τ2 must move in opposite directions: a larger λ means a tighter prior means a smaller τ2. At λ = 36 the same arithmetic gives τ2 = 0.25, which is indeed tighter. If they had moved together, the ratio was inverted.

What changes if the noise variance is larger. Hold τ2 = 1 and raise σ2 from 9 to 36, and λ goes from 9 to 36. Noisier data calls for more regularisation at the same beliefs about the weights, which is exactly right and falls straight out of the ratio. It is also why a λ tuned on one dataset should not be copied to another with a different noise level.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does the evidence term drop out when you compute a MAP estimate?
The evidence is a number that depends on the data alone, so it shifts the whole objective vertically and moves the argmax not at all. The second option is the one worth rejecting carefully: it is not negligible, it is often enormous, and it is irrelevant for a different reason. The first is false - it is a probability of the data, rarely 1. The third invents a cancellation that does not happen.
A ridge model uses λ = 4 and the residual variance is 16. What belief about the weights does that encode?
λ = σ^2/τ^2, so τ^2 = 16/4 = 4 and the prior is Normal(0, 4). The first option reaches the right distribution by the wrong route and would give a different answer for any other noise variance, which is exactly the reasoning this page replaces. The third swaps ridge for the lasso. The fourth is the misconception the whole page removes.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You are fitting a model whose residual variance is 2.0, and you believe each coefficient is very likely to lie between −0.5 and +0.5.

Turn that belief into a Gaussian prior variance, derive the λ it implies, and say what would change if you used a Laplace prior of the same width instead.

Hint

"Very likely between −a and +a" is the two-sigma band from Lesson 133, so a is about 2τ. Then use the ratio from section 2.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the belief as a two-sigma band. 0.5 is about 2τ, so τ = 0.25.
  2. Square it. τ2 = 0.0625.
  3. Apply the ratio. λ = σ2/τ2 = 2.0 ÷ 0.0625 = 32.
  4. Sanity-read the number. A tight belief on a fairly clean signal gives a strong penalty, which is what 32 is.
  5. Swap the prior. A Laplace of comparable width gives the lasso instead: the penalty becomes the sum of absolute values, coefficients are driven to exactly zero rather than merely small, and the estimator stops being the posterior mean as well as the mode.

Sanity check. A tighter belief must give a larger λ. Halve the band to ±0.25 and τ2 falls by four, so λ quadruples to 128 - the relationship is inverse-square in the band width, which is worth feeling: small changes in how confidently you state a prior make large changes to the penalty.

Your teacher. Next time you tune a regularisation strength, convert the winner into a prior and say it out loud: "cross-validation chose λ = 9, and with this noise level that says I believed each coefficient was within about ±2 before seeing any data". Sometimes that sentence is reasonable and sometimes it is obviously false, and either way you have learned something the grid search could not tell you. Ask an agent to invert a λ you have actually used.
