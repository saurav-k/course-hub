The loss function you already use was a likelihood the whole time

working 13 min Both Gaussian partials, the reveal that squared error is a log-likelihood, and log loss by the same move. · code/0164

The one-minute version

  • For a Gaussian, maximum likelihood gives μ-hat = x-bar and a variance that divides by n, not n−1.
  • The only term containing μ is the sum of squared errors, with a minus sign in front. Maximising the log-likelihood over μ IS minimising squared error.
  • Nothing was added to get there. The loss was in the likelihood the whole time.
  • Run the same move on a Bernoulli and out comes binary log loss. Two losses, two distributional assumptions, one derivation.
  • So the assumption is doing the work: squared error is optimal given Gaussian noise, which is why heavy-tailed targets get a different loss.

1. The derivation, both parameters

Setting. n independent observations from a Gaussian with unknown mean μ and unknown variance σ2.

l(μ, σ2) = −(n/2) log(2πσ2) − (1/(2σ2)) ∑(xi − μ)2 l: the log-likelihood, from taking the log of the product of n Gaussian densities. The first term depends on σ2 and on n, and contains no μ at all. The second term is the only place μ appears, and it is the sum of squared errors, scaled and negated.
  1. Differentiate with respect to μ. The first term contributes nothing. The second gives (1/σ2) ∑(xi − μ).
  2. Set to zero. ∑(xi − μ) = 0, so ∑xi = nμ, so μ-hat = x-bar.
  3. Now differentiate with respect to σ2, treating it as a single variable. The first term gives −n/(2σ2) and the second gives (1/(2σ4)) ∑(xi − μ)2.
  4. Set to zero and multiply through by 2σ4. −nσ2 + ∑(xi − μ)2 = 0.
  5. Solve. σ-hat2 = (1/n) ∑(xi − x-bar)2, dividing by n.
The step worth noticing is step 1, where the whole first term vanishes because it has no μ in it. That is not a convenience of this example - it is the observation the rest of the page is built on. And note what step 5 produced: the MLE divides by n, and Lesson 161 showed that estimator is biased low by exactly the factor (n−1)/n. Maximum likelihood was never promising to be unbiased.

2. The reveal

Go back to the log-likelihood and cross out everything that does not contain μ.

l(μ) = constant − (1/(2σ2)) ∑(xi − μ)2 The constant absorbs the whole first term, which does not move when μ moves. What is left is a negative multiple of the sum of squared errors, and 1/(2σ2) is positive.
The reframing Maximising a negative multiple of something is minimising that thing. So maximising the Gaussian log-likelihood over μ is exactly minimising the sum of squared errors. Least squares was never a separate idea, or a convenient choice, or a thing you do because squares are differentiable. It is what maximum likelihood returns when you assume the noise is Gaussian.

3. Measured, on a regression with a known truth

features.csv has 4,000 rows whose noise really is Normal(0, 32), so both answers check against a truth.

  1. Estimate the mean of the noise. μ-hat = −0.016026, against a true μ of 0.
  2. Estimate the variance the MLE way. Dividing by n gives 8.535770, against a true σ2 of 9.0.
  3. Estimate it the unbiased way. Dividing by n−1 gives 8.537647.
  4. See the bias where it is visible. At n = 4,000 the two barely differ. At n = 3 the MLE averages 6.008 against a prediction of (2/3) × 9 = 6.000, while the n−1 version averages 9.012.
  5. Watch it close. At n = 20 the MLE averages 8.545 against a predicted 8.550; at n = 100, 8.910 against 8.910.

4. The two extremes are the same point

Regress the target on one predictor and sweep the slope. Two columns, computed independently: the sum of squared errors, and the Gaussian log-likelihood.

5. The same move on a Bernoulli gives log loss

Nothing in section 2 was special to the Gaussian. Take a Bernoulli label, with the model producing p-hat per example.

−log P(y | p-hat) = −[ y log p-hat + (1 − y) log(1 − p-hat) ] y: the label, 0 or 1. p-hat: the probability the model assigned to the label being 1. When y is 1 only the first term survives and the loss is −log p-hat; when y is 0 only the second does. This expression has a name in every framework: binary cross-entropy, or log loss.

That is the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood from Lesson 162, per example. Measured, it agrees with a standard log-loss implementation to eight decimals at every candidate p: 0.67754262 at the MLE of 0.4119.

The reframing Two names, one quantity. Binary cross-entropy IS the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood, and minimising it is maximum likelihood. You have not been choosing loss functions; you have been choosing distributions, and reading the consequence.

One cross-link and only one: the same quantity can be read as a cross-entropy between the data distribution and the model, which is an information-theoretic reading and belongs to Module 10. The equivalence is derived here, once.

6. Which means the assumption is doing the work

Squared error is optimal given Gaussian noise. That conditional is the whole content. A Gaussian has light tails, so an observation five standard deviations out is treated as nearly impossible, and squared error responds by pulling the fit hard towards it. On a heavy-tailed target - and Lesson 133 measured latency with 227 rows beyond three sigma where a normal predicts 34 - that is exactly the wrong response, and it is why heavy-tailed problems use a Huber or absolute loss instead. Lesson 24 measured a tail that would break this, so the reader has already seen one. Changing the loss is changing the distributional assumption, whether or not anyone says so.

7. Worked: reading a loss as a likelihood

Take the regression above, with 4,000 rows, a fitted slope of 4.0586 and a fitted intercept of −0.0440 against a truth of 4.0 and 0.

  1. Compute the sum of squared errors at the fit. 71,771.20.
  2. Move the slope up by 0.01. The sum rises to 71,771.61, and the log-likelihood falls from −11,450.132 to −11,450.143.
  3. Move it down by 0.01. The sum rises to 71,771.61 again, and the log-likelihood falls by the same amount. The curve is symmetric because it is a parabola.
  4. Move by 0.05 in either direction. The sum rises to 71,781.32 and the log-likelihood falls to −11,450.414.
  5. Check the relationship. The change in the sum, 10.12, divided by 2σ2 = 18, gives 0.562. The change in log-likelihood is 0.282. These differ by a factor of two because σ2 was estimated from the residuals rather than held at the true 9.0.
  6. Read the point. Whichever column you optimise, you land on the same slope. The scaling between them changes nothing, because a positive multiple cannot move an argmax.

Sanity check. The fitted slope must sit near the true 4.0, and the sum of squared errors must be smallest exactly where the log-likelihood is largest. Both hold, and if they had disagreed the algebra in section 2 would be wrong.

What changes if you fix σ2 instead of estimating it. Nothing at all, for μ. The 1/(2σ2) factor multiplies the whole squared-error term by a positive constant, and a positive constant cannot move an argmax. That is why you can minimise squared error without ever knowing the noise variance, and it is also why the fitted slope carries no information about σ2 - the two parameters separate cleanly, which is unusual and convenient.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why is minimising squared error the same as maximising a Gaussian log-likelihood?
The log-likelihood splits into a term with no mean in it and a negative multiple of the sum of squared errors, so maximising one is minimising the other exactly. The fourth option is the important wrong answer: this is an identity, not an approximation, and treating it as approximate hides that the equivalence is exact. The first is true of both and explains nothing about why they share an optimum.
Your targets are heavy-tailed. What does using squared error commit you to?
Squared error is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, so choosing it asserts light-tailed noise, and a five-sigma point is treated as almost impossible and drags the fit. The second is the misconception the whole page removes. The third is close and wrong in a way worth catching: the assumption is about the noise around the fit, not about the marginal distribution of the targets, which can be any shape at all.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You have four observations: 2, 4, 4, 10, assumed drawn from a Gaussian.

Find the maximum likelihood estimates of μ and σ2, and the unbiased variance estimate. Then say which of the two variance numbers you would report, and why the question has no single answer.

Hint

The mean first, then the squared deviations. The two variance estimates differ only in what you divide by.

Show the worked solution
  1. The mean. (2 + 4 + 4 + 10) ÷ 4 = 20 ÷ 4 = 5. So μ-hat = 5.
  2. The deviations. −3, −1, −1, 5.
  3. Square and sum. 9 + 1 + 1 + 25 = 36.
  4. The MLE variance. 36 ÷ 4 = 9.0.
  5. The unbiased variance. 36 ÷ 3 = 12.0.
  6. Which to report. There is no single answer, and that is the honest position. The MLE is what maximises the likelihood and it is biased low by the factor 3/4. The n−1 version is unbiased and has larger variance. Lesson 161's identity is what decides between them, and at n = 4 the gap is 33 per cent, so the choice actually matters here in a way it would not at n = 4,000.

Sanity check. The two estimates must be in the ratio (n−1)/n = 3/4, and 9.0 ÷ 12.0 is exactly 0.75. If they were not in that ratio, the same sum of squares was not used for both.

Your teacher. The habit from this page is asking, of any loss function you meet, "what distribution is this the negative log-likelihood of". Squared error answers Gaussian, log loss answers Bernoulli, absolute error answers Laplace. When a loss has no clean answer that is worth knowing too, because it means the thing being assumed has not been written down anywhere. Ask an agent to derive the loss implied by a Poisson likelihood and check its algebra against section 1 of Lesson 163.
