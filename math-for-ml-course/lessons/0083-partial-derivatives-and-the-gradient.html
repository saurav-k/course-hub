Partial derivatives put every dial on its own axis, and the gradient collects them

foundation 11 min The object every optimiser in Module 06 reads, assembled from parts you already have.

The one-minute version

  • A partial derivative is the derivative you already know, taken while every other dial is held still. Nothing new is required, which is the point.
  • Collect one per dial and you have the gradient, a single object rather than a list of separate facts.
  • Its shape is checkable, and this course fixes one convention: a gradient has the shape of the thing you differentiate by.
  • The components of a real gradient can differ by a factor of 77, and that is a fact about the units of the columns, not about which feature matters.

1. Two dials instead of one

A curve has a slope. A surface does not: it tilts differently depending on which way you walk. So the question "how fast does the loss change" needs a direction attached before it has an answer.

The cheapest way to attach one is to pick a coordinate axis and walk along it, holding everything else still. Do that for each dial in turn and you have said everything a first-order answer can say.

2. The partial derivative

∂f/∂xj = lim ( f(x + h ej) − f(x) ) ÷ h as h → 0 f: a function taking several numbers and returning one, such as a loss. x: the whole vector of dials, written as a column. ej: the vector that is 1 in slot j and 0 everywhere else, so x + hej nudges dial j alone. ∂: a curly d, used instead of the straight one to signal that other variables exist and are being held fixed.

Compare that with the difference quotient in Lesson 80 and you will find it is the same expression. Only the step has changed, from a number to a nudge in one coordinate.

3. Collect them, and check the shape

The gradient is the vector holding one partial derivative per dial. It is written ∇f, and its length is the number of dials.

The convention, fixed onceTextbooks disagree about whether a gradient is written as a row or a column, and both are internally consistent. This course uses: a gradient has the shape of the thing you differentiate by. Vectors are columns, so the gradient of a scalar loss with respect to a column of parameters is a column. Mathematics for Machine Learning takes the other convention and says so explicitly; when you read it, transpose in your head rather than concluding one of you is wrong. The full table is in the notation sheet.

4. Worked: a gradient computed as two numbers

Return to the ten daily spends and fit a straight line through them, spend against day index. Two dials: an intercept a and a slope b. Start at the guess a = 1000, b = 0, which says "every day costs a thousand rupees and nothing trends". It is wrong. Which way should each dial move?

L(a, b) = ∑i ( yi − a − b di )² L: total squared error, one number, the thing to be made small. a: the intercept dial. b: the slope dial. yi: the spend actually observed on day i. di: the day index, running 1 to 10. yi − a − b di: the residual, how wrong the line is on that day.
  1. Differentiate in a, holding b still. Each term contributes −2 times its residual, so ∂L/∂a = −2∑(yi − a − b di).
  2. Evaluate it. With b = 0 the residuals are yi − 1000, and the ten spends total 12,250, so the residuals total 12,250 − 10,000 = 2,250. Hence ∂L/∂a = −4,500.
  3. Differentiate in b, holding a still. The chain rule adds a factor of di, so ∂L/∂b = −2∑di(yi − a − b di).
  4. Evaluate it. The day-weighted spends total 63,560 and 1,000 × (1+2+...+10) = 55,000, so the weighted residuals total 8,560 and ∂L/∂b = −17,120.
  5. Assemble. ∇L = (−4,500, −17,120), a column with one entry per dial.

Sanity check. ∂L/∂a must be −2 times the total residual, and the total residual at a = 1000 is positive because one day cost Rs 8,500. So the first component must come out negative, and it does. A positive value means a sign slipped.

What changes if the Rs 8,500 day were Rs 450 like its neighbours? The residuals then total 4,200 − 10,000 = −5,800, so ∂L/∂a flips to +11,600 and the intercept should come down instead. One observation owns the sign of the gradient.

In words. The gradient is not a verdict about the model. It is a local instruction: at this exact guess, raising both dials reduces the loss right now, and it says nothing about where you will end up.

Not derived here, on purposeSetting this gradient to zero and solving gives the normal equations, and that derivation belongs to Module 03, which reaches the same answer by dropping a perpendicular and deliberately needs no calculus to do it. Lesson 91 shows the calculus route and puts the two side by side.

5. The same thing at a scale you cannot do by hand

Twelve thousand sensor readings, six columns predicting a seventh. Seven dials now, and the interesting part is not the arithmetic but the spread.

The reframingA large gradient component does not mean an important feature. It means a column whose numbers are large. Lesson 89 turns that observation into the condition number, and that is the number that decides how fast anything can train.

6. One partial that is exactly zero, for a reason you can name

The intercept's partial in that run comes out at −9.597 × 10−15, which is zero to rounding. That is not luck.

The starting guess predicts the overall mean for every reading, and the mean is exactly the number that makes the residuals sum to zero. Their sum is the intercept's partial derivative, from step 1 of the worked example. So the guess already solves one dial exactly, and the other six are still wrong.

7. The trade-off: a zero gradient is not a minimum

Every component being zero makes a point critical, and nothing more. A maximum has a zero gradient. So does a saddle. Lesson 88 supplies the test that tells them apart, and until then "the gradient vanished" is an observation rather than a result.

def gradient(theta, x, y):
    """The closed form: -2/n * X^T (y - X theta).

    Component j is the partial derivative with respect to theta_j, so this
    one line is the whole lesson.
    """
    residual = y - x @ theta
    return -2.0 / len(y) * (x.T @ residual)


def numeric_partial(theta, x, y, j, h):
    """Nudge parameter j alone, hold the rest, take a central difference.
    This is the definition of a partial derivative, executed."""
    up, down = theta.copy(), theta.copy()
    up[j] += h
    down[j] -= h
    return (loss(up, x, y) - loss(down, x, y)) / (2.0 * h)

The program checks every one of the seven partials against the definition, agreeing to between 6 × 10−8 and 1 × 10−6. It also walks along the negative gradient and shows the loss falling to 53.72 and then climbing back to 54.13 and on to 592.43 as the step grows, which is the curvature limit Lesson 89 explains.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

For a loss taking d parameters, how many numbers does the gradient hold and what fixes its shape?
One partial per parameter, so d of them, and whether they are written as a row or a column is a convention rather than mathematics. The second option describes the Hessian, which is Lesson 88. The third confuses the output's shape with the derivative's. The fourth is a true-sounding statement about a different question: linearity changes the values, never the count.
On the sensor regression the pressure partial is 777 and the humidity partial is 10. What follows?
Pressure runs in the hundreds of kilopascals and dust index around one, so the partial inherits the units. The first two are the misconception the page exists to break: gradient magnitude is not feature importance, and either conclusion would survive standardising the columns only by accident. The fourth is false, and the check against the definition in the program confirms the numbers.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Fit a line to the ten daily spends by day index, starting from the guess a = 1000, b = 0.

∑ yi = 12,250     ∑ di yi = 63,560     ∑ di = 55 Three totals over the ten days, given so the arithmetic stays short. yi is the spend on day i and di is the day index from 1 to 10.

Write both partial derivatives symbolically, evaluate both, and say which way each dial should move and why the two magnitudes differ so much.

Hint

With b = 0 the residual on day i is just yi − 1000, so both sums can be written using the three totals above and nothing else.

Show the worked solution
  1. Symbolically. ∂L/∂a = −2∑(yi − a − b di) and ∂L/∂b = −2∑di(yi − a − b di).
  2. The first sum. 12,250 − 10 × 1,000 = 2,250, so ∂L/∂a = −4,500.
  3. The second sum. 63,560 − 1,000 × 55 = 8,560, so ∂L/∂b = −17,120.
  4. The direction. Both components are negative, so both dials should rise.
  5. Why the magnitudes differ. The day column runs 1 to 10 while the intercept's implicit column is all ones, so the second sum is weighted by numbers up to ten times larger. It is a scale artefact and says nothing about which dial matters.

Sanity check. ∂L/∂a must be exactly −2 times the total residual. The total residual is 2,250, so the answer must be −4,500 and nothing else. If it is not, recheck the sum of the spends.

Your teacher. If holding a variable fixed still feels like a trick, ask an agent to write out L(a, 0) as a function of a alone for these ten numbers and differentiate that, then confirm it matches. If the shape ledger is the part that did not stick, ask it for the shape of the derivative for each of the four rows with concrete dimensions substituted in.
