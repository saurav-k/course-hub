Saddle points and plateaus: what actually slows deep training

frontier 12 min Dauphin, Pascanu, Gulcehre, Cho, Ganguli & Bengio (2014). · arXiv:1406.2572

The one-minute version

  • A critical point is classified by the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues. All positive is a minimum, mixed is a saddle.
  • A minimum needs every one of n signs to come up positive, so saddles outnumber minima exponentially in n.
  • The good news gets skipped: eigenvalues are more likely positive at low cost, so the minima you actually reach tend to be good ones. The folk fear of bad local minima is misplaced.
  • And the twist: Newton's method is attracted to saddle points. The exact second-order step is a trap exactly where the landscape is hardest.

1. Three kinds of flat point

At any point where the gradient vanishes, the Hessian's eigenvalues tell you what kind of place you are standing in. Each eigenvalue is the curvature along its eigenvector: positive curves up, negative curves down.

2. Why saddles dominate: the coin-flip argument

Here is the model, stated as a model. At a critical point of a random function of n variables, treat the n eigenvalue signs as independent fair coin flips. This is the idealisation Goodfellow et al. use to carry the intuition; the real statement needs random-matrix theory and is cited rather than proved here.

  1. A minimum needs every sign positive. Probability 2-n.
  2. A maximum needs every sign negative. Also 2-n.
  3. Everything else is a saddle. Probability 1 − 2 × 2-n.
  4. Take the ratio. (1 − 21−n) ÷ 2-n = 2n − 2.
  5. Put numbers in. At n = 2 there are 2 saddles per minimum. At n = 10, 1,022. At n = 50, about 1.1 × 1015.
  6. Notice the shape. The ratio grows exponentially in n, so at any realistic parameter count it is a number with no name.
The independence assumption is an idealisation and the page says so. Real Hessian spectra are not independent coin flips. What the sources establish is that the conclusion survives a more careful treatment: Dauphin et al. confirm experimentally that real networks have very many high-cost saddle points.

3. The half that gets skipped

The counting argument alone sounds like bad news, and on its own it would be. The second result is what changes the picture, and it is the one worth carrying.

The reframing The Hessian's eigenvalues become more likely to be positive in regions of lower cost. So local minima are much more likely to have low cost than high cost, and critical points with high cost are far more likely to be saddle points. The minimum you actually land in tends to be a good one.

Read that against the folk belief. The fear is that training gets stuck in a bad local minimum. What the theory and the experiments say is close to the opposite: bad critical points exist in enormous numbers and they are almost all saddles, which you leave; and the ones that are genuinely minima are concentrated near the bottom.

4. The twist: Newton walks into the saddle

Work near a critical point in the Hessian's eigenvector basis, and write a displacement as a combination of eigenvectors. Along eigenvector i, the gradient is approximately λidi.

  1. Gradient descent along direction i. The step is −ηλidi, so the displacement becomes (1 − ηλi)di.
  2. When λi is positive, the factor is below 1 and the displacement shrinks: it moves towards the critical point, which is correct along a direction that curves up.
  3. When λi is negative, the factor is above 1 and the displacement grows: it moves away, which is exactly what you want along a direction that curves down.
  4. Now Newton. The step is −(1/λi) × λidi = −di, for every i.
  5. Read what that means. Dividing by a negative eigenvalue flips the step exactly as often as the negative eigenvalue flipped the gradient, so the two sign changes cancel. The step is −di regardless of the sign of λi.
  6. Collect. Newton drives every component of the displacement to zero, whatever its curvature. The critical point is an attracting fixed point of the Newton iteration.

That is the step that does the real work, and it is five lines. Dauphin et al. put it directly: because Newton moves towards the critical point along negative-curvature directions as well as positive ones, "the saddle point becomes an attractor for the Newton method".

5. Worked: reading a flat training curve

Your loss has been flat for two thousand steps. Four candidate explanations, and one measurement that separates them.

  1. A bad local minimum. Almost certainly not, by sections 2 and 3: high-cost critical points are overwhelmingly saddles.
  2. A saddle or the plateau around one. Plausible. The gradient would be small but not vanishing, and progress would resume.
  3. Not a critical point at all. Also plausible, and the commonest in practice: a step size too small for the local curvature looks identical on a loss curve.
  4. A step size past 2/L. Ruled out by Lesson 103's picture: that diverges rather than flattening, although a saturated or overflowing loss can look flat.
  5. The measurement. Log the gradient norm. Goodfellow et al. state the test plainly: if the norm has not shrunk to insignificance, the problem is neither local minima nor any other kind of critical point.
  6. Apply it to three real runs. A norm steady at 0.38: not a critical point, so raise the step or wait. A norm at 1.1 × 10-16: genuinely converged. A norm at 0.53 and climbing: diverging, so cut the step.

Sanity check. The three diagnoses must come out different, and they do. If two agree you have used the loss curve, which is identical in all three cases, rather than the gradient norm, which is not.

What changes with a stochastic gradient? The norm no longer reaches zero even at the optimum, because of Lesson 104's noise floor. Compare a running average of it against the floor you would expect from your batch size rather than against zero.

What it means. One logged quantity separates three situations that are indistinguishable on the curve everybody watches. It costs one line in a training loop, and it is the most useful habit this module can hand you.

The honest limit. All of this is random-matrix theory plus experiments on particular networks. It is not a theorem about the network in front of you, and a page that presented it as one would be overselling a genuinely useful picture.
code/0108-saddle-points-and-plateaus.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def classify_by_sampling(objective, point, radius=1e-3, draws=4_000, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng(0)
    here = objective(point)
    rose = fell = 0
    for _ in range(draws):
        direction = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, len(point))
        direction /= np.linalg.norm(direction)
        difference = objective(point + radius * direction) - here
        if difference > 1e-12:
            rose += 1
        elif difference < -1e-12:
            fell += 1
    if fell == 0:
        return "minimum", rose, fell
    if rose == 0:
        return "maximum", rose, fell
    return "saddle", rose, fell

The program classifies each critical point twice: once from the eigenvalue signs and once by this sampling, which is what you can do when the Hessian is out of reach. The two agreed on all three cases only after the check caught two errors in the test data itself, where a bowl and a dome had been given the wrong Hessians. A consistency test you never see fail is a test you have no reason to believe.

Practice 1frontier

Three training runs have all gone flat. Their gradient norms are:

run A: steady at 0.4     run B: 1 × 10-7     run C: climbing past 1 × 103 Read as: the size of the gradient vector, one number per run, measured over the last few hundred steps. It is what the loss curve does not tell you, and all three loss curves look flat.

Diagnose each and name the next action.

Hint

Apply the one test before considering anything else, and notice it splits the three immediately. For the run it does not settle on its own, ask which of Lesson 103's four regimes produces a loss that stops changing while a gradient keeps growing.

Show the worked solution
  1. Run A. A norm of 0.4 is not insignificant, so this is not a critical point at all. The flat loss has another cause: a plateau, or a step size too small for the local curvature.
  2. Action for A. Raise the learning rate, or wait, and keep watching the norm rather than the loss.
  3. Run B. A norm of 10-7 is a genuine critical point.
  4. Classify it. Given the dimension, sections 2 and 3 say it is far more likely a flat minimum at low cost than anything worrying. Stop, or decay the step and confirm nothing further moves.
  5. Run C. A growing norm with a flat loss is divergence: the loss has saturated or overflowed to a constant while the parameters run away.
  6. Action for C. Cut the learning rate immediately. This is Lesson 103's η > 2/L, and it will not recover on its own.
  7. Read the general lesson. Three different situations, three different actions, and one identical loss curve.

Sanity check. Your three diagnoses must be three different ones. If any two come out the same, you have reasoned from the loss curve rather than from the norms, and the loss curve is exactly the thing that cannot tell these apart. Note also that run C's action is the opposite of run A's, so getting them the wrong way round makes each one worse.
