The learning rate is bounded by curvature

working 12 min The condition-number analysis is Boyd & Vandenberghe, section 9.3. · Convex Optimization

The one-minute version

  • On a quadratic, gradient descent converges if and only if η < 2/L, where L is the curvature. Above that it diverges, and the two behaviours look nothing alike.
  • In many dimensions L is the largest Hessian eigenvalue, so one stiff direction caps the step for every direction, including the flat ones that needed a big step.
  • That ratio has a name. On this course's credit data, standardising the features moves it from 2.8 × 1012 to 26.5 and the largest safe step from 1.5 × 10-9 to 3.85.
  • Warmup, decay and the range test are all negotiations with that same bound. Feature scaling is not hygiene, it is the intervention that moves the bound.

1. One dimension, completely

Take the simplest curved objective there is, J(w) = ½Lw2, whose minimum is at w = 0 and whose curvature is the constant L. Its derivative is Lw, so the update is w ← w − ηLw, which is

wk+1 = (1 − ηL) wk,    so    wk = (1 − ηL)k w0 wk: the parameter after k steps. η: the learning rate. L: the curvature, the second derivative, a fixed positive number here. (1 − ηL): the factor the parameter is multiplied by on every single step. The exponent k: the same factor applied k times, which is what makes this a geometric sequence.

A geometric sequence rkw0 goes to zero from every starting point exactly when the size of r is below 1. So the condition is |1 − ηL| < 1, which is −1 < 1 − ηL < 1, which is 0 < ηL < 2, which is:

The bound Gradient descent on a quadratic of curvature L converges from any starting point if and only if η < 2/L. This is derived here rather than quoted, and the whole derivation is the three lines above.

2. Four regimes, read off one number

The factor 1 − ηL tells you everything, and there are exactly four cases.

A loss that becomes NaN in the first epoch is almost always the fourth row. The failure is not gradual and it does not look like slow training. Past 2/L the iterates grow geometrically, so a run that was going to diverge has usually done so within a few dozen steps.

3. Many dimensions: one stiff direction caps them all

Real objectives have curvature that differs by direction, and the Hessian is the matrix holding all of it. Rotate into its eigenvector basis and the problem separates: along each eigenvector the iteration is exactly the one-dimensional case, with L replaced by that direction's eigenvalue λi.

So the run converges only if η < 2/λi for every i, which means η < 2/λmax. The single stiffest direction sets the step size for all of them, and the flat directions, which needed a big step, are given the small one.

κ = λmax / λmin κ: the condition number of the Hessian. λmax: the largest eigenvalue, the curvature in the stiffest direction. λmin: the smallest, the curvature in the flattest. The ratio: how much more curved the worst direction is than the best. At 1 the surface is a perfectly round bowl, and large values mean a long narrow valley.

Boyd & Vandenberghe work the exact case. For J(x) = ½(x12 + γx22) with exact line search from x0 = (γ, 1), the objective is multiplied by exactly ((γ−1)/(γ+1))2 every step. Those are not adjectives:

4. Measured: what standardising actually buys

The credit dataset was built with feature scales spanning six orders of magnitude, from a utilisation ratio in [0, 1] to an income in the hundreds of thousands. Here is the logistic Hessian's spectrum before and after standardising, on all 20,000 rows.

  1. Form the Hessian on the raw features. Its eigenvalues run from 4.74 × 10-4 to 1.34 × 109.
  2. Take the ratio. κ = 1.34 × 109 ÷ 4.74 × 10-4 = 2.82 × 1012.
  3. Take the step bound. 2/λmax = 2 ÷ 1.34 × 109 = 1.50 × 10-9. Any learning rate you would type by hand diverges immediately.
  4. Standardise every column to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and recompute. The eigenvalues now run 1.96 × 10-2 to 5.20 × 10-1.
  5. Take the new ratio. κ = 26.5.
  6. Take the new bound. 2/λmax = 3.85.
  7. Compare. The conditioning improved by a factor of 1.06 × 1011, and the largest safe learning rate moved by a factor of 2.6 × 109.

Sanity check. The raw bound had to be tiny, because one feature is measured in hundreds of thousands and squaring it in a Hessian gives something of order 109 or more. The arithmetic agrees with what the units alone predict.

What changes if you standardise only some columns? The bound follows the worst remaining column, because λmax is a maximum. Standardising eleven of twelve features buys almost nothing if the twelfth is still measured in rupees.

What it means. "Scale your features" is usually taught as a preprocessing habit. It is a nine-orders-of-magnitude change in the one number you type into your optimizer, and that is why skipping it does not produce a slightly worse model but a run that will not move at all.

5. Where 2/L is exact, and where it is only a guide

Everything above was derived on a quadratic, and the honest scope of the result matters. The accompanying program checks the analytic bound against a measured one, twice.

On least squares the Hessian is XTX/n, the same matrix everywhere, so the bound is exact and the two routes agree to one per cent. On logistic regression they do not, and the reason is measurable: the logistic Hessian carries weights p(1−p), which shrink towards zero as the model grows confident. Its largest eigenvalue falls from 5.196 × 10-1 at the start to 3.066 × 10-1 at the optimum, a factor of 1.7. The bound moved while we were walking towards it, so the estimate taken at the start is conservative rather than wrong.

A threshold derived on a quadratic is exact on a quadratic and a guide everywhere else. This is worth carrying beyond this page: the first time a real run survives a learning rate the formula forbade, the formula was not lying, it was quoting a bound from a point you have since left.

6. The three schedules, and where each comes from

The learning-rate range test is the practical counterpart: run a few hundred steps while raising η geometrically and watch where the loss turns up. That turning point is a measurement of 2/L on the surface you are actually on, which is exactly the quantity you cannot compute directly on a real model.

code/0103-the-learning-rate-is-bounded-by-curvature.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def run(design, target, gradient_of, loss_of, learning_rate, steps=400):
    """Return the final loss, or inf if it blew up."""
    theta = np.zeros(design.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        theta = theta - learning_rate * gradient_of(design, target, theta)
        if not np.all(np.isfinite(theta)) or np.max(np.abs(theta)) > 1e12:
            return np.inf
    value = loss_of(design, target, theta)
    return value if np.isfinite(value) else np.inf

The program walks this over a ladder of step sizes expressed as multiples of the analytic bound, so the column that matters is the multiple rather than the raw number. It prints the four-regime table, the raw-against-standardised comparison, and the curvature measured at the optimum.

The second names where oscillation begins, which is a real boundary and a different one: between 1/L and 2/L the run overshoots and still converges. Why does standardising features usually make gradient descent faster?
Unequal scales stretch the loss surface, the largest curvature caps the step for every direction, and standardising shrinks that spread. The first is false: standardising changes no dimensions. The second overclaims, since it reduces the zigzag rather than removing it, as the contour figure shows. The third is what whitening or PCA does; standardising equalises variances and leaves correlations exactly where they were.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A quadratic objective has a Hessian whose eigenvalues are

λ = ( 40, 4, 0.4 ) Read as: the curvature along three perpendicular directions. The surface is forty times more curved along the first than along the third, and the middle one sits between them.

Find the largest converging step size, the condition number, the per-step contraction ((κ−1)/(κ+1))2 under exact line search, and roughly how many steps are needed to reduce the objective by 10-6. Then say what happens to that step count if preprocessing brings κ down to 10.

Hint

Only two of the three eigenvalues do any work, and they are not the two you might reach for. The step limit needs the largest alone. The condition number needs the largest and the smallest. The middle eigenvalue is a decoy and appears in neither answer.

Show the worked solution
  1. Largest converging step. 2/λmax = 2 ÷ 40 = 0.05.
  2. Condition number. κ = 40 ÷ 0.4 = 100.
  3. Contraction per step. ((100 − 1)/(100 + 1))2 = (99/101)2 = 0.9608.
  4. Steps to gain six digits. Solve 0.9608k = 10-6, so k = log(10-6) ÷ log(0.9608) = 345.
  5. Now with κ = 10. The contraction becomes (9/11)2 = 0.6694.
  6. New step count. log(10-6) ÷ log(0.6694) = 34.
  7. Read the result. Ten times better conditioning, ten times fewer steps. The relationship between κ and the iteration count is roughly linear, which is why an order of magnitude in one is an order of magnitude in the other.

Sanity check. Your contraction factor must lie strictly between 0 and 1. If it comes out negative you have subtracted after squaring rather than before; if it exceeds 1 you have the ratio inverted. Either mistake makes the step count come out negative, which is the tell. Also check that the middle eigenvalue appears nowhere in your working: if it does, you have used an average where a maximum or a ratio of extremes was needed.
