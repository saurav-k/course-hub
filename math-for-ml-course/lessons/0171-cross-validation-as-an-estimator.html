Your validation score is itself an estimate

frontier 14 min The number everyone quotes, treated the way lesson 161 taught you to treat any estimator.

The one-minute version

  • K-fold estimates the error of a model trained on n(K-1)/K rows, not on all n.
  • That gap is its bias, and the learning curve predicts it before you run anything.
  • Any step that learns from the labels must sit inside the fold loop, or the score is fiction.
  • How badly? Pure noise, true error 50 per cent, and screening outside the loop reports 0.00 per cent.
  • The spread across folds is not a standard error, and no universal unbiased one exists.

1. The reframe

You split the rows into K parts, train on K-1 of them, score the part you held back, rotate, and average. The number that comes out gets quoted as though it were a property of your model.

It is not. It is an estimate, produced by a procedure, and lesson 161 already told you what to ask of any estimate: what is it aiming at, is it centred there, and how much does it move. This page asks those three questions about the score itself.

The reframing Cross-validation does not measure your model. It estimates the average error of models like yours, trained on datasets slightly smaller than yours. Both of those words are doing work.

2. What it is aiming at

Err = E[ L(Y, f̂(X)) ] Read as: the expected loss L of the prediction f̂(X) against the truth Y, averaged over both a fresh test point and a fresh training set. The hat on f marks a model fitted from data rather than the true function.

Each fold's score is honest about its own fold: those rows were not used to fit the model that scores them. Averaging over folds averages over K such fits. So the quantity being estimated is the expected error over training sets of size about n(K-1)/K, and the model you will actually ship was trained on n.

3. The bias, predicted before it is measured

If the learning curve is still falling at your sample size, training on fewer rows means a worse model, so K-fold reports an error higher than the model you ship would have. If the curve has flattened, the gap closes. That is the whole mechanism, and it is checkable.

Read that figure the right way round. The temptation is to compare the K-fold number against the error of a model trained on all 200 rows and call the difference a defect. It is not a defect. K-fold never claimed to estimate that quantity, and the rings show it hitting what it did claim, to within a rounding error.

The practical consequence, and it is the opposite of the usual worry. On a small dataset with a still-falling learning curve, a 5-fold score is pessimistic about the model you will ship, sometimes by a lot. On a large one it hardly matters. Whether that pessimism is a problem depends on what you are using the number for: to choose between two models it mostly cancels, to promise a stakeholder an accuracy it does not.

4. The variance, and where this page parts from the folklore

The standard advice is five or ten folds, and the reason usually given is that leave-one-out is almost unbiased but highly variable. The first half of that is visible in the figure above. The second half is not something this page can show you, and rather than assert it anyway, here is what the measurement actually says.

Drawing forty fresh 200-row datasets and cross-validating each, the spread of the estimate is 1.335 at K = 5, 1.318 at K = 10, 1.312 at K = 20 and 1.282 at leave-one-out. The spread barely moves, and leave-one-out is if anything the tightest, because it trains on the most data.

That is not a refutation of the textbooks, it is a limit on this experiment. The spread measured here is dominated by which two hundred rows were drawn. The variance the recommendation is about is a different component, and Bengio and Grandvalet decompose the total into three of them, only one of which a crude re-draw can see. Their main theorem is that no universal unbiased estimator of that variance exists at all. Take the bias story from this page, where it is measured. Take the variance story on citation.

5. The failure that costs the most

Everything above assumes the fold loop wraps every step that learns. When it does not, the score stops being an estimate of anything.

Here is the standard demonstration, and the numbers are from the program below. Fifty samples. Five thousand predictors, every one of them standard-normal noise. Labels drawn independently of all of them, so no classifier can beat a coin and the true error is exactly 50 per cent.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because "leakage" gets used as a vague word. The screening step ranked five thousand predictors using every row, including the rows each fold later held back. The hundred survivors won partly because they happened to match the labels of those held-back rows. Removing the rows after the selection does not undo their vote.

You can see the vote. Measured on one held-out fold, the pre-selected predictors correlate with its labels at a mean of 0.4121, against 0.2732 for predictors that never saw it. Both are large, because ten rows give noisy correlations; the difference between them is the leak, in a number.

The rule Ask of every step: does this learn anything from the data? Standardising learns a mean and a scale. Feature selection learns a ranking. Imputation learns a fill value. Every one of them belongs inside the fold loop, and a pipeline object exists in every library precisely so you cannot forget.

6. Why the fold spread is not a standard error

Having averaged K numbers, the reflex is to take their standard deviation, divide by √K, and quote an interval. Lesson 160 licensed that move for independent draws, and these are not independent: the training sets of any two folds share most of their rows.

On the same 200 rows, the ten fold scores have a naive standard error of 0.8661, while the actual spread of the estimate across sixty re-partitions is 0.2439. The two numbers are not measuring the same thing, and the naive one is not a conservative version of the other, it is a different quantity.

7. Worked in full

Ten measurements, five folds of two, and the simplest model there is: predict the mean of whatever rows you trained on. Small enough to check every step by hand.

CVK = (1/K) ∑k=1..K MSEk Read as: the cross-validation score is the plain average of the K fold scores, where MSEk is the mean squared error on the rows held out in fold k, from a model fitted on all the other rows.
  1. Write the data. 4, 6, 5, 9, 7, 3, 8, 6, 5, 7. The total is 60, so the mean of all ten is 6.0.
  2. Fold 1 holds out 4 and 6. The other eight sum to 50, so the model predicts 50 ÷ 8 = 6.25. Errors (4 - 6.25)² = 5.0625 and (6 - 6.25)² = 0.0625, so MSE₁ = 2.5625.
  3. Fold 2 holds out 5 and 9. The rest sum to 46, giving 5.75. Errors 0.5625 and 10.5625, so MSE₂ = 5.5625.
  4. Fold 3 holds out 7 and 3. The rest sum to 50, giving 6.25. Errors 0.5625 and 10.5625, so MSE₃ = 5.5625.
  5. Fold 4 holds out 8 and 6. The rest sum to 46, giving 5.75. Errors 5.0625 and 0.0625, so MSE₄ = 2.5625.
  6. Fold 5 holds out 5 and 7. The rest sum to 48, giving 6.0. Errors 1.0 and 1.0, so MSE₅ = 1.0.
  7. Average the five. (2.5625 + 5.5625 + 5.5625 + 2.5625 + 1.0) ÷ 5 = 3.45.

Sanity check. Score the model on the rows it trained on instead: predict 6.0 for everything and the error is 3.00. The cross-validated 3.45 is larger, and it has to be. A held-out row was never allowed to pull the prediction towards itself.

What changes if you compute each fold's prediction from the overall mean of all ten rather than from its own training rows? Every fold predicts 6.0, the score becomes exactly 3.00, and you have reproduced section 5's leak in miniature: the held-out rows helped choose the number they were then scored against.

What it means. The 0.45 gap between 3.45 and 3.00 is the price of an honest answer on ten rows. It is not error your model added; it is error the training score was hiding.

8. The program

# WRONG: screen on all the data, then cross-validate the survivors.
corr = np.array([abs(np.corrcoef(x[:, j], y)[0, 1]) for j in range(p)])
chosen = np.argsort(corr)[-keep:]
x_screened = x[:, chosen]
wrong = [nearest_neighbour_error(...) for h in fold_indices(n, k, rng)]

# RIGHT: screen inside the loop, using only the training rows of each fold.
right = []
for held in fold_indices(n, k, rng):
    mask = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
    mask[held] = False
    c = np.array([abs(np.corrcoef(x[mask, j], y[mask])[0, 1]) for j in range(p)])
    pick = np.argsort(c)[-keep:]
    right.append(nearest_neighbour_error(x[mask][:, pick], y[mask],
                                         x[held][:, pick], y[held]))

assert wrong_error < right_error - 0.15, (
    "the wrong pipeline should report a materially lower error than the right one"
)

The two blocks differ by four lines and by 62 percentage points of reported accuracy. The assertion is there so the demonstration cannot quietly stop demonstrating: if a future change makes the leak disappear, the program fails rather than printing something reassuring. The full file also measures the learning curve, the bias table and the variance honestly, and it prints every number this page quotes.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You screen 5,000 features for label correlation, keep the best 100, then run 5-fold cross-validation. What is wrong?
The screening ranked every predictor using every row, so the survivors were chosen partly because they matched the labels of rows each fold later held back. On labels that are pure noise this reports 0.00 per cent error against a true 50. The first three name real concerns that would each shift a score by a point or two; none of them produces a fifty-point error, and the size of the gap is how you tell them apart.
A 5-fold score on 200 rows reads worse than the model you ship performs. Why is that expected?
K-fold estimates the error at the training size it actually used, and on a still-falling learning curve a model trained on 160 rows is genuinely worse than one trained on 200. Option 2 invents an error that averaging does not introduce. Option 3 describes variance, and this is a bias. Option 4 is the tempting one and it is false: held-out scoring is unbiased for the training size it used, which is exactly what the figure in section 3 shows.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

You have 200 labelled examples and a pipeline of three steps: standardise every column, keep the 20 features most correlated with the label, then fit a classifier.

Write the correct 5-fold procedure, saying which steps sit inside the fold loop and why. Then: the run reports a mean accuracy of 0.86 with a per-fold standard deviation of 0.04. May you report 0.86 ± 0.036 as a 95 per cent interval?

Hint

Ask of each step: does it learn anything from the data? Then ask what 0.04 ÷ √5 × 1.96 assumes about the five numbers it is combining.

Show the worked solution
  1. Split first. Divide the 200 rows into 5 folds before touching anything else.
  2. Standardise inside. Fit the mean and scale on the four training folds only, then apply them to the held-out fold. Fitting on all 200 leaks the held-out rows' mean and scale into the transform.
  3. Select inside. Rank features using the training folds' labels only. This is the step section 5 is about, and it is the expensive one to get wrong.
  4. Fit and score. Train the classifier on the transformed, selected training folds and score the held-out fold. Average the five scores.
  5. The interval. No. That arithmetic assumes the five scores are independent draws, and they are not: any two of the five training sets share three of their four parts. The naive figure can overstate or understate the true spread, so it is not conservative either.

Sanity check. If moving the selection step inside the loop changes your score by nothing at all, the selection is not learning from the labels and you should check it is doing anything. A step that cannot leak is a step that cannot help.

Your teacher. If the leak still feels abstract, ask an agent to run the section 5 setup with keep set to 1 instead of 100: a single pure-noise predictor, chosen because it best matched all fifty labels, and watch it still beat chance on the folds. One column is enough, which is the part that surprises people.
