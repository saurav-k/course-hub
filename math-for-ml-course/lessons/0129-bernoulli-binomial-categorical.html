Three distributions that are one idea at three widths

working 12 min One trial, n trials, one of k outcomes - and the output layer of nearly every classifier. · Hajek, ECE 313, sections 2.4.3 and 2.4.4

The one-minute version

  • Bernoulli is one trial with two outcomes and one parameter p. Its variance p(1−p) peaks at p = 0.5.
  • Binomial counts the successes in n independent trials at the same p. Both of those words are load-bearing.
  • Categorical widens a Bernoulli to k states. A softmax head outputs one of these.
  • At p = 0.5 the binomial is a symmetric hump; at p = 0.004 it is a decaying staircase peaking at zero.
  • In the dropout paper p is the probability of keeping a unit. In PyTorch it is the probability of dropping one.

1. Bernoulli: one trial

P(X = 1) = p,   P(X = 0) = 1 − p,   compactly   P(X = x) = px(1 − p)1−x X: a random variable taking only the values 0 and 1. p: the one parameter, the probability of the outcome labelled 1. The compact form works because raising to the power 0 gives 1, so each case picks out one factor. Mean p, variance p(1 − p) - both are Module 08's to derive, and here they are facts to use.

The variance is worth sitting with. It is 0 at p = 0 and at p = 1, where the outcome is certain, and largest at p = 0.5. A fair coin is the hardest coin to predict, and the variance is the arithmetic saying so.

On the module's log, cache_hit has p = 0.24928 with variance 0.18714, and flagged has p = 0.00396 with variance 0.00394. A rare event has almost no variance, because it is almost always the same.

2. Binomial: n trials, counted

P(X = k) = C(n, k) × pk × (1 − p)n−k,   for k = 0, 1, …, n n: how many trials. k: how many of them succeeded, which is what X counts. p: the success probability, the same on every trial. C(n, k): the number of ways to choose which k of the n trials were the successes, built in Module 01.

Where the formula comes from

What is assumed: the trials are independent, and every trial has the same p. Both assumptions are used, and each is used exactly once.

The shape: price one particular sequence, count how many sequences qualify, add them up.

  1. Fix one specific sequence with k successes and n − k failures, say 1101 for n = 4, k = 3.
  2. Price it. Because the trials are independent, the probability of that exact sequence is the product of the per-trial probabilities: p multiplied k times, and (1 − p) multiplied n − k times.
  3. Notice what the price does not depend on. Because p is the same on every trial, that product depends only on how many successes there were, not on which positions they occupied. Every qualifying sequence costs the same.
  4. Count the sequences. There are C(n, k) ways to choose which trials succeeded.
  5. Add them. The sequences are mutually exclusive, so axiom P.2 adds C(n, k) copies of one number.
The step that does the real work is step 2, and independence is spent there and nowhere else. Step 3 is where the constant-p assumption is spent. Drop either and the derivation stops: without independence you cannot multiply, and without constant p the sequences have different prices and cannot be counted and multiplied. That it sums to 1 follows from the binomial theorem: summing C(n,k) pk(1−p)n−k over all k is the expansion of (p + (1 − p))n = 1n = 1, which is the check that no arrangement was counted twice or missed.

4. Categorical: one trial, k states

Widen a Bernoulli from two outcomes to k and you get the categorical distribution, also called the multinoulli. Exactly one of the k states occurs, and the parameter is a vector of k probabilities summing to 1.

The naming genuinely trips people, and the Deep Learning book says so directly: "Many texts use the term multinomial to refer to multinoulli distributions without clarifying that they are referring only to the n = 1 case." A multinomial counts n categorical draws; a categorical is one draw.

5. Where this bites: these are your output layers

  • A sigmoid head produces a Bernoulli parameter. The Deep Learning book says exactly this: the logistic sigmoid is used to produce the parameter of a Bernoulli because its range is (0, 1).
  • A softmax head produces a categorical parameter vector. Positive, and summing to 1, which is what Lesson 121 checked against the axioms.
  • A dropout mask is one Bernoulli per unit.
  • The number of positives in a minibatch is binomial, which the worked example below computes.
  • A class prior is a Bernoulli or a categorical, depending on how many classes there are.
The convention trap, and it costs real experiments. In the dropout paper the mask is drawn as Bernoulli(p) where p is the probability of a unit being kept, and the reported settings are p = 0.5 for hidden units and p = 0.8 for input units. PyTorch's nn.Dropout(p) documents p as the "probability of an element to be zeroed", which is the opposite. So the paper's input-layer setting is written nn.Dropout(0.2), not nn.Dropout(0.8). Read the docstring, not the letter.

6. Worked: why a rare class needs oversampling

The log has 99 flagged requests among 25,000, so p = 0.00396. Take minibatches of 256 and let X count the flagged requests in a batch.

  1. Check the assumptions. Rows drawn with replacement are independent, and every row has the same flag probability. Both hold, so the binomial applies. (Drawing without replacement breaks the first, as Lesson 122 showed, though at 25,000 rows the difference is in the sixth decimal.)
  2. Compute P(X = 0). C(256, 0) = 1 and p0 = 1, so this is just (1 − 0.00396)256 = 0.3621.
  3. Read it. 36 percent of minibatches contain no flagged request at all.
  4. Compute P(X = 1). C(256, 1) = 256, so 256 × 0.00396 × (0.99604)255 = 0.3686.
  5. Add up what that means. 0.3621 + 0.3686 = 0.7307, so nearly three quarters of batches contain at most one example of the class you care about.
  6. Find the mean. n × p = 256 × 0.00396 = 1.0138 flagged requests per batch.

Sanity check. The first four probabilities are 0.3621, 0.3686, 0.1868 and 0.0629, totalling 0.9804, so the remaining mass above k = 3 must be about 0.02. If the four had summed past 1, the arithmetic was wrong.

What changes at batch 1,024. P(X = 0) falls from 0.3621 to 0.0172 and the mean rises to 4.055. Quadrupling the batch quadrupled the mean and cut the empty-batch rate twenty-one fold, because that rate is exponential in n while the mean is only linear. There is also a neat identity hiding here: doubling the batch squares the empty probability, and indeed 0.36212 = 0.1311, which is exactly P(X = 0) at batch 512.

7. The formula as written, and the formula as used

code/0129-bernoulli-binomial-categorical.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def binomial_from_the_definition(n, k, p):
    """C(n, k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k), with the coefficient written out in full."""
    coefficient = factorial(n) / (factorial(k) * factorial(n - k))
    return coefficient * (p ** k) * ((1 - p) ** (n - k))


def binomial_by_recurrence(n, k, p):
    """Start at P(X = 0) = (1-p)^n and step upward. No factorial is formed."""
    value, odds = (1 - p) ** n, p / (1 - p)
    for j in range(k):
        value *= (n - j) / (j + 1) * odds
    return value

At n = 256 the two agree to machine precision. The program then pushes both until they break, and they break differently: at n = 5000 and k = 2500 the coefficient C(5000, 2500) has 1,504 digits and cannot become a float at all, while the recurrence returns 0.00000000, which is underflow rather than an answer, because it starts from 0.55000. The true value is about 0.0113 and reaching it needs log space, which is Module 10. Two routes, two different breaking points, and the honest lesson is that neither is safe everywhere.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

The dropout paper reports p = 0.8 for input units. What does writing nn.Dropout(0.8) actually do?
PyTorch documents p as the probability of an element being zeroed, so 0.8 drops four units in five. The paper means p as the keep probability, so its setting is nn.Dropout(0.2). The first two options both describe the paper's intent correctly and neither describes what the code does, which is exactly why this costs experiments. The third has it backwards: dropout is active in training and disabled at evaluation.
Which binomial assumption fails when a sampler draws a minibatch without replacement?
Removing a row changes the pool the next draw sees, so the draws are dependent and step 2 of the derivation no longer holds. The first option is tempting because the pool does shift, but the honest failure is the multiplication in step 2, and the resulting distribution is the hypergeometric. The third and fourth still hold perfectly well: each row is still flagged or not, and the batch size is still fixed.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Same file, same flag rate of p = 0.00396, but now a batch size of 512.

Find the probability a batch contains no flagged request, and the expected number of flags. Then work out roughly how large the batch must be for the empty-batch probability to fall below 5 percent.

Hint

The first is a single power. For the third, set (1 − p)n = 0.05 and take logarithms of both sides.

Show the worked solution
  1. Empty batch at 512. (1 − 0.00396)512 = 0.1311.
  2. Expected flags. 512 × 0.00396 = 2.0275.
  3. Set up the threshold. Solve (0.99604)n = 0.05 for n.
  4. Take logs. n × ln(0.99604) = ln(0.05), so n = −2.9957 ÷ −0.003968 = 755.
  5. Round up sensibly. About 760 rows per batch, so 1,024 comfortably clears it and 512 does not.

Sanity check. The answer at 512 must sit between the 256 value of 0.3621 and the 1,024 value of 0.0172, and it does. Better still, it must be exactly the square of the 256 value, because doubling the batch squares the empty probability: 0.3621 × 0.3621 = 0.1311. If your answer is not that square, recheck the exponent.

Your teacher. Whenever you meet a binomial in the wild, say the two assumptions out loud and ask whether they hold: are the trials independent, and is p really the same on every one. Conversion rates that drift over a week break the second; users who influence each other break the first. Ask an agent to compute the binomial probabilities for a batch of 64 at this page's p, and check by hand that they sum to something just under 1.
