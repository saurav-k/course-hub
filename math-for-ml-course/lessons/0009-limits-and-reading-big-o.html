A bound is a claim about growth, not about your data

foundation 12 min The last page of the on-ramp, and the one a reader in a hurry can skip.

The one-minute version

  • Terms going to zero is necessary and nowhere near sufficient for a series to converge, and the harmonic series is the counterexample.
  • A learning-rate schedule has to satisfy two conditions pulling opposite ways, so "smaller is safer" is wrong in both directions at once.
  • (1 − 1/n)n → 1/e is why a bootstrap sample leaves out about 37% of the data.
  • O is order at most, and using it as a lower bound is the misuse Knuth wrote a letter to stop. O(f) is a set, and the equals sign means "is in".
  • An asymptotic bound never settles a race, because it hides constants and hides which variable is growing.

1. A sequence, and what converging means

A sequence is a list with an index: a1, a2, a3, …. It converges to a limit if its tail gets close to that limit and stays close. The word "stays" is the whole definition: a sequence that touches 5 repeatedly and wanders off in between has not converged to 5.

There is a formal version with epsilons, and this course does not need it. What it needs is the reading habit, and you already have two sequences to practise on: a loss curve, and a learning-rate schedule.

2. A series, and the trap

A series is the running total of a sequence. Whether that total settles is a different question from whether the terms shrink, and conflating the two is the most common error in this territory.

The general result decides both cases at once.

The p-series test Σk≥1 1/kp converges when p > 1 and diverges when p ≤ 1.

Proof, by grouping. Take p = 1 first. Group the terms into blocks of length 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on. The block beginning at 1/(2m+1) holds 2m terms, each at least 1/2m+1, so the block totals at least 1/2. There are infinitely many blocks, each adding at least a half, so no finite total is possible. For p < 1 every term is larger still, so those diverge too.

For p > 1, group the same way but bound from above: the block beginning at 1/(2m)p holds 2m terms each at most 1/(2m)p, so it totals at most (21−p)m. Since p > 1 that ratio is below 1, and a geometric series with ratio below 1 has a finite total.

The step that does the work The grouping itself. It replaces a sum nobody can evaluate with a geometric series everybody can, and the same trick works in both directions - from below to force divergence, from above to force convergence.

An honest boundary: this is the engineering proof for the two cases the course needs. The general statement is proved with the integral test, which needs M05, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

3. Why a learning rate has two conditions, not one

Stochastic gradient descent takes noisy steps, so its step size has to do two incompatible-sounding things. The Deep Learning textbook states the pair as equations 8.12 and 8.13.

Σk=1∞ εk = ∞    and    Σk=1∞ εk2 < ∞ Read as: the step sizes must add up to infinity, and their squares must add up to something finite. εk is the learning rate at step k. The first says the steps can still carry you anywhere, however far away the answer is. The second says the noise in each step dies out fast enough to settle.

Now put the p-series test to work. For εk = C/kp, the first condition needs p ≤ 1 and the second needs 2p > 1. Both hold exactly when 0.5 < p ≤ 1.

That last row is the one worth sitting with. A schedule that decays too aggressively fails, and it fails by stalling before it arrives. "Smaller is safer" is wrong at both ends of the range, and the two conditions are what tell you where the safe middle is.

These are sufficient conditions in the convex stochastic-approximation setting, which is not a deep network. They tell you which schedules are ruled out, not that a surviving one will converge on a real loss surface. The book is careful about this and so is this page.

4. e as a limit, and the bootstrap's 37%

Draw n rows with replacement from a dataset of n rows. What fraction never gets picked?

  1. One row, one draw. The chance a particular row is not chosen on one draw is 1 − 1/n.
  2. One row, all draws. The draws are independent, so the chance it is missed every time is (1 − 1/n)n.
  3. Take logs, because the exponent is moving. ln[(1 − 1/n)n] = n ln(1 − 1/n) by the power rule from lesson 06.
  4. Substitute. Write h = 1/n, so this is ln(1 − h)/h with h → 0. The series ln(1 − h) = −h − h2/2 − … gives −1 − h/2 − …, which tends to −1.
  5. Undo the log. exp is continuous, so the quantity itself tends to e−1 = 0.3679.

The step that does the work is step 3. Taking the logarithm first turns an exponent that is itself changing into an ordinary product, which is lesson 06 paying for itself. An honest boundary: step 4 uses a series expansion this course states rather than derives.

Breiman states the consequence plainly: "Each bootstrap sample leaves out about 37% of the examples." The 0.3679 above is this course's own derivation of that number, and the convergence is quick enough to be practical.

5. Knuth's three symbols

In April 1976 Knuth wrote to SIGACT News because people were misusing O, and his complaint is still the most common error in applied writing: rejecting a sorting method "because its running time is O(n2)". That sentence uses an upper bound as though it were a lower one.

His definitions, in his words: O(f(n)) is "order at most f(n)", Ω(f(n)) is "order at least", and Θ(f(n)) is "order exactly".

Knuth is explicit about the notation: "The equal sign here really means ⊆ (set inclusion)". This is lesson 02 arriving in an unexpected place - a set statement wearing an equals sign.

6. Why a bound never settles a race

The transformer paper's own Table 1 gives self-attention O(n2·d) per layer and a recurrent layer O(n·d2), and section 4 draws the conclusion: self-attention is faster "when the sequence length n is smaller than the representation dimensionality d". Setting the two equal gives n = d, and the paper's base model has dmodel = 512. So the crossing is at 512 tokens, and at n = 2,048 attention costs exactly four times as much.

A second thing a bound hides. The base of a logarithm vanishes inside O, because logb N = ln N / ln b differs by a constant factor and constants are what O discards. At N = one million, N / log N is 72,382 with natural logs and 50,172 with base 2. Different numbers, same class - which is lesson 06's change-of-base rule showing up one last time.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A reviewer rejects a sorting method "because its running time is O(n2)". What is wrong with the sentence?
Saying a method is O(n squared) says it is no worse than that, which is not grounds for rejection - it might also be O(n log n). The third option is closer than it looks and is still wrong: Theta would make the sentence meaningful, but the defect is the direction of the bound rather than its tightness. The second and fourth are simply false. This is the exact sentence Knuth wrote his 1976 letter to stop.
Both 1/k and 1/k2 have terms going to zero. Which sum converges?
The p-series test settles it: p = 2 converges and p = 1 does not. The first option is the misconception this page exists for, and terms going to zero is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. The second is false, since one of them settles at pi squared over six. The fourth has it exactly backwards, and the harmonic series is the standard counterexample.

Practice about 20 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

(a) For εk = C/kp, for which p do both of the conditions in section 3 hold? Check p = 0, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.
(b) A brute-force nearest-neighbour search costs O[D N2] and a tree-based one O[D N log N]. With N = 1,000,000, roughly how many times fewer distance calculations? Does the base of the log change your answer?
(c) At what sequence length does a self-attention layer cost four times a recurrent layer, with d = 512?

Hint

For (a), apply the p-series test twice: once to εk, and once to εk2, whose exponent is 2p rather than p.

Show the worked solution
  1. Apply the test to the first condition. Σ 1/kp must diverge, which the test says happens exactly when p ≤ 1.
  2. Apply it to the second. εk2 = C2/k2p, so that series must converge, which needs 2p > 1, that is p > 0.5.
  3. Intersect. Both hold exactly when 0.5 < p ≤ 1. So p = 0 and p = 0.5 fail the second, and p = 0.75 and p = 1 pass.
  4. Take the ratio for (b). D N2 over D N log N is N / log N, and the D cancels.
  5. Evaluate it twice. 106/ln(106) = 72,382, and 106/log2(106) = 50,172.
  6. Answer the second half of (b). The base changes the number and not the class, because the two logs differ by the constant factor ln 2, and a constant factor is exactly what O discards. Roughly 104 to 105 times fewer is the honest answer.
  7. Set up (c). n2d = 4 n d2, so n = 4d = 2,048 tokens.

Sanity check. In (a) the two conditions must pull in opposite directions and leave a band, not a single value - if your answer admits a constant learning rate, you have only checked one of them. In (b), a bound that hides a constant cannot give a single exact number, so an answer quoted to five significant figures is claiming more than it knows.

Your teacher. That is the end of the on-ramp. From here every module assumes you can read an index, a set, a function, an argmin, a logarithm and a bound - which is most of what stops people reading papers, and none of which was the actual mathematics. If the divergence of the harmonic series still feels wrong, ask an agent to "sum 1/k until the total passes 20 and tell me how many terms that took" - the answer is in the hundreds of millions, and that is why it looks convergent on every plot you will ever draw.
