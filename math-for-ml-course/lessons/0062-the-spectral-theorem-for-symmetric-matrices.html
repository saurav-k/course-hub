Symmetric matrices are the well-behaved ones

working 14 min The theorem the rest of this module stands on, with its proof.

The one-minute version

  • A real symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues, with no complex surprises.
  • Its eigenvectors can always be chosen perpendicular to each other, and there are always enough of them.
  • So it factors as A = QΛQT, where the change of basis is a rotation you undo by transposing.
  • Perpendicular eigenvectors come from symmetry, not from being an eigenvector. A general matrix does not get them.
  • Nearly every matrix machine learning hands you is symmetric, so this is the common case rather than a special one.

1. What a general matrix does not promise

Lessons 60 and 61 left three open disappointments. A matrix can have complex eigenvalues, as the quarter-turn does. Its eigenvectors need not be perpendicular. And it may not have enough of them to describe every direction. Symmetry removes all three at once.

A matrix is symmetric when it equals its own transpose, so the entries mirror across the diagonal. That is the whole hypothesis.

2. The theorem

The spectral theorem Let A be a real square matrix with A = AT. Then every eigenvalue of A is real, and the space has an orthonormal basis made entirely of eigenvectors of A. Equivalently A = QΛQT with Q orthogonal and Λ real and diagonal. Conversely, any matrix of that form is symmetric.
A = Q Λ QT Read as: Q holds the orthonormal eigenvectors as its columns, so QTQ is the identity. Λ is diagonal and carries the eigenvalues. Because Q is orthogonal, its inverse is simply its transpose, which is why no inversion appears anywhere in the formula.

3. The proof, in three parts

Part 1, the eigenvalues are real. Let Av = λv with v non-zero, allowing complex entries for the moment, and write v* for the conjugate transpose. Then v*Av = λ(v*v). The number v*v is a sum of squared magnitudes, so it is real and strictly positive. The number v*Av is also real, because its conjugate transpose is v*ATv, which is v*Av again since A is real and symmetric, and a single number equal to its own conjugate is real. So lambda is a real number over a positive real number, hence real. Being real, A - λI is a real singular matrix and a real eigenvector can be chosen.

Part 2, eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular. Let Av = λv and Aw = μw with lambda and mu different. Compute vTAw two ways. Substituting for Aw gives μ(vTw). Using symmetry instead, vTAw = (ATv)Tw = (Av)Tw = λ(vTw). So (λ - μ)(vTw) = 0, and since the eigenvalues differ, vTw = 0.

Part 3, there are enough of them. Work by induction on the size. For a one-by-one there is nothing to prove. Otherwise part 1 supplies a real unit eigenvector q with real eigenvalue lambda. Let W be every vector perpendicular to q. The key step is that A carries W into itself: for w in W,

(Aw)Tq = wTATq = wTAq = λ(wTq) = 0 Read as: take any vector w perpendicular to the eigenvector q. The first step transposes, the second uses symmetry, the third uses that q is an eigenvector, and the last uses that w was perpendicular to q to begin with. The result is zero, meaning Aw is perpendicular to q as well, so it is still inside W.

So A restricted to W is again symmetric, and W is one dimension smaller. By the inductive hypothesis W has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and adding q gives one for the whole space.

The converse is one line. If A = QΛQT then AT = (QΛQT)T = QΛTQT = QΛQT = A, because a diagonal matrix is its own transpose.

The step that does the real work Part 3's invariance argument. It is what turns "there is at least one eigenvector" into "there are enough of them, and they are perpendicular". It uses symmetry at exactly one point, the second equality above, and that is precisely where a non-symmetric matrix fails. The figure in section 1 shows the failure: for [[4, 1], [2, 3]] the two eigendirections have dot product 0.3162, not zero.
The honest boundary. Part 1 used complex vectors to prove a statement entirely about real numbers, which is the standard route rather than an accident. The complex analogue, where A equals its own conjugate transpose, is true with the same proof and this course does not need it. Part 3 also assumed an eigenvalue exists at all, which over the complex numbers is the fundamental theorem of algebra, named on lesson 61 and not proved here.

4. Why this is the common case

5. Worked in full

  1. Take the matrix. S = [[5, 2], [2, 2]], which is symmetric.
  2. Invariants first. trace = 7 and det = 10 - 4 = 6, so the characteristic polynomial is λ2 - 7λ + 6.
  3. Solve. The roots are 6 and 1. Both real, as part 1 promised.
  4. First eigenvector. S - 6I = [[-1, 2], [2, -4]], whose rows both say v1 = 2v2, so v = (2, 1).
  5. Second eigenvector. S - I = [[4, 2], [2, 1]], so 2v1 + v2 = 0 and v = (1, -2).
  6. Check perpendicularity. (2)(1) + (1)(-2) = 0, exactly zero, as part 2 requires.
  7. Normalise. Both have length √5, so q1 = (0.894427, 0.447214) and q2 = (0.447214, -0.894427).
  8. Sanity check. Reassemble as a weighted sum of the two rank-one pieces: 6q1q1T + 1q2q2T gives back [[5, 2], [2, 2]] exactly. If it does not, the eigenvectors were not normalised.
What changes if. Break the symmetry by changing one off-diagonal entry, giving [[5, 3], [2, 2]]. Its eigenvalues stay real here, since the trace is 7 and the determinant 4 leave a positive discriminant of 33. But the eigenvectors stop being perpendicular. Realness and perpendicularity are separate promises, and only symmetry buys the second.

6. The spectral sum

Step 8 above is worth naming, because the shape returns twice more in this module. Reassembling a symmetric matrix from its eigenpairs writes it as a stack of weighted rank-one pieces.

A = λ1q1q1T + λ2q2q2T + … + λnqnqnT Read as: each qiqiT is a rank-one matrix that projects onto one eigendirection, and λi says how heavily that direction is weighted. The whole matrix is those layers added up.

Lesson 66 produces the same shape for a matrix that is not symmetric, using two sets of vectors instead of one, and lesson 68 truncates the sum on purpose.

7. Checked at 24 dimensions

code/0062-spectral-theorem.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
eigenvalues, Q = np.linalg.eigh(cov)             # cov is 24 x 24, symmetric

# Promise 1: real eigenvalues, asked of the routine that does NOT assume symmetry
assert np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(cov).imag).max() < 1e-12
# Promise 2: the eigenvectors are orthonormal
assert np.abs(Q.T @ Q - np.eye(24)).max() < 1e-12
# Promise 3: the factorisation rebuilds the matrix
assert np.allclose(Q @ np.diag(eigenvalues) @ Q.T, cov)

All three hold on the 24-channel covariance, with the orthonormality defect at 1.9e-15 and the reassembly error at 1.5e-16. The program then runs the same checks on [[4, 1], [2, 3]] and the second one fails by a wide margin: its eigenvector matrix misses orthonormality by 0.316, which is the dot product the figure in section 1 shows.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which of these is NOT guaranteed for a real symmetric matrix?
Symmetric matrices repeat eigenvalues freely: the identity is symmetric and repeats its eigenvalue n times, and when two eigenvalues coincide the whole plane they span is an eigenspace, so even the directions are not pinned down. The other three are exactly what the theorem promises.
Which matrix is NOT guaranteed to be symmetric?
A layer's weight matrix is usually not even square, let alone symmetric, since it maps one dimension to another. The first two are symmetric by construction, because swapping the indices computes the same sum. The Laplacian is symmetric because D is diagonal and W is symmetric on an undirected graph.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Diagonalise the matrix below orthogonally, and verify your answer by reassembling it from the spectral sum.

S = [[3, 1], [1, 3]] Read as: a symmetric two-by-two matrix. Both diagonal entries are 3 and the repeated off-diagonal entry is 1.
Hint

The trace and the determinant give the characteristic polynomial with no expansion. For a matrix of the shape [[a, b], [b, a]] the two eigenvalues are always a + b and a - b, which you can check against your answer.

Show the worked solution
  1. Read the invariants. trace = 6 and det = 9 - 1 = 8.
  2. Solve. λ2 - 6λ + 8 = 0 gives 4 and 2, matching the hint's 3 + 1 and 3 - 1.
  3. First eigenvector. S - 4I = [[-1, 1], [1, -1]] says v1 = v2, so v = (1, 1).
  4. Second eigenvector. S - 2I = [[1, 1], [1, 1]] says v1 + v2 = 0, so v = (1, -1).
  5. Normalise. Both have length √2, giving q1 = (0.7071, 0.7071) and q2 = (0.7071, -0.7071).

Sanity check. The two eigenvectors have dot product (1)(1) + (1)(-1) = 0, so they are perpendicular as part 2 requires. The eigenvalues satisfy 4 + 2 = 6 and 4 × 2 = 8, reproducing both invariants. And the spectral sum 4q1q1T + 2q2q2T rebuilds [[3, 1], [1, 3]]. If it rebuilds something with the right diagonal but the wrong off-diagonal, the eigenvectors were not normalised.

Your teacher. If part 3 of the proof is the piece that will not stay put, ask an agent to run the invariance argument on a concrete three-by-three symmetric matrix: pick one eigenvector, find two vectors perpendicular to it, and check by hand that the matrix keeps them perpendicular to it. Seeing it happen once with numbers is what makes the induction feel inevitable rather than clever.
