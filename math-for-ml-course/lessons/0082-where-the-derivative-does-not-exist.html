Where the derivative does not exist, and why machine learning ships anyway

working 12 min The page that makes the rectified linear unit and the absolute loss legal.

The one-minute version

  • A kink is not a disqualification. It is a point where the derivative stops being a number and becomes a set.
  • ReLU has no derivative at zero, and every framework returns one of the two one-sided values rather than raising.
  • Setting a derivative to zero gives you the mean. The same move is unavailable for the absolute loss, whose answer is the median.
  • The absolute loss can have a whole interval of minimisers, which is what "the median" quietly names one point of.

1. The most used function in deep learning has a corner

The rectified linear unit is g(z) = max(0, z). It is a straight line of slope 0 to the left of the origin and a straight line of slope 1 to the right. What is its slope at zero?

There are two defensible answers and no way to choose between them, which is precisely the situation the definition in Lesson 80 rules out.

2. One-sided derivatives

left derivative at z: lim ( g(z + h) − g(z) ) ÷ h as h → 0 from below The same difference quotient as Lesson 80, but the step h is only ever negative, so you approach the point from the left. The right derivative is the same thing with h positive. A function is differentiable at the point only if both exist and agree.

For ReLU at zero the left derivative is 0 and the right derivative is 1. Both exist. They disagree. So the derivative does not exist there, and that is a fact rather than a problem.

3. What frameworks do, and why it is fine

Software returns one of the one-sided derivatives rather than reporting that the derivative is undefined or raising an error. Deep Learning says so plainly, and gives the reason that matters:

The reframingTraining never arrives at a point where the gradient is exactly zero. It reduces the loss a great deal and stops. So it is acceptable for the minima of the cost function to sit at points with an undefined gradient, because you were never going to land on one.

The non-differentiable set is also tiny: for ReLU it is a single point out of a continuum, and a pre-activation landing exactly on 0.0 in floating point is a measure-zero event you can ignore.

4. The subgradient, and the two theorems it settles

At a kink, every slope between the one-sided slopes gives a line through the point that stays below the function. That set of slopes is the subdifferential, and the optimality condition becomes "zero belongs to it".

Two results make this concrete, and they are the reason anyone in data science argues about means and medians.

Theorem 1. The mean minimises total squared error, uniquely.

  1. Differentiate. The total squared error is smooth, and its derivative in the summary c is −2 × the sum of (xi − c).
  2. Set it to zero. That sum vanishes exactly when c equals the mean. One equation, one root.
  3. Confirm it is a minimum. The second derivative is 2n, positive for any non-empty sample, so the function is strictly convex and that root is the unique global minimum. QED

Theorem 2. A median minimises total absolute error, and the minimising set can be an interval.

What is assumed: nothing but a finite list of numbers. The shape of the argument: the function is convex, so zero in the subdifferential is both necessary and sufficient; then count points. The step that does the real work is the counting, because it is what turns a calculus condition into an order statistic.

  1. Away from the data, differentiate. Each term contributes +1 when c is above that point and −1 when below, so the derivative is (count below c) − (count above c).
  2. Watch the sign. That count is non-decreasing in c. It is negative while fewer than half the points lie below, and positive once more than half do.
  3. At a data point, take the subdifferential. The one-sided derivatives differ by twice the multiplicity of that value, and every slope between them is a subgradient.
  4. Read the condition. Zero lies in the set exactly when at most half the points lie strictly below and at most half strictly above, which is the definition of a median.
  5. Notice the even case. With an even count and distinct middle values, every c strictly between them has exactly half the points on each side, so the derivative is genuinely zero across the whole open interval, and the endpoints join by continuity. QED

5. Worked: ten days of spending, and two different answers

Ten daily spends in rupees: 400, 350, 500, 420, 8,500, 380, 410, 440, 390, 460. Sorted, the two middle values are 410 and 420.

S(c) = ∑(xi − c)²     A(c) = ∑|xi − c| c: a single number you propose as the typical day. xi: the spend on day i. S: total squared error, which punishes a big miss quadratically. A: total absolute error, which punishes it in proportion. Both are functions of c alone, and the whole question is which c makes each smallest.
  1. Solve the squared loss. The sum of the ten spends is 12,250, so the derivative vanishes at c = 1,225. That is the mean.
  2. Try the same move on the absolute loss. There is nothing to set to zero: the derivative fails to exist at every one of the ten data points.
  3. Count instead. Five values lie below 415 and five above, so the count difference is zero and 415 is a median.
  4. Check the whole interval. Total absolute error is 8,390 at c = 410, at c = 415 and at c = 420. It is 8,392 at 409 and at 421.
  5. Compare the two answers under the absolute loss. 14,550 at the mean against 8,390 at the median. The single Rs 8,500 day drags the mean 810 rupees past every other observation.

Sanity check. The mean of a right-skewed list must exceed its median, and 1,225 exceeds 415. It must also exceed nine of the ten values here, because one value is twenty times the others, and it does. A mean sitting inside the bulk of the data would mean the 8,500 was dropped.

What changes if the Rs 8,500 day becomes Rs 85,000? The median does not move at all: it still sits between the fifth and sixth values. The mean goes from 1,225 to 8,875. The calculus reason is in the two derivatives: the squared loss weights each point by its distance, and the absolute loss weights every point by +1 or −1 no matter how far out it sits.

In words. Choosing squared error is choosing to let the largest day decide what a typical day looks like. That is sometimes right and it is always a choice.

6. The trade-off: an interval is a weaker answer

A minimising interval is a real cost, not a curiosity. Two implementations of the same median can return different numbers, both correct, and a test asserting equality will fail on one of them. Where that matters, say which convention you use. NumPy averages the two middle values for an even count; that is a convention and not a theorem.
code/0082-where-the-derivative-does-not-exist.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def sae_subgradient(x, c):
    """The subdifferential of sum |x_i - c| at c, as a closed interval.

    Ties contribute [-1, +1] each, which is exactly what widens a point
    into an interval.
    """
    below = int(np.sum(x < c))
    above = int(np.sum(x > c))
    ties  = int(np.sum(x == c))
    return float(below - above - ties), float(below - above + ties)


# On 20,000 real session durations:
#   mean 171.7490   median 98.9000
#   subgradient at the median      : [-2, 16]   contains zero
#   subgradient one second below   : [-162, -146]   does not

The program runs on 20,000 session durations rather than ten spends, and it shows the other half of the story. Because those durations are recorded to one decimal, many rows tie at 98.9, so there the minimiser is a single point and it is the subdifferential that is an interval. The ten-number example above is the reverse case. Between them they cover both.

What does a deep learning framework return for the derivative of ReLU at exactly zero?
Software returns a one-sided value rather than reporting the derivative undefined. The first option is what the mathematics might suggest and what no framework does. The third would poison every downstream weight and is exactly the outcome the convention exists to avoid. The fourth sounds principled and is an invention: the midpoint is a subgradient, but it is not what implementations return.
Over the ten spends, total absolute error is smallest at c = 415. What else is true?
Every candidate in the closed interval from 410 to 420 gives exactly 8,390, so the minimiser is an interval. The first option is a true-sounding claim about a different question: the derivative does not exist at 415 because 415 is between two data points but the argument people reach for is the derivative one, and the right statement is that zero lies in the subdifferential. The second is the trap the whole page exists to break. The fourth is the value at the mean, not at the median.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Take the ten daily spends from section 5:

400, 350, 500, 420, 8500, 380, 410, 440, 390, 460 Read as: ten observations of one quantity, in rupees, one per day. Nothing is assumed about how they were generated.

Show that setting the derivative of the squared loss to zero gives the mean. Compute the mean and the median. Evaluate the total absolute error at both. Then explain why the second answer needed a subgradient.

Hint

For the absolute loss, do not differentiate. Pick a candidate and count how many observations lie below it and how many above.

Hint for the last part

Ask where the absolute loss fails to be differentiable, and check whether your candidate is one of those places.

Show the worked solution
  1. Differentiate the squared loss. d/dc ∑(xi − c)² = −2∑(xi − c), which is zero when c = (1/n)∑xi.
  2. Compute the mean. The ten values total 12,250, so the mean is 1,225.
  3. Compute the median. Sorted: 350, 380, 390, 400, 410, 420, 440, 460, 500, 8500. The middle pair is 410 and 420, so 415.
  4. Total absolute error at the mean. 14,550.
  5. Total absolute error at the median. 8,390, and the same at every c in [410, 420].
  6. Why a subgradient was needed. The absolute value is not differentiable at any data point, so "set the derivative to zero" has nothing to act on. The condition that replaces it is that zero lies in the subdifferential, and that holds across the whole interval rather than at one point.

Sanity check. The mean must exceed nine of the ten values, because one observation is twenty times the rest, and 1,225 does. The median must lie between the fifth and sixth sorted values, 410 and 420, and 415 does. If either lands elsewhere, an observation was dropped or the sort is wrong.

Primary source to go deeper

Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, section 6.3, is the honest half-page on this: it states that ReLU's one-sided derivatives at zero are 0 and 1, that software returns one of them, and that because training never reaches a point of zero gradient it is acceptable for minima to sit where the gradient is undefined. For convexity and the first-order condition that makes "zero in the subdifferential" both necessary and sufficient, see Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization, section 3.1. The mean-against-median argument on real data is worked in Statistical Foundations, Part 2, which is where these ten numbers come from.
