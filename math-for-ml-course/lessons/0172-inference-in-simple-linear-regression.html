Module 09 · Estimation, testing, and inference · Lesson 0172

A fitted slope is an estimate, so everything in this module applies to it

frontier 12 min Nothing here is new machinery. It is the module's own machinery, pointed at a slope.

The one-minute version

  • Re-run the experiment and the fitted slope changes, so it is a random variable with a nameable distribution.
  • That distribution is Normal, centred on the true slope, with variance sigma-squared over Sxx, and the proof is four lines.
  • Sigma is unknown, so it is estimated with SSE over n minus 2, and that swap is exactly what turns a z into a t.
  • Sxx is the half of the variance you control. Spreading the predictor out buys precision at no extra sample size.

1. The model, and what is random in it

Yi = α + βxi + ei,  with ei independent Normal(0, σ2) Read as: the response for row i is a true intercept α, plus a true slope β times that row's predictor xi, plus an error ei. The errors are independent, centred on zero, and share one spread σ. The xi are fixed numbers you chose, so all the randomness lives in the errors and therefore in the responses.

Because the responses are random, anything computed from them is random too. Write the estimates as A and B, and the truths they chase as α and β, which you never see.

Least squares is the maximum likelihood estimator here. Lesson 0164 showed that maximising a Gaussian likelihood means minimising a sum of squared deviations from the mean. Swap the constant mean for the line α + βxi and the same argument runs unchanged: those are the only terms carrying the parameters, and they enter the log-likelihood with a negative sign. So minimising Σ(yi - a - bxi)2 is not a separate convention that happens to be convenient. It is the likelihood.

2. Why the noise estimate divides by n minus 2

Lesson 0022 divided by n - 1 because one parameter, the mean, had been estimated from the same rows. Here two have been, so two degrees of freedom are spent. That is a pattern rather than a rule, and it is visible in the residuals themselves: they satisfy Σêi = 0 and Σxiêi = 0, because those two equations are the normal equations least squares solved. So the n residuals are pinned by two linear constraints and live in an n - 2 dimensional space.

3. The slope's sampling distribution, with the proof

Theorem B is Normal with mean β and variance σ2/Sxx, where Sxx = Σ(xi - x̄)2.

The shape of the argument. Write the slope as a fixed set of weights applied to the random responses; then its mean and variance are those of a weighted sum of independent normals, and normality is free.

B = Σi wiYi,  wi = (xi - x̄)/Sxx,  Σwi = 0, Σwixi = 1 Read as: the weights depend only on the predictor values, which are not random, so they are constants. Their summing to zero is what lets the ȳ term drop out; their weighted sum against the x values being one is what makes the estimator land on β rather than a multiple of it.

The mean follows immediately: E[B] = Σwi(α + βxi) = α·0 + β·1 = β, so the estimator is unbiased.

The step that does the real work is the variance, because it is the only place the independence assumption is spent. Independence is what lets the variance of the sum be the sum of the variances, with no covariance terms surviving:

Var(B) = Σi wi2σ2 = σ2Σ(xi - x̄)2/Sxx2 = σ2/Sxx Read as: squaring each weight and summing gives Sxx over Sxx squared, which collapses to one over Sxx. The noise variance divided by the spread of the predictor.

A linear combination of independent normals is normal, which closes it. The program measures the prediction rather than trusting it: over 20,000 simulated experiments the slope's observed spread is 0.068423 against a predicted 0.068876.

Replacing the unknown σ by its estimate widens the tails from a normal into a t on n - 2 degrees of freedom. That is the whole reason a t appears in regression output.

4. The test, on four thousand rows

The program regresses y on x01 in features.csv, where the true slope is 4.0 by construction.

  1. The fit. B = 4.0586 against a true 4.0, and A = -0.0440 against a true 0.
  2. The predictor's spread. Sxx = 4,047.21 across 4,000 rows.
  3. The noise estimate. SSE = 71,771.20, so σ̂2 = 71,771.20 ÷ 3,998 = 17.95.
  4. The standard error. √(17.95 ÷ 4,047.21) = 0.0666.
  5. The statistic. 4.0586 ÷ 0.0666 = 60.94, against a critical value near 1.96.
  6. The interval. 4.0586 ± 1.96 × 0.0666 gives 3.928 to 4.189, which contains the true 4.0.

The same machinery on a predictor that is genuinely nothing. Regress y on x20, whose true coefficient is zero: the slope comes out -0.0574 with a standard error of 0.0926, so t is -0.62 and the test fails to reject, correctly. The procedure was not told which predictor was real.

σ̂2 comes out at 17.95, not the generator's 9.0, and that is right rather than a bug. This is a simple regression on one predictor, so everything the model cannot see lands in the residual: 9.0 + 2.52 + 1.52 + 1.02 + 0.62 = 18.86. In a simple regression σ2 means "everything left out", and here we happen to know exactly what that is.

5. The two intervals that are not the same

mean response: √(1/n + (x₀ - x̄)2/Sxx)   one new observation: √(1 + 1/n + (x₀ - x̄)2/Sxx) Read as: the widths of the two intervals at a new predictor value x₀, each multiplied by the estimated noise and a t critical value. They differ by one term: the extra 1, which is the variance of the new observation's own error.

That extra 1 does not shrink with n. Collect a million rows and the band for the line closes to nothing while the band for one new observation stops at the noise floor, here about ±8.3.

6. Sxx is yours to choose, and the assumptions are not

Everything above rests on errors that are independent, equal in variance, and normal. Normality buys the exact t; without it the test is still roughly valid at large n, by the same Central Limit Theorem this module has leaned on throughout. Independence and equal variance are the two that fail silently, and no number on this page checks either of them. Plotting the residuals against the fitted values is not optional.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

The residual sum of squares is divided by n - 2 rather than by n. What does the 2 pay for?
An intercept and a slope were fitted first, so the residuals obey two linear constraints and live in an n - 2 dimensional space; dividing by n understates the noise, which the bar chart shows as 15.67 against a true 18.86. No data points are dropped at any stage. The t distribution arises because sigma was estimated, so citing it reverses cause and effect. And the two modelling assumptions license the distribution rather than being counted by the degrees of freedom.
The confidence band for the mean response is at its narrowest where along the line?
The width carries a term in (x₀ - x̄) squared, which vanishes at the mean and grows either side, so the band is a waist at x̄ and flares outwards. A constant width would need that term to be absent. The smallest predictor value is typically one of the widest points, not the narrowest. And local density is not in the formula at all: only n, the noise, and the distance from x̄ appear.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Anscombe's first set, from anscombe.csv, gives Sxx = 110.0, a slope of 0.500, n = 11 and SSE = 13.76.

σ̂2 = SSE/(n-2),  se(B) = √(σ̂2/Sxx),  T = B/se(B) Read as: estimate the noise variance with the n minus 2 divisor, take the slope's standard error from it, then divide the slope by that standard error.

Compute all three, compare T against t(9, 0.975) = 2.262, and give the 95 per cent interval for the slope.

Hint

The divisor is n minus 2, not n minus 1. Eleven points and two fitted parameters leave nine degrees of freedom, which is also why the critical value is read at 9.

Show the worked solution
  1. Estimate the noise variance. 13.76 ÷ (11 - 2) = 13.76 ÷ 9 = 1.529.
  2. Divide by the predictor spread. 1.529 ÷ 110.0 = 0.013900.
  3. Take the square root. se(B) = √0.013900 = 0.1179.
  4. Form the statistic. T = 0.500 ÷ 0.1179 = 4.24 on 9 degrees of freedom.
  5. Decide. 4.24 is beyond 2.262, so reject H0: this slope is not plausibly zero.
  6. Build the interval. 0.500 ± 2.262 × 0.1179 = 0.500 ± 0.267, giving 0.233 to 0.767.

Sanity check, and a warning. The interval must contain the estimate and exclude zero whenever T beats the critical value, and 0.233 to 0.767 does both. Now run the identical arithmetic on Anscombe's fourth set: you get the same t from a dataset whose entire slope rests on one point at x = 19. The test cannot see that. Only the plot can, which is lesson 0026's warning arriving where it does the most damage.

Your teacher. Never read a slope on its own. Ask for its standard error and for Sxx before you ask what it means: the first says whether the number is distinguishable from nothing, and the second says whether the study was designed well enough to find out. Then ask to see the residual plot, because it is the only one of the three that can tell you the model is the wrong shape.

Primary source to go deeper

The chi-square distribution of SSE over sigma-squared with n - 2 degrees of freedom is quoted here rather than proved. It, and both interval formulas, are derived in full in the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section 4.4 on linear least squares regression, which is free and stable.
