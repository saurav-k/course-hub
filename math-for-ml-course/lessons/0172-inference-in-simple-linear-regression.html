Module 09 · Estimation, testing, and inference · Lesson 0172

A fitted slope is one draw from a distribution you can name

working 11 min Fitting a line is arithmetic. Deciding whether the line means anything is inference, and it needs three more numbers.

The one-minute version

  • Least squares hands you a slope. Re-run the experiment and you get a different one, so the slope is a random variable.
  • Its distribution is exactly nameable: B is Normal with mean the true slope and variance sigma-squared over Sxx.
  • You do not know sigma, so you estimate it with SSE divided by n minus 2, and that swap is what turns a z into a t.
  • Sxx is a design lever. Spreading your x values wider buys precision on the slope for free: here 9.7 times, at the same sample size and the same noise.

1. The model, and what is random in it

Yi = α + βxi + ei,  with ei independent Normal(0, σ2) Read as: the response for row i equals a true intercept α, plus a true slope β times that row's predictor value xi, plus an error ei. The errors are independent of one another, centred on zero, and all share one spread σ. The xi are treated as fixed numbers you chose; the randomness is entirely in the errors, and therefore entirely in the responses.

That last sentence is the whole page in one line. Because the responses are random, anything computed from them is random too, and the fitted slope is computed from them. Write the estimates as A and B to keep them visually distinct from the truths α and β, which you never see.

2. The fit, worked small enough to check

Seven points, chosen so every intermediate number is one you can verify on paper.

  1. Write the pairs. x runs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and y is 3, 7, 8, 12, 14, 19, 20.
  2. Find the two means. x sums to 28, so x̄ = 28 ÷ 7 = 4. y sums to 83, so ȳ = 83 ÷ 7 = 11.8571.
  3. Centre and square the x values. The deviations are -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, and their squares sum to 9 + 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 + 9 = Sxx = 28.
  4. Centre both and cross-multiply. Summing (xi - x̄)(yi - ȳ) gives Sxy = 81.
  5. Divide for the slope. B = 81 ÷ 28 = 2.892857.
  6. Back out the intercept. A = 11.8571 - 2.892857 × 4 = 0.285714.
  7. Square the residuals. They come to -0.179, 0.929, -0.964, 0.143, -0.750, 1.357, -0.536, and their squares sum to SSE = 4.5357.
  8. Estimate the noise. 4.5357 ÷ (7 - 2) = 0.9071, so σ̂ = 0.9524.

Sanity check. The residuals must sum to zero and must also sum to zero after multiplying each by its own x, because those two conditions are the equations least squares solved. Both hold here to fifteen decimal places. What changes if you move the last point from 20 up to 26: Sxy rises to 99, the slope goes to 3.536, and the residual spread roughly doubles, so the fit moves and its precision falls at the same time.

3. The slope's distribution, with the proof

The theorem B is Normal with mean β and variance σ2 / Sxx. The estimator is unbiased, and its precision is set by how far apart you put your x values.

The shape of the argument. Write the slope as a weighted sum of the responses. Then its mean and variance follow from the mean and variance of a sum of independent normals, which Module 8 established, and normality comes free because a linear combination of independent normals is normal.

B = Σi wi Yi,  where wi = (xi - x̄) / Sxx Read as: the fitted slope is a fixed set of weights applied to the random responses. Each weight wi depends only on the x values, which are not random, so the weights are constants.

Two facts about those weights do all the work: they sum to zero, and multiplied by the x values they sum to one. The first is what lets the ȳ term drop out of the definition; the second is what makes the estimator land on β rather than on some multiple of it. With those in hand:

E[B] = Σi wi(α + βxi) = α·0 + β·1 = β Read as: take the expected value of each response, which is the line's own value there, and push it through the weights. The intercept term vanishes because the weights sum to zero, and the slope term survives intact because the weights times the x values sum to one.

The step that does the real work is the variance. Independence is what allows the variance of the sum to be the sum of the variances, with no covariance terms, and that is the only place the model's independence assumption is spent:

Var(B) = Σi wi2σ2 = σ2 Σi(xi - x̄)2 / Sxx2 = σ2 / Sxx Read as: squaring each weight and summing gives Sxx divided by Sxx squared, which collapses to one over Sxx. So the variance of the slope is the noise variance divided by the spread of the predictor.

The program for this page measures that prediction rather than trusting it: over 20,000 simulated experiments the observed spread of the slope is 0.068423 against a predicted 0.068876.

4. You do not know sigma, so you spend two degrees of freedom

Every formula above contains σ, which is not observable. Replace it with an estimate built from the residuals. The divisor is n - 2, not n, because two parameters were fitted to the same data before the residuals were taken.

T = (B - β) / √( (SSE/(n-2)) / Sxx )   follows a t distribution on n - 2 degrees of freedom Read as: the estimated slope minus the true slope, divided by the estimated standard error of the slope. Had you known σ this would be a standard normal; because you estimated it from the same data, the extra uncertainty widens the tails into a t distribution with n minus 2 degrees of freedom.

5. The test, on four thousand rows

The program regresses y on x01 in features.csv, where the true slope is 4.0 by construction.

  1. The fit. B = 4.058611 against a true slope of 4.0, and A = -0.043960 against a true intercept of 0.
  2. The spread of the predictor. Sxx = 4,047.21 across 4,000 rows.
  3. The noise estimate. SSE = 71,771.20, so σ̂2 = 71,771.20 ÷ 3,998 = 17.951777.
  4. The standard error. √(17.951777 ÷ 4,047.21) = 0.066600.
  5. The test statistic. 4.058611 ÷ 0.066600 = 60.94, against a critical value of 1.9606 at 3,998 degrees of freedom.
  6. The interval. 4.058611 ± 1.9606 × 0.066600 gives 3.928 to 4.189, which contains the true 4.0.
The estimated noise variance is 17.95, and the generator's own error term has variance 9.0. The gap is not a bug. This is a simple regression on one predictor, so everything the other four real predictors contribute lands in the residual: 2.52 + 1.52 + 1.02 + 0.62 = 9.86, and 9.0 + 9.86 = 18.86. In a simple regression σ2 means "everything the model does not see", and here we happen to know exactly what that is.

6. Where the interval is tight, and where it is not

The interval for the fitted line at a new point x0 carries a term in (x0 - x̄)2 / Sxx, so it is narrowest at the mean of your predictor and widens either side of it.

A second interval exists at every point and is much wider: the one for a single new observation rather than for the line. It carries an extra 1 under the root, which is the irreducible noise of the new draw, so unlike the band above it never shrinks towards zero however much data you gather. Lesson 0202 works that comparison end to end on a second dataset.

7. Sxx is yours to choose

Of the two quantities in Var(B) = σ2/Sxx, the noise is a fact about the world and the spread of x is a decision. Where you can choose the x values, that decision is worth more than a larger sample.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

The residual sum of squares is divided by n - 2 rather than by n. What does the 2 pay for?
An intercept and a slope were estimated from these rows before the residuals were computed, so the residuals are two constraints short of free and dividing by n would understate the noise, which the bar chart shows as 15.67 against a true 18.86. The tails of the t distribution are a consequence of estimating sigma, not the reason for the divisor. The interval's endpoints are downstream of the estimate entirely. And the two modelling assumptions are what license the distribution, not what the degrees of freedom count.
Two studies estimate the same slope with the same noise and the same number of rows. One reports a smaller standard error. Why?
The standard error is sigma over the square root of Sₓₓ, and with sigma and n held equal the only thing left to vary is the spread of x: the bar chart shows a 9.7-fold difference from that alone. The size of the fitted slope does not enter the formula at all. The confidence level scales the interval but not the standard error, which is computed before any level is chosen. And smaller residuals would mean smaller sigma, which the question fixed as equal.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Use the seven-point fit from section 2, where B = 2.892857, Sxx = 28 and SSE = 4.5357, with n = 7.

σ̂2 = SSE/(n-2),  se(B) = √(σ̂2/Sxx),  T = B/se(B) Read as: estimate the noise variance by dividing the residual sum of squares by n minus 2, take the standard error of the slope from it, then divide the slope by its own standard error.

Test H0: β = 0 against the critical value 2.5706 for 5 degrees of freedom, build the 95 per cent interval, and then say what would have to change about the seven x values, leaving the y values alone, to halve the standard error.

Hint

For the last part, do not look for more data. Look at which single quantity in the standard-error formula you are allowed to change by choosing where to measure, and remember it sits under a square root.

Show the worked solution
  1. Estimate the noise variance. 4.5357 ÷ (7 - 2) = 4.5357 ÷ 5 = 0.9071.
  2. Divide by the predictor spread. 0.9071 ÷ 28 = 0.032397.
  3. Take the square root. se(B) = √0.032397 = 0.179994.
  4. Form the statistic. T = 2.892857 ÷ 0.179994 = 16.07 on 5 degrees of freedom.
  5. Decide. 16.07 is far beyond 2.5706, so reject H0: this slope is not plausibly zero.
  6. Build the interval. 2.892857 ± 2.5706 × 0.179994 = 2.892857 ± 0.462687, giving 2.430 to 3.356.
  7. Halve the standard error. The error goes as one over √Sxx, so halving it needs four times the Sxx, which is 112. Doubling every deviation from the mean does it: x values of -6, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, 6 about the same centre, that is 1 through 7 stretched to span 2 to 14, give exactly 4 × 28 = 112.

Sanity check. The interval must contain the point estimate and exclude zero whenever T beats the critical value, and 2.430 to 3.356 does both. The width should be roughly 2 × 2.57 × 0.18, which is about 0.93, and 3.356 - 2.430 = 0.926. If your interval had straddled zero while T was 16, an arithmetic slip in steps two or three is the place to look.

Your teacher. The habit to take from this page is to never read a slope on its own. Whenever you meet a fitted coefficient, ask for its standard error and its Sxx before you ask what it means: the first tells you whether the number is distinguishable from nothing, and the second tells you whether the study was designed to find out.
