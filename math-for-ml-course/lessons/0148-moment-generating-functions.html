One transform that turns adding into multiplying

frontier 11 min Convolution is painful. Multiplication is not. That trade is the whole of this page.

The one-minute version

  • The moment generating function is M(t) = E[etX], one function of a dummy variable t that encodes the whole distribution.
  • For independent variables the MGF of a sum is the product of the MGFs, so convolution becomes multiplication.
  • Differentiating it at zero reads the moments off one at a time, which is where the name comes from.
  • It is the rung of the tail-bound ladder that makes Chernoff possible, and that is why an engineering course keeps it.

1. The mental model: weigh the distribution with an exponential

Pick a dial t. Multiply every outcome by etX and average. Turn the dial up and the large outcomes dominate, because the exponential grows so fast; turn it down and the small ones do. Sweeping t traces a curve that carries the whole distribution.

M(t) = E[etX] Read as: the moment generating function of X, evaluated at the dummy variable t. Take the random variable, exponentiate t times it, and average. t is not part of the experiment; it is a dial you choose, and the whole function is what carries the information.

Two facts fall out. At t = 0 the exponential is 1 for every outcome, so M(0) = 1 always, which is a free check. And turn the dial too far and the average can be infinite, so the MGF need not exist everywhere.

A lognormal has no moment generating function for any positive t at all: the density's tail does not decay fast enough for the average to be finite. Since latency, spend and file sizes are routinely lognormal, this is not an exotic corner case, and it is the reason the tail bound this page builds towards does not apply to every column you own.

2. Why it is called moment generating

Expand the exponential as a power series inside the expectation and the moments fall out as coefficients.

M(t) = E[1 + tX + t2X2/2! + …] = 1 + tE[X] + t2E[X2]/2! + … Read as: replace the exponential by its infinite series, then use linearity of expectation to average term by term. The exclamation marks are factorials. Each power of t now carries one moment: E[X], then E[X squared], and so on.

Differentiate k times and set t to zero, and every term but one vanishes.

M(k)(0) = E[Xk] Read as: the k-th derivative of the moment generating function, evaluated at zero, is the k-th moment of X. So one derivative gives the mean, two give the average of the square, and variance follows from the two together.

3. The property everything else rests on

Theorem If X and Y are independent, the moment generating function of their sum is the product of their moment generating functions: MX+Y(t) = MX(t) · MY(t).

Proof. Two lines, and the second is where independence enters.

MX+Y(t) = E[et(X+Y)] = E[etX · etY] Read as: apply the definition to the sum, then split the exponential of a sum into a product of exponentials, which is an ordinary rule of indices and involves no probability at all.

Now the step that does the work. X and Y independent makes etX and etY independent too, since a function of one is independent of a function of the other. And for independent variables the expectation of a product splits, which is the one identity from lesson 142 that does require independence.

= E[etX] · E[etY] = MX(t) · MY(t) Read as: the expectation splits across the product because the two factors are independent, and each half is the definition of one MGF.

Compare that with the previous page. Adding two variables meant a convolution; here it is one multiplication. That trade is why the Central Limit Theorem is usually proved this way: n identical independent pieces make M(t)n, and the limit of an n-th power is a problem calculus already knows.

4. The payoff: the third rung of the tail-bound ladder

The next two lessons build tail bounds from the mean and from the variance. This one goes further: apply Markov's inequality to etX, which is non-negative for every t, then choose the t that makes the bound tightest.

P(X ≥ a) ≤ M(t) ÷ eta   for every t > 0 Read as: the probability of exceeding a is at most the moment generating function divided by e to the ta. Because it holds for every positive t, you are free to pick the one that makes the right-hand side smallest, and that optimisation is what makes the bound sharp.

For a sum of independent pieces M(t) is a product, so the bound decays like a product of n factors: exponentially in n, rather than like the 1/k2 the variance alone buys. That gap is what learning theory is built on.

5. Worked example: the MGF of an exponential, and a sum of two

Service times are modelled as exponential with rate λ, meaning a mean of 1/λ.

  1. Write the definition. M(t) = E[etX] = ∫0∞ etx λe-λx dx.
  2. Collect the exponentials. The integrand becomes λe-(λ-t)x.
  3. Integrate. That is a standard exponential integral and gives λ ÷ (λ - t), provided λ - t is positive.
  4. Note the condition. If t reaches λ the integral diverges, so the MGF exists only for t < λ. This is the vertical line in the figure above.
  5. Check M(0). λ ÷ (λ - 0) = 1, as it must be for every distribution.
  6. Read off the mean. Differentiating gives λ ÷ (λ - t)2, and at t = 0 that is 1/λ, which is the mean the model started from.
  7. Add two independent ones. With λ = 2, the sum's MGF is the product: [2/(2-t)]2. That is the MGF of a Gamma with shape 2, so the sum is Gamma rather than exponential.
  8. Sanity check by simulation. Averaging etX over 400,000 draws at t = 1.5 gives 4.043 against the closed form 2/(2-1.5) = 4. What changes if you push t to 2 is that both the formula and the average blow up.
code/0148-moment-generating-functions.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260828)
sample = rng.exponential(1 / 2.0, 400_000)          # rate 2

def mgf(x, t):                                       # in logs, or it overflows
    scaled = np.outer(t, x)
    peak = scaled.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return np.exp(peak.ravel() + np.log(np.exp(scaled - peak).mean(axis=1)))

grid = np.array([-1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 1.5])
print("measured   ", mgf(sample, grid).round(5))
print("closed form", (2.0 / (2.0 - grid)).round(5))

The full program also differentiates the MGF numerically to recover the first two moments, and shows the sum-becomes-product identity holding for an independent pair and failing by 12 percent at t = 0.5 for a dependent one.

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does the MGF of a sum equal the product of the MGFs?
The exponential splitting is only algebra and holds always; what needs independence is taking the expectation of the resulting product apart into two expectations. The first option names the algebra step and stops one line short, which makes it the strongest distractor, and it will lead you to apply the identity to dependent variables where it fails.
Your latency column is lognormal. What does that mean for a Chernoff-style bound?
A lognormal's MGF is infinite for every positive t, so the bound has nothing to optimise over and simply does not exist. The second option is the seductive one: an inapplicable bound is not a loose bound, and treating it as one produces a guarantee that was never proved. Standardising shifts and scales but does not change the tail's decay rate.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

A single Bernoulli variable takes the value 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise.

Find its moment generating function, then the MGF of a sum of n independent copies, and say what distribution that identifies. Then recover the mean of the sum by differentiating.

Hint

With only two outcomes the expectation is a two-term sum, so no integral is needed. For the derivative, you do not have to expand the whole n-th power: the chain rule on a power is enough.

Show the worked solution
  1. Write the two-term expectation. M(t) = et·1·p + et·0·(1-p).
  2. Simplify. e0 is 1, so M(t) = 1 - p + pet.
  3. Check M(0). 1 - p + p = 1, as required.
  4. Multiply n copies. Independent, so the MGF of the sum is (1 - p + pet)n.
  5. Identify it. That is the moment generating function of a Binomial with parameters n and p, which is right: a sum of n independent Bernoulli trials counts successes.
  6. Differentiate. By the chain rule, the derivative is n(1 - p + pet)n-1 · pet.
  7. Evaluate at zero. The bracket becomes 1, e0 is 1, so the whole thing is np.
  8. What changes if the trials are dependent. The mean is still np, because linearity never needed independence. The MGF is no longer the n-th power, so the Binomial identification and every tail bound built on it are gone.

Sanity check. M(0) must be exactly 1 both for the single trial and for the n-th power, and the mean must come out as np, which you already know from linearity. If your derivative gives something depending on t after setting t to zero, the chain rule dropped a factor.

Your teacher. Before you next quote a concentration bound at someone, check whether the quantity you are bounding actually has a moment generating function. If it is a latency, a spend or a file size, the honest answer is usually no, and the bound you want is one that needs only a variance.

Primary source to go deeper

Rick Durrett, Probability: Theory and Examples (5th edition), section 3.3, which develops the closely related characteristic function and uses it to prove the Central Limit Theorem in section 3.4. The characteristic function replaces etX with eitX, which always exists, and that is exactly how the theory sidesteps the lognormal problem this page raises.
