A monotone change moves every number and no ordering

foundation 11 min The theorem on this page is why you are allowed to take the logarithm of a likelihood three lessons from now.

The one-minute version

  • About six shapes account for most of the curves in this field, and knowing where each is flat and where it is steep is most of reading a plot.
  • A strictly increasing transform preserves the location of every maximum. That is a theorem, and this page proves it.
  • The word strictly is the whole proof. A transform that merely never decreases can move an argmax, and clipping is exactly such a transform.
  • Metrics that read the ordering survive it. Metrics that compare against a fixed threshold do not, and that is not the transform's fault.

1. Six shapes carry most of the field

Two of them are worth a sentence now because later pages lean on their shape rather than their formula. The logarithm grows without limit but ever more slowly, which is what lets it tame a number that would otherwise be unusable. The sigmoid is nearly flat at both ends, which is why a signal arriving out there is small - the mechanism belongs to M05, but the shape is the reason.

2. Monotone, and the word that carries the theorem

A function is increasing if it never goes down as its input grows, and strictly increasing if it always goes up. The gap between those two is where all the trouble lives.

strictly increasing:   a < b  ⇒  g(a) < g(b) Read as: whenever a comes before b, the transformed a comes strictly before the transformed b. Merely "increasing" would allow g(a) and g(b) to be equal, and that single allowance is what breaks the theorem below.

3. The theorem, and its proof

Here is the result the rest of this module spends.

The monotone-transform theorem Let f map some set into the reals, and let g be strictly increasing on the values f takes. Then x maximises f exactly when it maximises g ∘ f. In symbols, argmax f = argmax (g ∘ f).

Proof, forwards. Suppose x* maximises f, so f(x) ≤ f(x*) for every x. A strictly increasing function is in particular non-decreasing, so applying g to both sides keeps the inequality: g(f(x)) ≤ g(f(x*)). So x* maximises g ∘ f too.

Proof, backwards. Suppose x* maximises g ∘ f but does not maximise f. Then some x' has f(x') > f(x*). Because g is strictly increasing, that strict inequality survives: g(f(x')) > g(f(x*)). But that contradicts x* being a maximiser of g ∘ f. So no such x' exists and x* maximises f.

The step that does the work Strictness is used exactly once, in the backwards direction, and the theorem is false without it. Take g constant: it never decreases, it turns every point into a maximiser of g ∘ f, and almost none of them maximise f. That is not a pathological example - clipping is the same failure in mild form, and section 5 shows it moving a real argmax.

What the theorem does not say. It preserves the location of the maximum and says nothing about its value, nothing about the gap to the runner-up, and nothing about any quantity measured against a fixed number. It keeps the ordering and discards the scale. That is exactly the trade lesson 07 wants, and exactly why the next section goes wrong.

4. What survives, and what does not

  1. Note that the ordering did not move. Reading down either column gives the same six items in the same sequence, so anything computed from ranks alone must be unchanged.
  2. Compute AUC before. Every urgent item outranks every normal one, so AUC is 1.000.
  3. Compute AUC after. Same ordering, so still 1.000.
  4. Compute accuracy at 0.5 before. Five of the six land on the right side of the line, so 0.833.
  5. Compute accuracy at 0.5 after. Only one item now exceeds 0.5, giving 0.667.
  6. Find the culprit. The threshold stayed at 0.5, but the score that now maps to 0.5 is √0.5 = 0.707. Every item between 0.5 and 0.707 crossed the line.
Sanity check. An AUC of 1.000 says every urgent item outranks every normal one, and reading down the score column confirms it. What changes if the cutoff travels with the transform, to 0.52 = 0.25: accuracy returns to 0.833 exactly. The defect was never the transform.

Which metrics survive is a property of what they read, and it is worth holding as a picture rather than a list.

5. The same claim on 721 real rows

The hand-worked six were chosen to be checkable. The program for this lesson runs the identical argument on the held-out split of the ticket data, where nothing was chosen at all.

code/0004-the-shape-of-a-function.py · runnable Python, numpy and pandas only · download · data: tickets.csv
transformed = scores ** 2
assert auc_by_ranks(labels, scores) == auc_by_ranks(labels, transformed)
assert not np.isclose(accuracy_at(labels, scores, 0.5),
                      accuracy_at(labels, transformed, 0.5))
assert np.isclose(accuracy_at(labels, transformed, 0.5 ** 2),
                  accuracy_at(labels, scores, 0.5))     # transport the cutoff

On those 721 rows the AUC is 0.933462 before and 0.933462 after - equal as exact floating-point numbers, not merely close. Accuracy at 0.5 moves from 0.851595 to 0.852982, and transporting the cutoff to 0.25 returns it to 0.851595 exactly.

Notice that here the accuracy went up, where in the six-item example it went down. That is worth pausing on: the theorem promises the ordering is safe and promises nothing about which way a thresholded metric will move. If you cannot predict the direction, you cannot argue that a rise means the transform helped.

The program also clips the scores at 0.6, which is non-decreasing but not strictly increasing. It creates 168 ties, and the argmax moves. That is the theorem's converse failing on real data, exactly where the proof said it would.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Every score in [0,1] is squared. Which metric is guaranteed unchanged?
Squaring is strictly increasing on that interval, so the ordering holds and any rank-based metric is safe. The first and fourth options both compare against a fixed number, and the score that now maps to 0.5 is 0.707, so items cross the line. The third is not scale-free at all: this page's own worked example moves log loss from 0.421 to 0.514.
Which transform can change which class an argmax picks?
Clipping is the only one that is not strictly increasing: everything above the cap arrives at the same value, and a tie is precisely where the theorem's backwards direction fails. The other three are all strictly increasing on the relevant domain and preserve the order exactly. The program for this lesson clips at 0.6, creates 322 ties, and watches the argmax move.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Take the same six items: urgent at 0.80, 0.60, 0.55 and normal at 0.52, 0.30, 0.10. Apply the logit g(s) = ln(s / (1 − s)), which is strictly increasing on the open interval from 0 to 1.

(a) What are the six transformed scores?
(b) Recompute AUC and accuracy at a fixed 0.5 cutoff, before and after.
(c) A downstream system takes the top 3 by score and pages someone. Does what it pages change?

Hint

For (c), do not recompute anything. Ask which of the two things the theorem protects - the values or the ordering - a "top 3" reads.

Show the worked solution
  1. Transform each score. ln(0.80/0.20) = 1.386, then 0.406, 0.201, 0.080, -0.847, -2.197.
  2. Check the ordering. Still descending, and still the three urgent items above the three normal ones. The logit is strictly increasing, so this was guaranteed before you computed anything.
  3. AUC before and after. Every urgent item outranks every normal one in both columns, so 1.000 and 1.000.
  4. Accuracy at 0.5 before. Four items exceed 0.5 and three of them are urgent, with 0.52 the one mistake: 0.833.
  5. Accuracy at 0.5 after. Only 1.386 exceeds 0.5, so two genuinely urgent items are now called normal: 0.667.
  6. Answer (c) without arithmetic. A top-3 reads the ordering and nothing else. The ordering did not move, so the same three items are paged.

Sanity check. If your AUC moved, you re-sorted rather than transformed - a strictly increasing map cannot reorder anything, so a changed AUC means an arithmetic slip rather than a discovery. And the transformed scores should straddle zero, because the logit sends 0.5 to 0 and these scores sit on both sides of a half.

Your teacher. The proof on this page is four sentences and one of them carries all the weight. If the backwards direction slid past, ask an agent to "take a non-decreasing but not strictly increasing g, and construct a concrete f where argmax f and argmax of g composed with f differ" - building the counterexample yourself is what makes the word strictly stop being decoration.

Primary source to go deeper

Fawcett's An introduction to ROC analysis is where the ranking claim comes from: section 7 states that the AUC "is equivalent to the probability that the classifier will rank a randomly chosen positive instance higher than a randomly chosen negative instance", and section 4 contrasts that with accuracy, which "imposes a threshold".
