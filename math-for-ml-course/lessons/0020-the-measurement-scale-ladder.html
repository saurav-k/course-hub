A column's scale decides which summaries mean anything

foundation 10 min The cheapest bug prevention in the module, and it takes two seconds per column.

The one-minute version

  • Every column sits on one of four measurement scales, and the scale is a fact about the column rather than about how it happens to be encoded.
  • Each scale is defined by the transformations that leave its meaning intact. That is the whole definition and everything else follows from it.
  • The test: apply one of those transformations and see whether your conclusion moves. If it moves, the statistic was never meaningful on that column.
  • A mean will compute on any numeric column. Computing is not the same as meaning, and nothing in the tooling will tell you the difference.

1. Two numbers that compute and mean nothing

Here are two summaries taken from a real table of 30,000 people. Both ran without complaint.

mean(region) = 2.0881      mean(satisfaction) = 3.5782 Read as: the arithmetic average of a column holding a region name encoded as a number, and the arithmetic average of a five-point satisfaction rating. region is a label, satisfaction is an ordered rating, and both were stored as integers, which is the only reason either one computed at all.

The first is meaningless and the second is a hidden assumption. Neither raised an error, neither is flagged by any dashboard, and both will happily go into a report. This page is about the two seconds of thought that catch them.

2. The test, stated once

A measurement scale is not a category you memorise. It is defined by the transformations you are allowed to apply without changing what the column means. Recoding regions from one numbering to another does not change which region anyone lives in. Converting a temperature from Celsius to Fahrenheit does not change how warm the room is.

The test Apply a transformation the scale allows, recompute your statistic, and see whether your conclusion moves. If a legitimate transformation changes the answer, the statistic was never a fact about the data. Everything below is this one test, run four times.

3. Nominal: labels, and nothing else

A nominal column carries names. Any one-to-one relabelling is allowed, because the labels were arbitrary to begin with.

Encode the region column one way and the mean comes out at 2.0881. Encode the same five regions in a different order, which is exactly as valid, and the mean is 1.9119. Same data, same question, two answers. The mode, meanwhile, is us-east under both encodings, with 9,375 people, because counting does not care what the labels are called.

So on a nominal column the mode and the frequency count survive, and the mean does not exist. Not "is discouraged" - does not exist.

4. Ordinal: an order, with unknown spacing

An ordinal column has a genuine order and no promise about the gaps. Any order-preserving transformation is allowed.

The satisfaction column is scored 1 to 5. Respace the top category as 10 instead of 5 - the order is untouched, so this is legitimate - and watch what happens.

5. Interval: differences carry, ratios do not

An interval scale has meaningful differences and a zero that is a convention rather than an absence. Celsius is the standard example: 0 C is the freezing point of water, not the absence of temperature. The allowed transformations are x → a·x + b with a > 0, which is exactly what a Celsius-to-Fahrenheit conversion is.

6. Ratio: a real zero, so ratios survive

A ratio scale has a zero that means none. The only transformation it allows is x → a·x, a change of unit, and that is why ratios come through untouched: the scale factor appears in the top and the bottom and cancels.

The spend column's p99 is 14.4574 times its median in pounds, and 14.4574 times its median in pence. Identical, not merely close. "The tail is fourteen and a half times the median" is a sentence that means something, and you can check that it does by converting the units and watching the number stay put.

7. The ladder

8. Where this bites in a model

A model handed an ordinal column as though it were on a ratio scale has been told a false fact: that the distance from rating 1 to rating 2 equals the distance from 4 to 5. It will use that fact. A linear model will fit a single coefficient to a spacing nobody measured, and a tree will split on thresholds that mean less than they appear to.

This is not an argument for a particular encoding - that is a modelling decision and it belongs elsewhere. It is an argument for knowing which decision you are making.

9. Worked, on one column per rung

Four columns of the same 30,000 rows, one from each rung, with the same test applied to each.

  1. Take the nominal column and relabel it. Two encodings of region, both valid. Mean: 2.0881 against 1.9119. Mode: us-east both times. The mean failed the test.
  2. Take the ordinal column and respace it. satisfaction's top category moved from 5 to 10, order preserved. Median 4.0 both times; mean 3.5782 then 4.6847. The median passed, the mean failed.
  3. Take the interval column and convert it. office_temp_c into Fahrenheit. Mean 20.997 C becomes 69.795 F, which is the correct conversion of that mean. The difference 8.330 C becomes 14.994 F, also correct. Both passed.
  4. Now take the ratio of the same interval column. 1.4941 in Celsius, 1.2405 in Fahrenheit. Failed, and this is the one that surprises people.
  5. Take the ratio column and change its unit. spend from pounds to pence, ratio 14.4574 and 14.4574. Passed, bit for bit.
Sanity check. A statistic that survives should survive exactly, not approximately - the spend ratio is identical to four decimals because the scale factor cancels algebraically, not because the arithmetic was lucky. What changes if you convert spend to euros at some rate instead: still identical, because that is the same kind of transformation.

10. The test, in code

code/0020-the-measurement-scale-ladder.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download · data: population.csv
# nominal: two encodings that are equally valid
first  = {name: i for i, name in enumerate(sorted(regions))}
second = {name: i for i, name in enumerate(sorted(regions, reverse=True))}
print(frame.region.map(first).mean(), frame.region.map(second).mean())

# interval: a legitimate conversion, and what it does to a ratio
fahrenheit = frame.office_temp_c * 9 / 5 + 32
print(c_p99 / c_p01, f_p99 / f_p01)      # 1.4941 against 1.2405

The program runs the test on all four columns and prints both conclusions, before and after. Printing both is the point: a statistic that was never meaningful looks exactly like one that was, right up until you transform and compare.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A satisfaction survey is scored 1 to 5. Which scale is that column on?
The ratings are ordered and the gaps are not guaranteed equal, which is the definition of ordinal. The first option is true of the encoding and false of the scale: the integers are evenly spaced on the page, and nothing establishes that the feeling behind them is. The second is false, there is no 0 rating and no absence of satisfaction. The fourth throws away the order, which is real information the column does carry.
Office temperature in Celsius is converted to Fahrenheit. Which summary fails to survive?
Only the ratio changes, from 1.4941 to 1.2405, because Celsius has no true zero for the scale factor to cancel against. The mean converts correctly and so does a difference, which is exactly what "interval" means. The median only reads rank, and a positive linear map preserves rank.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

One room is 10 C and another is 20 C.

(a) Is the second twice as warm? Convert both to Fahrenheit and answer again.
(b) Do the same for two spends of 10 and 20 pounds, converted to pence.
(c) Say in one sentence what the difference between (a) and (b) is.

Hint

Do not argue about it. Convert, recompute the ratio, and compare the two answers you get.

Show the worked solution
  1. Take the ratio in Celsius. 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0. So far it looks like "twice as warm".
  2. Convert both. 10 × 9/5 + 32 = 50 and 20 × 9/5 + 32 = 68.
  3. Take the ratio again. 68 ÷ 50 = 1.36. The conversion was legitimate, so a claim that changed under it was never about the temperature.
  4. Now the spend. 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0; in pence, 2000 ÷ 1000 = 2.0. Unchanged.
  5. Name the difference. Converting pounds to pence multiplies by 100 and adds nothing. Converting Celsius to Fahrenheit multiplies and adds 32, and it is the added constant that does not cancel in a ratio.
  6. State it as the rung. Spend has a true zero, so it is on a ratio scale and ratios mean something. Celsius has a conventional zero, so it is interval, and ratios do not.

Sanity check. If your two ratios agree, the column has a true zero. If they disagree, it does not, and no amount of care in the arithmetic will fix that - the fix is to report a difference instead.

Your teacher. This page names no theorem; a measurement scale is a definition and the ladder is a consequence of it, so there is nothing here to prove. The habit is what matters. Point an agent at any table you own and ask it to "name the measurement scale of every column and flag any summary in my code that the scale does not support" - the first run usually finds something.

Primary source to go deeper

Stevens, S. S., "On the Theory of Scales of Measurement", Science 103 (1946), 677-680. Four pages, and it is the source of the framing this page uses: a scale is defined by the transformations that leave it invariant, and a statistic is meaningful on that scale only if it survives them.
