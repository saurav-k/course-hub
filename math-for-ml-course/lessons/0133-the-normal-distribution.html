The default distribution, and the tail where it stops being true

working 13 min Two parameters, one tabulated curve, and two theorems that earn it the default. · Hajek, ECE 313, section 3.6.2

The one-minute version

  • Two parameters: μ slides the curve and σ stretches it. Neither does anything else.
  • The Z-transform, (X − μ) ÷ σ, turns any normal into the one tabulated normal.
  • 68.27, 95.45, 99.73 percent inside one, two and three standard deviations - and 0.27 percent outside three.
  • It is the default for two theorems, not for an empirical reason: the central limit theorem and maximum entropy.
  • On this module's log there are 227 rows beyond three sigma where the normal predicts 34. The default lies about tails.

1. The density, and what its two parameters do

f(u) = 1 ÷ √(2πσ2) × exp( −(u − μ)2 ÷ (2σ2) ) u: a value the variable might take, anywhere on the real line. μ (mu): the centre, where the peak sits. σ (sigma): the scale, which sets the width. σ2: the variance. exp(−something squared): falls away fast and symmetrically on both sides, which is what makes the shape a bell. The leading fraction is exactly what is needed to make the total area 1, and section 2 proves it.

Replace μ by μ + 3 and the whole curve slides three units right, unchanged in shape. Double σ and it stretches sideways and flattens. The peak height is 1 ÷ √(2πσ2), so at σ = 0.5, 1 and 2 the peaks are 0.7979, 0.3989 and 0.1995 - and the area under all three is exactly 1.

2. Why the density integrates to 1

What is assumed: only the definition and the tools of Module 05. It is enough to do the standard case μ = 0, σ = 1; the general case follows from the Z-transform in section 3.

The shape: the one-dimensional integral has no elementary antiderivative, so you cannot attack it head on. Square it instead, read the square as an integral over the plane, and switch to polar coordinates where it becomes elementary.

  1. Name the integral. Let I be the integral of e−u2/2 over the whole real line. We want to show I = √(2π).
  2. Square it. I2 is a product of two identical integrals in different variables, which is a double integral of e−(x2 + y2)/2 over the entire plane.
  3. Change to polar coordinates. Here x2 + y2 is just r2, and the area element becomes r dr dθ.
  4. Do the inner integral. It is e−r2/2 times r, and that extra factor of r is exactly the derivative needed: substituting w = r2/2 turns it into the integral of e−w, which is 1.
  5. Do the outer integral. θ runs from 0 to 2π, and there is nothing left depending on it, so the answer is 2π.
  6. Take the square root. I2 = 2π, so I = √(2π), and dividing by it is exactly the constant in the density.
The step that does the real work is step 2, squaring the integral, and it is famously unmotivated until you see where it lands. The reason it works is step 4: the polar area element hands you a spare factor of r, and without that factor the inner integral is exactly as hard as the one you started with. The whole trick is that the two-dimensional problem is easier than the one-dimensional one.

3. The Z-transform, and why one table serves every normal

Z = (X − μ) ÷ σ X: a normal variable with mean μ and standard deviation σ. Z: the same quantity re-expressed as how many standard deviations from the mean. It has no units: subtracting the mean removes the offset and dividing by σ removes the scale.

Claim. If X is normal with mean μ and variance σ2, then Z is normal with mean 0 and variance 1.

  1. Work with the CDF, where transforms are easy. P(Z ≤ z) = P((X − μ)/σ ≤ z) = P(X ≤ μ + σz).
  2. Write that as an integral of X's density, up to the limit μ + σz.
  3. Substitute v = (u − μ)/σ. Then du = σ dv, and the upper limit becomes simply z.
  4. Watch the σ cancel. The σ arriving from du cancels the σ in the density's leading constant, and the exponent collapses to −v2/2.
  5. Read the result. What is left is the standard normal density integrated up to z.

The step that does the real work is the substitution in step 3, and it works because the normal family is closed under shifting and scaling. Not every family is: shifting a lognormal does not give a lognormal. So one tabulated curve, written Φ, answers every question about every normal - which is why Φ is tabulated and nothing else is.

4. Why it is the default, and what that does not mean

The reframing The normal is the default for two theorems, not because anyone measured the world and found it bell-shaped. Both are arguments from ignorance, correctly deployed.

The central limit theorem. A sum of many independent contributions is approximately normal, whatever the individual contributions look like. So a quantity made of many small additive effects tends to this shape by construction. Module 08 owns the theorem and proves the case this course needs. This page states it, uses it as motivation and does not prove it, because a reader who thinks they have seen a proof here has been misled.

Maximum entropy. Among all distributions on the real line with a given variance, the normal is the one carrying the most uncertainty. So if the variance is genuinely all you know, the normal assumes the least beyond it. Module 10 owns entropy and makes that precise.

5. Worked: standardising a real latency

Take the latency_ms column, restricted to the 24,788 rows under 300 ms, where the normal genuinely fits. That bulk has mean 181.38 ms and standard deviation 29.32 ms.

  1. Standardise a 240 ms request. z = (240 − 181.38) ÷ 29.32 = 1.9993, so it is two standard deviations slow.
  2. Read the tail. Two sigma leaves 2.28 percent above, so about one request in 44 is at least this slow.
  3. Check the bands against the data. Within one sigma the bulk gives 69.49 percent against 68.27 predicted; within two, 95.45 against 95.45; within three, 99.44 against 99.73.
  4. Standardise a fast one. z = (150 − 181.38) ÷ 29.32 = −1.0700, and 14.23 percent of requests are faster than that.
  5. Notice what standardising bought. Two questions in different units, both answered from one table, because both became a number of standard deviations first.

Sanity check. Standardising the mean itself must give exactly z = 0, and a z of 2.00 must correspond to a tail near 2.3 percent. Both hold.

What changes if you put the 212 slowest rows back in. The mean rises to 183.22 and the standard deviation to 37.18, so the same 240 ms request now standardises to z = 1.53 and looks unremarkable. Two hundred rows in twenty-five thousand moved the yardstick by half a standard deviation, which is what a heavy tail does to a fitted normal: it inflates σ and then everything looks normal-ish by comparison.

6. The warning this page has earned

Fit a normal to the whole latency column and the three bands come out at 79.58, 97.73 and 99.09 percent, against 68.27, 95.45 and 99.73. Beyond three standard deviations there are 227 rows where the normal predicts 34, which is 6.7 times too many. Restrict to the bulk and the fit is nearly exact - but even there, 109 rows sit beyond three sigma against 34 predicted. Real serving latency is not normal, and neither are loss values, activation magnitudes or token frequencies. Three-sigma alerting on a heavy-tailed metric fires constantly, and the reason is arithmetic rather than bad luck.

7. Where this bites

Weight initialisation. He et al. propose a zero-mean Gaussian with standard deviation √(2/n), where n is the number of incoming connections. For a 3×3 convolution with 256 input channels, n = 2,304 and the standard deviation is 0.02946. Such a layer with 256 output channels holds 589,824 weights, so about 1,592 of them start beyond three sigma. Contrast Lesson 130's Glorot initialiser, which is uniform and has a hard maximum where this one has a tail.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two normals share a mean; one has σ = 0.5 and the other σ = 2. Which carries more total probability?
Every density integrates to exactly 1, so neither carries more. The peaks are 0.7979 and 0.1995, a ratio of four, and that is height rather than probability. The second and third options each grab one visible feature, height or width, and mistake it for the area - which is the density misconception from Lesson 128 in its most natural disguise. The fourth is irrelevant: sliding a curve changes nothing about its area.
Which of these is not one of the two reasons the normal is a sensible default?
The third is the misconception: it is an empirical claim, and this page's own latency column refutes it with 227 rows against a predicted 34. The first two are exactly the two stated reasons, the central limit theorem and maximum entropy. The fourth is true and useful and is not one of the two reasons - it is what makes the normal convenient once chosen, rather than what justifies choosing it.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Using the bulk figures μ = 181.38 ms and σ = 29.32 ms:

Find the z of a 150 ms request, the share of requests the normal says are faster than that, and the latency the normal puts at the 99th percentile. Then say which of the three is least trustworthy and why.

Hint

Φ(−1.07) is about 0.1423, and the 99th percentile of a standard normal sits at z = 2.326.

Show the worked solution
  1. Standardise. z = (150 − 181.38) ÷ 29.32 = −1.0700.
  2. Read the lower tail. Φ(−1.0700) = 0.1423, so about 14.2 percent of requests are faster.
  3. Go the other way for the percentile. The 99th percentile is μ + 2.326σ = 181.38 + 2.326 × 29.32 = 249.6 ms.
  4. Judge the three. The third is the least trustworthy, and by a long way.
  5. Say why. It is a statement about the tail, and the bulk fit excluded the tail by construction. The file's real 99th percentile is 283.93 ms, so the normal is short by 34.4 ms - about 14 percent low on exactly the number an SLO would be written against.

Sanity check. A negative z must give a share below one half, and the 99th percentile must sit above the mean by roughly two and a third standard deviations. If your percentile came out below the mean, the sign of z was dropped somewhere. The wider lesson is in step 5: the first two answers are interpolation and the third is extrapolation, and a fitted distribution is always weakest where you have least data.

Your teacher. Build one habit from this page: before you use a normal for a tail question, check the tail. Count how many points lie beyond two and three sigma and compare against 4.55 percent and 0.27 percent. It takes two lines and it is the difference between a model and a hope. If the counts come in high, you have heavy tails, and the honest move is to report a measured quantile rather than a fitted one.
