One joint, two ways to read it, and only one of them is prediction

working 12 min A classifier does not output a number. It outputs a conditional distribution.

The one-minute version

  • Marginalising sums a variable away and gives you back a smaller joint, or a single distribution.
  • Conditioning takes one slice of the joint and divides it by its own total, and the division is what makes the slice a distribution again.
  • Every conditional distribution sums to one. That is a free correctness check and it catches most arithmetic slips.
  • Conditioning is not intervening. Narrowing the population you look at is a different act from changing the mechanism that made the data.

1. The mental model: sum it away, or slice it and rescale

Start from the joint table of lesson 01: customer tier down the side, latency band across the top, nine cells adding to one. There are exactly two useful questions to ask it, and they are different operations.

The first is marginalising. Sum a whole row and the latency variable disappears, leaving the probability of that tier alone. The name comes from the arithmetic: people wrote those row totals in the margin of the page.

The second is conditioning. Take that same row, but instead of adding it up, divide every cell in it by the row's own total. Now the row describes the latency of enterprise requests only, and it adds to one because you made it add to one.

2. The mechanism, and the one division that does the work

Marginalising is the sum rule. In the discrete case you add, and in the continuous case you integrate, and it is the same act either way.

pX(x) = ∑y p(x, y)     fX(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy Read as: to get the distribution of X on its own, run over every value y that the other variable could have taken and add up the joint probability of each pair. The subscript X marks that only one variable survives. The integral is the same instruction for a continuous variable, where "every value" is a continuum rather than a list.

Conditioning is a division, and the thing you divide by is the marginal you just computed.

p(y | x) = p(x, y) ÷ pX(x) Read as: the probability of y given x is the joint probability of the pair divided by the probability of x on its own. The vertical bar is read "given". The denominator is the size of the slice you restricted yourself to, and dividing by it is what turns a piece of a distribution back into a whole one.

Two consequences fall straight out. First, it is undefined when the denominator is zero, and that is not a technicality: you cannot ask what usually happens on a day that never occurs. Second, rearranging gives the chain rule, which is how every generative model factorises a joint it could not otherwise write down.

p(x, y) = pX(x) × p(y | x) Read as: the joint probability of a pair is the probability of the first thing times the probability of the second given the first. Nothing was assumed to get here. It is the conditioning definition with the denominator moved across.

3. Marginalising is easy on a table and hard in a model

On a nine-cell table, summing a variable away is arithmetic. In a model with a latent variable it is the integral that stops everything.

p(x) = ∫ p(x, z) dz Read as: the probability of the data x is the joint probability of the data together with a hidden variable z, integrated over every value z could take. z is the thing you never observe: a topic, a cluster label, a latent code.

That integral runs over every possible value of a high-dimensional hidden variable, and for anything but a toy model it has no closed form and no feasible numerical approximation. A variational autoencoder exists because of this one line. It cannot compute the integral, so it optimises a bound on it instead.

Worth carrying Marginalising is trivial when you already hold the joint and intractable when you are trying to learn it. Almost every hard problem in probabilistic machine learning is a marginalisation somebody could not do.

4. Conditioning is not intervening

This is the misconception that costs money, because it turns a correct calculation into a wrong decision.

Suppose sixty percent of the users who churn filed a support ticket in the week before. Conditioning on the ticket genuinely raises the estimated probability of churn, and a churn model that uses the feature will be more accurate for it. What does not follow is that suppressing tickets would reduce churn.

The textbook statement of this is worth memorising in someone else's words: the conditional probability that a person is from Germany given that they speak German is high, but teaching a randomly chosen person German does not change where they were born. Every time you read a conditional as a lever, check whether you have any evidence for the arrow's direction. Usually you do not, and the right response is an experiment.

5. Worked example: conditioning on tier, on 40,000 requests

  1. Take the enterprise row of the joint. Its three cells are 0.0518, 0.0264 and 0.0016.
  2. Marginalise to get the denominator. 0.0518 + 0.0264 + 0.0016 = 0.0799, so about 8 percent of traffic is enterprise.
  3. Divide the first cell. 0.0518 ÷ 0.0799 = 0.6489, so 65 percent of enterprise requests are fast.
  4. Divide the rest. 0.0264 ÷ 0.0799 = 0.3304 and 0.0016 ÷ 0.0799 = 0.0207.
  5. Run the free check. 0.6489 + 0.3304 + 0.0207 = 1.0000. If it had not, a cell or the denominator would be wrong.
  6. Compare against the unconditional answer. Overall, 23.22 percent of requests are slow.
  7. Read the gap. Given enterprise, only 2.07 percent are slow. Given free tier, 29.69 percent are. That is a factor of 14.4.
  8. Say what it means. Knowing one categorical fact moved the answer by more than an order of magnitude. That is what makes a column a feature rather than a column.
code/0141-conditional-distributions.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import pandas as pd

frame = pd.read_csv("../datasets/inference_runs.csv")
frame["band"] = pd.cut(frame["latency_ms"], [0, 120, 260, float("inf")],
                       labels=["fast", "normal", "slow"], right=False)

counts = pd.crosstab(frame["tier"], frame["band"])
joint = counts / counts.to_numpy().sum()

# Conditioning: divide each row by its own total.
conditional = joint.div(joint.sum(axis=1), axis=0)
print(conditional.round(4))
print(conditional.sum(axis=1))          # every row must be 1.0

# The same table by filtering instead of dividing, which is what
# the division above is actually doing.
subset = frame[frame["tier"] == "enterprise"]
print(subset["band"].value_counts(normalize=True))

The full program also reverses the conditional with Bayes and checks it against direct counting, and does the continuous case by narrow binning. That last section finds that the free tier keeps 4.33 percent of its latency mass beyond 600 ms against enterprise's 0.00 percent, which is the heavy tail measured rather than described.

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You take one row of a joint table and add it up. What have you produced?
Summing a row collapses the column variable away and leaves one marginal probability. The first option needs the division step, which has not happened, and that confusion is exactly what this page exists to fix. The fourth is wrong because one number is not a distribution, and this row's cells did not sum to one before you added them.
Sixty percent of churned users filed a support ticket first. What does that license?
The data supports conditioning, which is updating a belief once you see the ticket. The second and third options both read the conditional as a lever: the third is the stronger distractor because it states the intervention outright, and it is the reasoning that leads teams to hide the symptom. The fourth contradicts the number given.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A fraud model scores 100,000 transactions. Of these, 500 are genuinely fraudulent. The model flags 90 percent of the fraudulent ones and also flags 2 percent of the legitimate ones. A transaction has just been flagged. What is the probability it is actually fraudulent, and what does that imply for the review queue?

Hint

You are given P(flag | fraud) and want P(fraud | flag), so this is a reversal. Build the four-cell joint table in counts first, then condition on the flagged column by dividing it by its own total. Do not skip to a formula.

Show the worked solution
  1. Split the population. 500 fraudulent and 99,500 legitimate.
  2. Flagged fraudulent. 0.90 × 500 = 450.
  3. Flagged legitimate. 0.02 × 99,500 = 1,990.
  4. Total flagged. 450 + 1,990 = 2,440. This is the denominator, and it is the step people skip.
  5. Condition on the flag. 450 ÷ 2,440 = 0.1844.
  6. Read it. Fewer than one flagged transaction in five is genuinely fraudulent, even though the model catches 90 percent of fraud.
  7. Say why. The 2 percent false-positive rate applies to a group 199 times larger, so it contributes 1,990 flags against fraud's 450. The base rate, not the model, dominates.
  8. The consequence. A reviewer working this queue sees four false alarms for every real case. Raising the threshold trades recall for that ratio, and there is no setting that escapes the base rate.

Sanity check. P(fraud | flag) must be larger than the base rate of 500/100,000 = 0.005, because a flag is evidence, and smaller than P(flag | fraud) = 0.90, because most flags come from the larger group. Here 0.005 < 0.1844 < 0.90. If your answer falls outside that bracket, you have divided by the wrong total.

Your teacher. Take one conditional probability your team quotes in a dashboard and write down, in one sentence each, the observation it licenses and the intervention it does not. If the two sentences sound the same to you, that dashboard is being read as a lever and somebody will eventually act on it.
