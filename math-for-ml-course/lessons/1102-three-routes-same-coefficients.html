Module 11 · Capstone: regression, end to end · Lesson 1102

Two modules compute the same two numbers by completely different means

frontier 11 min Part 2 of 3. Module 3 owns the normal equations, Module 6 owns descent. This page runs both and shows why they cannot disagree.

The one-minute version

  • Module 3 solves the fit by inverting a 2 by 2 matrix. Module 6 solves it by taking 60 small steps downhill.
  • They return the same slope to five significant figures, and the leftover gap of 9 × 10-8 is travel descent had not finished, not disagreement.
  • That is not luck. Both are looking for the point where the gradient is zero, and the least-squares surface has exactly one.
  • Standardising the column first was not cosmetic: on raw seconds the largest safe step is 1.08 × 10-5, and the step that works standardised blows the fit up in four iterations.

1. The model, stated not derived

The model is the one Module 9 sets up and Module 3 solves. It is written here only so the symbols on this page have names.

spendi = β0 + β1 × secondsi + ei Read as: for session number i, the spend equals an intercept β0, which is the spend predicted for a session of zero length, plus a slope β1, which is the extra spend bought by one more second, multiplied by that session's length in seconds, plus an error ei, which is everything about this particular session the two numbers do not explain.

We never see β0 or β1. We see 20,000 rows and compute estimates, which this page writes as b0 and b1. What makes an estimate the right one is a choice, and the choice both routes make is least squares: pick the pair that makes the total squared miss as small as possible.

SS(b0, b1) = Σi ( yi - b0 - b1 xi )2 Read as: the sum, over every one of the n rows, of the squared vertical distance between the observed spend yi and the spend the line predicts at that session length xi. Squaring makes every miss positive, so misses above and below the line cannot cancel.

2. Route A: solve it directly

Module 3 turns that sum into two equations in two unknowns and solves them. Here it is on eight rows small enough to check by hand, taken from the middle of the dataset.

  1. Write the eight pairs. Session lengths 28, 46, 68, 85, 109, 204, 247, 332 seconds, against spends 57, 26, 33, 24, 98, 30, 66, 60.
  2. Find the two means. The lengths sum to 1,119, so x̄ = 1119 ÷ 8 = 139.9. The spends sum to 394, so ȳ = 394 ÷ 8 = 49.250.
  3. Centre both columns and multiply. Summing (xi - x̄)(yi - ȳ) across the eight rows gives Sxy = 4,989.2.
  4. Centre the lengths and square. Summing (xi - x̄)2 gives Sxx = 82,958.9.
  5. Divide for the slope. b1 = 4,989.2 ÷ 82,958.9 = 0.060141 rupees per second.
  6. Back out the intercept. b0 = 49.250 - 0.060141 × 139.9 = 40.8377.

Run the same two lines over all 20,000 rows and the slope is 0.060111 and the intercept 30.179946. The eight-row slope and the twenty-thousand-row slope agree to four significant figures, which looks like a triumph and is not one. Part 3 is about why.

Module 3's picture of why this is the answer is the one worth carrying: the fitted values are the projection of the spend column onto the plane spanned by the two predictor columns, so the residual has to be perpendicular to both. On the full dataset that is checkable arithmetic rather than a claim.

3. Route B: walk downhill instead

Module 6 never forms the matrix. It starts both estimates at zero and repeatedly steps against the gradient. On the standardised column, with a step size of 0.1, the slope estimate climbs and settles.

Undo the standardising and the descent estimate is a slope of 0.0601105 and an intercept of 30.179961, against the closed form's 0.0601106 and 30.179946. The slopes differ by 9 × 10-8, which is the distance descent still had left to travel at iteration 60, not a disagreement about the answer. Run it to 80 iterations and the two agree to every digit printed here.

4. Why the two routes cannot disagree

This is the one argument the capstone makes for itself rather than borrowing. It is short.

Why they meet Descent stops where the gradient is zero. The normal equations are the statement that the gradient is zero. So the two methods are not solving related problems, they are solving the identical equation by different means.

The argument, in four steps. Differentiate the squared-error sum with respect to each estimate. For the intercept, the derivative of SS is -2 Σ(yi - b0 - b1xi), and for the slope it is -2 Σ xi(yi - b0 - b1xi). Setting both to zero and dividing by -2 gives exactly the two normal equations Module 3 derives, which is step one. Step two is that descent's update stops changing the estimate precisely when the gradient it subtracts is the zero vector, so any point descent settles on satisfies those same two equations. Step three is the one doing the real work: SS is a quadratic in the two estimates whose second-derivative matrix is 2XTX, and that matrix is positive definite whenever the session-length column is not constant, so the surface is strictly convex and has exactly one stationary point. Step four is the conclusion: one stationary point, both methods land on a stationary point, so both land on the same one.

Step three is where the boundary of this course sits. Module 6 states the convexity result and shows the picture; a full proof that a positive definite Hessian implies a unique global minimum belongs to a convex optimisation course, and this page is not the place to smuggle one in. What matters here is knowing which step the weight is resting on.

5. Why standardising mattered

Module 4 introduced standardising as a scaling convenience. On this dataset it is the difference between converging and exploding, and the reason is a number you can compute.

κ = λmax / λmin, and the largest safe step is 2 / λmax Read as: the condition number κ is the largest eigenvalue of the loss surface's second-derivative matrix divided by the smallest. The step size must stay under 2 divided by the largest eigenvalue, or each step overshoots by more than it corrects and the estimate grows without bound.

On raw seconds, one column is 20,000 ones and the other holds values in the hundreds, so the two eigenvalues are 1.36 and 185,070. The condition number is 1.357 × 105 and the largest safe step is 1.081 × 10-5. Standardised, both eigenvalues are exactly 2, the condition number is exactly 1.0000, and any step below 1.0 is safe.

Run descent on the raw columns at the step size that worked standardised, 0.1, and the mean squared error goes 4.3 × 1011, then 1.5 × 1020, then 5.0 × 1028, and reaches 5.9 × 1045 by the fifth iteration. Nothing warns you. There is no error, no exception and no clue in the output beyond numbers that stopped meaning anything, which is why a diverging fit is usually diagnosed as a bug in the data rather than a step size that is 10,000 times too large.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Descent returned 0.0601105 and the closed form 0.0601106, differing at the seventh decimal. Why?
Both hunt the same zero-gradient point on a strictly convex surface, so the gap is remaining travel and shrinks with more iterations. Calling descent an approximation to a target it cannot reach gets the relationship backwards: on a convex quadratic it converges to the exact minimiser. The inversion is not the culprit either, since a well-conditioned 2 by 2 solve is accurate far beyond eight decimals. And there is only one objective here; both routes minimise the same sum.
The raw loss surface has eigenvalues 1.36 and 185,070. What does that predict?
The safe-step bound is 2 divided by the largest eigenvalue, which is 1.08 × 10-5 here, and past it every step overshoots by more than it corrects. It does not converge to a different answer: it does not converge at all. The closed form is untouched, because it solves the equation rather than walking the surface, and a condition number of 105 is nowhere near the precision limit of a 2 by 2 solve. And more iterations make divergence worse, not better, which is the trap: the loop looks like it is working.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

Descent on the standardised column starts both estimates at zero. The gradient with respect to the standardised slope, at estimate w, works out to 2(w - 0.487) because the standardised columns are orthonormal in the relevant sense.

wk+1 = wk - η × 2(wk - 0.487) Read as: the next estimate wk+1 equals the current estimate wk minus the step size η times the gradient at that point. The 0.487 is the standardised slope the closed form already found.

Compute the first two iterations by hand with η = 0.1, then say what value of η would land exactly on the answer in a single step, and explain what η = 1.1 would do.

Hint

Rewrite the update in terms of the distance still to travel, dk = wk - 0.487. One line of algebra turns the update into dk+1 = (1 - 2η) dk, and every part of the question is then about the single factor (1 - 2η).

Show the worked solution
  1. First step. w0 = 0, so the gradient is 2(0 - 0.487) = -0.974. Then w1 = 0 - 0.1 × (-0.974) = 0.0974.
  2. Second step. The gradient at 0.0974 is 2(0.0974 - 0.487) = -0.7792. Then w2 = 0.0974 + 0.07792 = 0.17532.
  3. Check against the run. The program for this page prints 0.097400 and 0.175320 for the same two iterations, so the hand arithmetic matches it.
  4. Rewrite as remaining distance. Subtract 0.487 from both sides of the update: dk+1 = dk - 2ηdk = (1 - 2η) dk.
  5. Land in one step. The distance goes to zero in one move when 1 - 2η = 0, so η = 0.5. That is the step size at which descent stops being iterative at all on this surface.
  6. The oversized step. At η = 1.1 the factor is 1 - 2.2 = -1.2. Each step flips to the far side of the answer and lands 20 per cent further away, so the estimate oscillates outward and diverges.

Sanity check. Every step at η = 0.1 must move toward 0.487 and must not pass it, because the factor 1 - 2η = 0.8 is between 0 and 1. Both computed values, 0.0974 and 0.17532, are larger than the one before and smaller than 0.487, so both pass. If a step had overshot, the step size would have to exceed 0.5.

Your teacher. If the divergence result surprised you, reproduce it rather than believing it. Take the program for this page, delete the two standardising lines, leave everything else alone, and watch the loss. Seeing a fit explode because of a step size, once, is worth more than reading the eigenvalue bound five times.
