Module 09 · Estimation, testing and inference · Lesson 167

The null is the hypothesis you can compute under

working 15 min Lesson 168 covers how the result gets misread. This page covers how it is produced.

The one-minute version

  • H0 pins the distribution down and H1 does not, which is the whole reason the null gets to be the null.
  • Five steps: state the pair, fix alpha before looking, compute a statistic, compare, decide.
  • The critical-value rule and the p-value rule are the same rule written two ways.
  • You never accept H0. You fail to reject it, which is a statement about your evidence, not about the world.
  • Lowering alpha raises the miss rate at fixed n. Only more data buys both.

1. Why the null, and not the interesting hypothesis

You would rather test "the new button is better". You cannot. "Better" is a family of hypotheses, one for every size of improvement, and a family has no single sampling distribution to compute with. "The two rates are equal" is one hypothesis, it fixes the distribution completely, and that is the entire justification for putting it in the null's chair. Nothing more principled than computability is going on.

2. The test, and the theorem under it

Theorem The pooled two-proportion test. Under H0: pA = pB = p, with independent samples of sizes nA and nB, the statistic below is approximately standard normal for large samples.
Z = (p̂B - p̂A) / √( p̂pool(1 - p̂pool)(1/nA + 1/nB) ) Read as: the numerator is the difference actually observed between the two rates. The denominator is its standard error computed as if the null were true, which is why the two samples are pooled into a single p̂pool = (kA + kB)/(nA + nB) rather than estimated separately.

What is assumed: the two samples are independent, and within each one the observations are independent with a constant success probability. The shape of the argument is one line of variance algebra followed by the central limit theorem.

Step 1. Under the null both arms share the rate p, and the two sample proportions are independent, so their variances add:

Var(p̂A - p̂B) = p(1-p)/nA + p(1-p)/nB = p(1-p)(1/nA + 1/nB) Read as: the variance of a difference of independent quantities is the sum of their variances, and because the null says both arms share one p, the same p(1-p) factors out of both terms.

Step 2. Each proportion is asymptotically normal by the central limit theorem, so their difference is too, and dividing by its standard deviation gives a standard normal.

The step that does the real work is pooling. Estimating the standard error from the combined sample rather than from each arm separately is not a convenience: it is what makes the denominator a quantity computed under H0. A statistic whose denominator used the separate rates would not have the null distribution the whole test is compared against.

3. Worked, on numbers from the sibling course

The hub's Statistical Foundations course works an A/B example to two confidence intervals, observes that they overlap, and says the sharper tool is a two-proportion test. That course's own roadmap runs through probability rather than inference, so the test is not on it. Here it is, on the same numbers, so the reader can carry them across.

  1. The setting. Grey button 300 conversions in 1,000, gold button 340 in 1,000. So p̂A = 0.30 and p̂B = 0.34.
  2. Step 1, the hypotheses. H0: pA = pB against H1: pA ≠ pB, two-sided because a drop would matter as much as a rise.
  3. Step 2, the level. alpha = 0.05, chosen now, before the arithmetic.
  4. Step 3, pool. p̂pool = (300 + 340)/(1,000 + 1,000) = 0.32.
  5. Step 3, the standard error. √(0.32 × 0.68 × (1/1,000 + 1/1,000)) = √0.00043520 = 0.020861.
  6. Step 3, the statistic. Z = 0.04 / 0.020861 = 1.9174.
  7. Step 4, compare. |Z| = 1.9174 against 1.96, or equivalently p = 0.0552 against 0.05. The two comparisons agree because they are the same comparison.
  8. Step 5, decide, and say it correctly. Fail to reject H0. The honest report is that a difference is not established, not that there is no difference: an effect of exactly the size observed would produce data at least this extreme more than 5 per cent of the time under the null, and that is all we have shown.

4. The same test where it actually cost something

The committed experiment in datasets/experiment.csv ran 12,000 users per arm and saw 596 conversions against 643. Pooling gives p̂pool = 0.05162, a standard error of 0.002857, and Z = 0.00392 / 0.002857 = 1.3711. Again a failure to reject. Lesson 169 shows that this experiment had about a 55 per cent chance of detecting the effect it was looking for, which was knowable before a single user was assigned.

5. The two ways to be wrong

Never accept the null. Failing to reject means the data was not strange enough under H0 to cross a line you chose. A study with fifty users fails to reject almost everything, and that is a statement about the study rather than about the world. Report the interval alongside the verdict so the reader can see whether "no effect found" means "small effect ruled out" or "we could not have seen an elephant".

6. The programme

code/0167-hypothesis-testing.py · runnable Python, NumPy and Pandas only
p_pool = (k_a + k_b) / (n_a + n_b)
se     = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1/n_a + 1/n_b))
z      = (k_b/n_b - k_a/n_a) / se
p      = 2 * (1 - normal_cdf(abs(z)))       # two-sided
# grey against gold: z = 1.9174, p = 0.0552  -> FAIL TO REJECT

It runs both worked pairs and prints the five steps for each, so the arithmetic on this page can be checked line by line rather than trusted.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A two-proportion test returns p = 0.0552 at alpha = 0.05. What have you shown?
Failing to reject is a statement about the strength of the evidence against H0, not about H0 being true. Option 1 accepts the null, which no test licenses. Option 2 swaps the conditional round: the p-value is computed assuming H0, so it cannot be a probability about H0, which lesson 168 takes apart. Option 4 asserts an effect the test did not establish.
Why does the two-proportion test pool the two samples in the denominator?
The whole statistic is compared against its distribution under the null, so the denominator has to be computed under the null too, and the null says both arms share one rate. Option 2 describes a true effect of pooling without being the reason for it. Option 3 is false, since pooling is not simpler. Option 4 is false and the formula carries both sizes separately.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

Arm A converts 120 of 2,000 and arm B converts 150 of 2,000. Run the five steps at alpha = 0.05 two-sided, then say what the verdict would have been at alpha = 0.01.

Hint

Pool first, then the standard error, then the statistic. For the second part you need the critical value at the 1 per cent level, which is 2.576.

Show the worked solution
  1. Rates. p̂A = 0.06, p̂B = 0.075, a difference of 0.015.
  2. Pool. (120 + 150)/(2,000 + 2,000) = 270/4,000 = 0.0675.
  3. Standard error. √(0.0675 × 0.9325 × 0.001) = √0.0000629438 = 0.0079338.
  4. Statistic. Z = 0.015 / 0.0079338 = 1.8907.
  5. At alpha = 0.05. 1.8907 < 1.96, so fail to reject. The two-sided p-value is about 0.0587.
  6. At alpha = 0.01. The critical value is 2.576 and the statistic is further from it, so the verdict is the same and less close. Tightening alpha can only ever make rejection harder.

Sanity check. The two arms are the same size, so the pooled rate must fall exactly between the two observed rates, and 0.0675 is the midpoint of 0.06 and 0.075. A statistic near 1.9 on a difference of 1.5 percentage points at these sizes is the right order. If your Z came out above 3, the 1/nA + 1/nB was probably entered as 1/(nA + nB).

Your teacher. If step 5 keeps coming out as "so there is no difference", ask an agent to rerun the worked pair with 100 users per arm instead of 1,000 and report the verdict. Nothing about the world changed and the test still fails to reject, which is the clearest way to feel why the verdict is about evidence.
