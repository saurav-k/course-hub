A derivative is the exchange rate between a small input change and a small output change

foundation 11 min The first page of the module that explains how a model knows which way to move.

The one-minute version

  • A derivative is not a formula you look up. It is a number that converts an input nudge into an output nudge, and it is different at every point.
  • It is defined as a limit of slopes you can actually measure, so you can compute one before you can differentiate anything.
  • Everything this module builds rests on one line: f(x + ε) is about f(x) + f′(x)ε.
  • On a real machine that limit does not converge. The error curve is a valley with a floor, and knowing where the floor is is what makes a gradient check trustworthy.

1. The question a product manager actually asks

A credit model turns each application into a single number called a logit, and a function called the logistic sigmoid turns that logit into a probability of approval. Someone asks you: if we shift the score by 0.01, how many more people get approved?

The honest answer is not "a few". It is a number, and it depends entirely on where the applicant already sits. That number is the derivative, and this page is about what it is and how to get one.

2. A slope you can measure, before a limit you take

Pick a point x and a step h. Walk from x to x + h, see how far the function climbed, and divide by how far you walked. That is a slope, and nothing about it requires calculus.

slope over the step = ( f(x + h) − f(x) ) ÷ h f: the function, anything you can evaluate. x: the point you care about. h: the size of the step you take, a plain number you choose. f(x + h) − f(x): how much the output moved. Dividing by h turns that into a rate, so the answer is output units per input unit.

This quantity has a name, the difference quotient, and the whole of the next figure is what happens when you shrink h.

Shrink h and the line stops moving. The value it settles on is the derivative, written f′(x) or dy/dx. Those two notations mean the same thing and this course uses both.

3. Where a derivative fails to exist, and why that matters here

A function with a jump in it has no derivative at the jump, because the measured slope grows without bound as the step shrinks across the gap. That is not a curiosity. It is the reason no model in this hub is trained on accuracy.

Why the loss is not the metricAccuracy counts correct predictions, so it moves in steps: change a weight by a hair and accuracy either stays put or jumps by one whole example. A step function has a derivative of zero almost everywhere and no derivative at all where it jumps, so there is nothing to descend. Cross-entropy is smooth in the same weight, which is why models train on it and report accuracy afterwards.

4. The theorem that connects the two

Smoothness and having a derivative are related, and the relation runs one way only.

The reframingDifferentiability is the stronger property. Every differentiable function is continuous, and plenty of continuous functions are not differentiable.

Theorem. If f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a.

What is assumed: only that the limit defining f′(a) exists. The shape of the argument: write the change in the output as the difference quotient multiplied by the change in the input, then let both factors take their limits. The step that does the real work is that multiplication, because it turns a statement about a ratio into a statement about a difference.

f(x) − f(a) = [ ( f(x) − f(a) ) ÷ ( x − a ) ] × ( x − a ) An identity, true for every x other than a, because the two copies of (x − a) cancel. The bracket is the difference quotient from section 2, with the step written as x − a instead of h.
  1. Take the limit of the bracket. As x approaches a it tends to f′(a), which exists by assumption. That is the whole use of the hypothesis.
  2. Take the limit of the second factor. (x − a) tends to 0. Nothing is assumed here.
  3. Multiply the limits. The limit of a product is the product of the limits when both exist, so the left-hand side tends to f′(a) × 0 = 0.
  4. Read what that says. f(x) − f(a) tends to 0, which is exactly the statement that f(x) tends to f(a). That is continuity at a.
The converse is false, and you will meet the witnessThe absolute value function is continuous everywhere and has no derivative at 0: approach from the left and the measured slope is −1, approach from the right and it is +1, so there is no single number to settle on. That single point is not a footnote. It is the rectified linear unit, and it is the corner of the L1 ball.

5. The line the rest of the module rests on

Rearrange the definition and you get the sentence every optimiser trusts for exactly one step.

f(x + ε) ≈ f(x) + f′(x) ε ε: a small step, small enough that the curve has not had room to bend. f′(x)ε: the predicted change in the output, which is the exchange rate multiplied by the size of the nudge. The wavy equals sign means the two sides agree well when ε is small and drift apart as it grows.

Everything in Module 06 is this line, applied over and over, with a rule for choosing ε. Lesson 89 is about how far you can trust it before the curve bends away.

6. Worked: what a 0.01 nudge is worth

Back to the credit model. The logistic sigmoid and its derivative are:

σ(z) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−z)    and    σ′(z) = σ(z) × (1 − σ(z)) z: the logit, the model's raw score for one application, any real number. σ(z): the probability of approval, always strictly between 0 and 1. e: the base of natural logarithms, about 2.71828. σ′(z): the derivative, which for this particular function happens to be expressible using only the function's own value. Lesson 81 derives it.
  1. Evaluate the probability at the applicant's score. At z = 2.0, σ(2.0) = 0.880797. They are already likely to be approved.
  2. Evaluate the derivative there. σ′(2.0) = 0.880797 × (1 − 0.880797) = 0.880797 × 0.119203 = 0.104994.
  3. Multiply by the nudge. 0.104994 × 0.01 = 0.00105. The probability rises by about one tenth of a percentage point.
  4. Repeat at the decision boundary. At z = 0, σ(0) = 0.5 and σ′(0) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25, so the same nudge is worth 0.0025.
  5. Compare. 0.0025 ÷ 0.00105 = 2.38. The identical score change is worth 2.38 times more at the boundary.

Sanity check. σ′ is a product of two numbers that each lie between 0 and 1, so it can never exceed 1, and it is largest when the two factors are equal, which happens at σ = 0.5 and gives 0.25. Any derivative above 0.25 on this page is an arithmetic error.

What changes if the logit were −2.0 rather than +2.0? Nothing at all: σ′ is symmetric about zero, so the answer is 0.00105 again. Sensitivity depends on distance from the boundary, not on which side of it you are.

In words. A uniform score change does not have a uniform effect. It concentrates almost entirely on the applicants the model was least sure about, which is exactly the population a policy change is usually meant to reach.

7. The trade-off: the limit does not converge on a real machine

The definition says take h to zero. A computer cannot. Two errors fight as h shrinks: truncation error, the gap between a finite slope and the limit, falls; and round-off error, from subtracting two nearly equal numbers, rises.

The second formula straddles the point instead of stepping off it, which cancels the leading error term:

central difference = ( f(x + h) − f(x − h) ) ÷ 2h Two evaluations, one on each side, and the distance walked between them is 2h. Everything else is the same as the difference quotient in section 2.

Measured on the curve above, the forward difference's error falls by a factor of 10 for each factor of 10 in h, and the central difference's falls by 100. Those are first order and second order accuracy, and they are why every gradient check in this module uses the second formula with h = 1e-5 rather than the smallest step the machine can represent.

code/0080-derivative-as-a-limit.py · NumPy only · download
def forward_difference(fn, x, h):
    """The difference quotient exactly as the definition writes it."""
    return (fn(x + h) - fn(x)) / h


def central_difference(fn, x, h):
    """Straddles x instead of stepping off it, which cancels the leading error."""
    return (fn(x + h) - fn(x - h)) / (2.0 * h)


# Measured, not asserted: the ratio between consecutive errors is the order.
#   forward error ratio  10.06   (first order predicts 10)
#   central error ratio 100.00   (second order predicts 100)

The full program prints the whole table and finds each formula's floor. It needs no dataset, because this page's object is a function rather than a table.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why does the central-difference error stop improving below about h = 1e-5?
Subtracting two nearly equal numbers throws away significant digits, and that loss grows as the two get closer, eventually beating the falling truncation error. The first option is false: the function is smooth everywhere here. The second is a true statement answering a different question, since second-order accuracy explains the falling part of the curve, not the floor. The third is false: 1e-5 is represented exactly enough, and the trouble is the subtraction rather than the step.
A logistic model sits at logit z = 2.0 and the logit rises by 0.01. The predicted probability rises by roughly what?
The derivative at z = 2.0 is 0.104994, so the change is 0.104994 x 0.01, near 0.00105. The first option assumes the derivative is 1, which it never is for a sigmoid. The third uses the derivative at z = 0, which is the maximum value 0.25 and belongs to a different point. The fourth multiplies by the function's value rather than its slope, which is the most common confusion on this page.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

Take the curve this page has been using:

f(x) = sin(x) ex/3 Read as: the sine of x, multiplied by e raised to the power x divided by 3. A product of two functions, each of which you can differentiate on its own.

Find the exact derivative at x = 1.2, then compute the central difference at h = 1e-3 and at h = 1e-10, and say which is closer to the exact value and why.

Hint

It is a product, so you need the product rule: the derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second. The derivative of ex/3 carries a factor of one third.

Hint for the second half

Do not reason about which step "should" be better. Look at the figure in section 7 and read off where h = 1e-3 and h = 1e-10 sit on the central-difference curve.

Show the worked solution
  1. Apply the product rule. f′(x) = cos(x) ex/3 + sin(x) × (1/3) ex/3.
  2. Evaluate at x = 1.2. cos(1.2) = 0.362358, sin(1.2) = 0.932039, e0.4 = 1.491825. So f′(1.2) = 0.362358 × 1.491825 + 0.932039 × 1.491825 ÷ 3 = 0.540577 + 0.463477 = 1.004054.
  3. Central difference at h = 1e-3. The error is 2.832e-07.
  4. Central difference at h = 1e-10. The error is 3.830e-07.
  5. Compare. The larger step is closer, by about a third.
  6. Say why. h = 1e-5 is the bottom of the valley. At 1e-3 you are on the truncation side, still falling. At 1e-10 you are well past the bottom on the round-off side, climbing again.

Sanity check. The curve is rising steeply at x = 1.2, so the derivative must be positive and of order 1, and 1.004 is. A negative answer means a sign slipped in the product rule, and an answer near 0.36 means only the first term was kept.

Your teacher. If the limit is the part that did not land, ask an agent: "tabulate (sin(1.2+h) - sin(1.2))/h for h = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 and tell me what number it is approaching". If it was the round-off floor, ask it to print (1 + 1e-16) - 1 and explain the answer. Both are worth seeing run rather than argued.
