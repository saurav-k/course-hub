Module 08 · Expectation, Limits and Simulation · Lesson 153

Turn an integral you cannot do into an average you can

working 14 min The error does not care how many dimensions you are in. The variance does.

The one-minute version

  • A computer has uniform random numbers and nothing else. Every other distribution is a transformation of them.
  • Inverse-CDF sampling reads the CDF backwards. Rejection sampling proposes and filters, and its acceptance rate collapses with dimension.
  • Monte Carlo turns ∫f(x)p(x)dx into an average of f over samples, and its error is σf/√N.
  • That rate has no d in it. The constant does, and at d = 20 none of 200,000 points landed in the target region.

1. The only primitive is a uniform

A random number generator produces one thing: numbers spread evenly between zero and one. Every normal, exponential, Poisson or Dirichlet draw you have ever made was built from those.

The most direct construction reads the cumulative distribution function backwards. Draw a uniform u, then return the value at which the CDF reaches u.

X = F-1(U),   U ~ Uniform(0, 1) Read as: take a uniform draw U, then apply the inverse of the cumulative distribution function F to it. F(x) is the probability of landing at or below x, so its inverse asks "at what value is that much probability accumulated".

The picture is the proof. Uniform ticks are evenly spaced up the vertical axis. Where the CDF is steep, a wide band of ticks maps into a narrow band of x, so draws pile up there. Steep CDF means high density, so draws pile up exactly where the density is high.

2. When you cannot invert: propose and filter

Most CDFs have no closed-form inverse, the normal's included. The fallback is to propose from something easy and throw away the proposals that do not fit.

M multiplies across independent dimensions. Targeting a standard normal with a Cauchy proposal gives M = 1.52 and a 66 percent acceptance rate in one dimension, which is fine. In ten dimensions the bound becomes 1.5210 = 66, so acceptance falls to 0.015 percent, about 6,600 proposals per usable sample. That number, at only ten dimensions, is why the next lesson exists.

3. Monte Carlo: an integral is an expectation

The reframing is the whole idea. An integral weighted by a density is an expectation, and an expectation is something the law of large numbers lets you average your way to.

∫ f(x) p(x) dx = Ep[f(X)] ≈ (1/N) ∑i f(xi) Read as: the integral of a function f weighted by a density p is the expectation of f under p, and that expectation is approximated by drawing N samples from p and averaging f over them. The subscript p says which distribution the samples come from.

The estimator is unbiased by linearity of expectation, and its variance follows from lesson 143 with nothing new required.

Var(IN) = σf2 ÷ N  ⇒  standard error = σf ÷ √N Read as: the variance of the Monte Carlo estimate is the variance of f under p, divided by the number of samples. So the error falls with the square root of N, and sigma-f is the only other quantity involved.

Read what is not in that expression. There is no dimension. A grid over d dimensions needs points exponentially in d; Monte Carlo's rate does not know d exists. That is the entire reason the method is used.

4. The honest limit

The rate is dimension-free. σf is not, and when the region you care about becomes a vanishing fraction of where you are sampling, the constant destroys you even though the rate never broke.

5. Worked example: sampling a latency, and pricing an estimate

  1. Write the CDF. For an exponential with mean 415 ms, F(x) = 1 - e-x/415.
  2. Set it equal to u and solve. u = 1 - e-x/415, so e-x/415 = 1 - u.
  3. Take logs. x = -415 · ln(1 - u).
  4. Draw u = 0.25. x = -415 × ln(0.75) = -415 × (-0.28768) = 119.4 ms.
  5. Sanity check. u = 0.25 is the lower quartile, so the answer must sit below the median, which is 415 × ln 2 = 287.7 ms. It does.
  6. Now price a Monte Carlo estimate. Estimating π by counting uniform points inside a quarter circle gives σf = 4√(p(1-p)) = 1.642.
  7. Read the cost of accuracy. A standard error of 0.0001 needs N = (1.642 ÷ 0.0001)2 = 270 million samples.
  8. What changes if you reduce the variance. Every hundredfold rise in N buys one decimal place, so raw N is a bad lever. Halving σf instead is worth a fourfold cut in N, which is why importance sampling and control variates are where the real gains are.
code/0153-sampling-and-monte-carlo.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260902)

# Inverse CDF: uniforms in, exponentials out.
u = rng.random(400_000)
x = -415.0 * np.log(1.0 - u)
print("mean", x.mean(), "median", np.median(x), "theory", 415 * np.log(2))

# Monte Carlo, and its measured spread against sigma_f / sqrt(N).
for N in (100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000):
    est = np.array([4.0 * ((rng.random((N, 2)) ** 2).sum(axis=1) <= 1).mean()
                    for _ in range(400)])
    print(N, round(est.std(ddof=1), 5), round(1.64218 / np.sqrt(N), 5))

6. Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A Monte Carlo estimate has standard error 0.01 at N = 10,000. What N gives 0.001?
Error falls as one over root N, so ten times the precision costs a hundred times the samples. The second option is the strongest distractor because it is a true statement about a different property: unbiasedness says the estimate is centred, not that it is precise, and no amount of it removes the variance.
Monte Carlo error is called dimension-independent. What is the honest version?
The 1/root N rate has no d in it, but sigma-f does, and measured at a fixed budget the relative error went from 0.37 percent at d = 2 to total failure at d = 20. The second option is the overclaim the measurement disproves, and it is the one that leads people to trust a high-dimensional estimate that never sampled the region it was integrating over.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

You want to estimate the probability that a request exceeds 2 seconds, under a model where latency is exponential with a mean of 415 ms. The true answer is about 0.0081.

Find how many naive Monte Carlo samples you would need for a standard error of 10 percent of that probability, then say why this is a bad use of naive Monte Carlo and what changes if the threshold moves to 5 seconds.

Hint

The function being averaged is an indicator, so it is Bernoulli and its variance is p(1-p) with p the probability you are estimating. Notice that the quantity you want appears in its own error formula, which is the shape of the whole problem.

Show the worked solution
  1. Identify f. It is the indicator of exceeding 2 seconds, so each sample is 0 or 1.
  2. Its variance. Bernoulli with p = 0.0081, so σf2 = 0.0081 × 0.9919 = 0.00800.
  3. The target error. Ten percent of 0.0081 is 0.00081.
  4. Set the standard error. √(0.00800 ÷ N) = 0.00081.
  5. Solve. N = 0.00800 ÷ 0.000812 = 0.00800 ÷ 0.000000656 = 12,200 samples.
  6. Say why it is wasteful. Only about 99 of those samples exceed 2 seconds. The other 12,100 contribute a zero each and tell you nothing.
  7. Push the threshold out. At 5 seconds the true probability is about 0.0000059, so p(1-p) is roughly p, and to keep a 10 percent relative error you need about 1/(0.12 × p) = 17 million samples for about 100 hits.
  8. The general shape, and the fix. Estimating a rare event to a fixed relative accuracy costs about 100/p samples however small p is, so naive Monte Carlo becomes hopeless exactly where you most want an answer. Importance sampling fixes this by drawing from a distribution that visits the tail often and reweighting.

Sanity check. The sample count must scale like one over the probability, so moving from p near 0.0081 to p near 0.0000059, a factor of about 1,380, must raise N by roughly the same factor. 12,200 times 1,380 is about 17 million, which matches. If your two answers are not in that ratio, the p(1-p) was not simplified to p in the rare case.

Your teacher. Next time you simulate a rare failure, count how many of your samples actually hit the event. If it is fewer than about a hundred, your estimate has a relative error above 10 percent no matter how impressive the total sample count looks, and the fix is a better proposal rather than a longer run.
