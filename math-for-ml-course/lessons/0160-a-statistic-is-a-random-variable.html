The number you computed is one draw from a distribution of numbers you could have computed

working 12 min The sampling distribution, the standard error, and the difference from a standard deviation. · code/0160

The one-minute version

  • You have a sample, not a population. Every number you compute from it is a statistic.
  • A statistic is a function of a random draw, so by Lesson 127 it is a random variable, with a distribution of its own.
  • That distribution is the sampling distribution, and its spread is the standard error.
  • For a sample mean the standard error is σ/√n. A hundred times the data buys a tenfold improvement, and no more.
  • Standard deviation describes the data. Standard error describes the estimate. They differ by √n and confusing them is the commonest error bar in a benchmark table.

1. Two words that are easy to say and easy to conflate

Two analysts run the same experiment in different weeks and report different numbers. Neither made a mistake. That observation is the whole page.

The population is everything you would like to know about; the sample is what you measured. A quantity of the population is a parameter, a fixed number you do not know. The same quantity computed from your sample is a statistic, a number you do.

Notation keeps them apart: a parameter is a plain Greek letter and an estimate of it carries a hat. So μ is the population mean and x-bar estimates it.

The reframing You did not measure μ. You measured x-bar, which is a function of which rows happened to be in your sample. Change the sample and x-bar changes. That variability is not a nuisance to be apologised for - it is the object every remaining page in this module studies.

2. The mental model: re-run the whole experiment a thousand times

Imagine drawing a fresh sample, computing the statistic, writing it on a card, and repeating a few thousand times. Histogram the cards. That histogram is the sampling distribution of the statistic.

You never actually do this - you have one sample. The distribution exists whether or not you draw it, and the mathematics below gives its centre and spread without the repetition. Where no formula exists, resampling your one dataset - the bootstrap - approximates the histogram directly, and that is the onward road.

"Sampling" means two different things and you met the other one seven pages ago. Lesson 27 used it for survey design: how you choose which rows to collect, and what stratifying or clustering buys. Here it means drawing repeatedly from a distribution, in a thought experiment you never actually run. The designs on Lesson 27 change the sampling distribution this page describes, so the two ideas meet - but the word is doing different work in each.

4. The centre and the spread of a sample mean

E[x-bar] = μ   and   SE(x-bar) = σ ÷ √n x-bar: the sample mean, your statistic. μ: the population mean, the fixed thing you are estimating. σ: the population standard deviation, describing how much individual observations vary. n: the sample size. SE: the standard error, the standard deviation of the statistic across repeated samples.

Why, in four lines

What is assumed: the n draws are independent and share a mean μ and variance σ2. Nothing about their shape.

  1. The centre. Expectation is linear, so E[x-bar] = (1/n) ∑E[xi] = (1/n)(nμ) = μ.
  2. Pull the scaling out of the variance. Dividing by n divides the variance by n2, so Var(x-bar) = (1/n2) Var(∑xi).
  3. Add the variances. Independence lets variances add, so Var(∑xi) = nσ2.
  4. Combine. Var(x-bar) = nσ2/n2 = σ2/n, and the standard error is its square root.
The step that does the real work is step 3, and independence is spent there and nowhere else. Step 2 is where the square root comes from: the variance falls like n, so the standard deviation falls like √n. The square root is not an empirical fact about data - it is what happens when you divide a variance by n2 and then take a root.

The first says the sample mean is unbiased: on average across repeated samples it lands on the truth. The second says its spread shrinks, but only with the square root.

5. The square root is the thing you have to buy

Halving the standard error costs four times the data. Dividing it by ten costs a hundred times the data. On the module's session log, where σ = 242.04:

On the module's session log, where σ = 242.04: at n = 100 the standard error is 24.20; at n = 400, four times the data, it is 12.10, twice as good; at n = 10,000, a hundred times the data, it is 2.42, ten times as good.

This is why detecting a small effect needs a very large sample, and why that is a budget question before it is a statistics question. If an experiment is under-powered, more cleverness in the analysis does not fix it; only more n does, and the exchange rate is punishing.

6. Standard deviation and standard error are not the same number

The reframing The standard deviation answers "how much do individual observations vary". The standard error answers "how much would this estimate move if I ran the study again". They differ by a factor of √n, and putting the first on an error bar that means the second is the commonest bug in a benchmark table.

7. The proportion case, derived, because it is every ML metric

Accuracy, precision, recall, click-through rate and conversion rate are all proportions. So the standard error of a proportion is the one formula worth being able to rebuild from scratch.

Corollary of section 4. A proportion is a sample mean of 0/1 draws. Let Xi be 1 on success and 0 otherwise, so p-hat is their mean. Each Xi is Bernoulli(p) with variance p(1−p) from Lesson 129, and substituting that for σ2 in section 4 gives SE(p-hat) = √(p(1−p)/n) directly. No new proof is needed.

The independence in step 3 is the assumption that fails most often in practice. Repeated measurements of one user, or rows from one cluster, are correlated, so variances do not simply add, the true standard error is larger than this formula, and every interval built on it is too narrow. That is the commonest way a confidence interval lies.

8. Worked: the returning-visitor rate

The session log has 20,000 rows and 41.19 per cent of them are returning visitors, so treat p = 0.411900 as the population rate.

  1. Compute p(1−p). 0.411900 × 0.588100 = 0.242238.
  2. Predict the standard error at n = 100. √(0.242238 ÷ 100) = √0.00242238 = 0.049218.
  3. Measure it. Redrawing samples of 100 many times and taking the spread of p-hat gives 0.049271.
  4. Repeat at n = 1,000 and n = 12,000. Predicted 0.015564 and 0.004493; measured 0.015552 and 0.004490.
  5. Check the centres. p-hat averages 0.411962, 0.411926 and 0.411865 against a truth of 0.411900. Unbiased at every size.

Sanity check. A standard error must be smaller than √(p(1−p)) itself, since it is that divided by √n, and it must fall as n grows. Both hold. If your SE came out larger than 0.5 for a proportion, the n was left out.

What changes if the rate is nearer zero. p(1−p) is largest at p = 0.5 and collapses towards the ends, so a rare event has a small standard error in absolute terms and a large one relative to the rate itself. At p = 0.004 and n = 1,000 the SE is 0.0020, which is half the rate being estimated. A rare-class metric needs far more data to reach the same relative precision, which is the same arithmetic that made precision collapse in Lesson 126.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A benchmark table reports accuracy 0.82 with an error bar of ±0.38, taken from the standard deviation of the per-example correctness on 400 examples. What is wrong?
The error bar on a mean should be the standard error, which is the standard deviation divided by the square root of 400, so twenty times smaller. The first option is the misconception, and it is common precisely because the standard deviation is a real, correctly computed number - it just answers a different question. The fourth is wrong for the reason this whole page exists: accuracy is a statistic and therefore has a sampling distribution.
Your standard error is 24.2 and you want it down to 2.42. How much more data do you need?
The standard error falls with the square root of n, so a tenfold improvement costs a hundredfold in data. The second option is the natural and wrong guess: it reads the relationship as linear. The third is the same error in a different disguise. The fourth confuses the standard error, which does shrink without limit, with the standard deviation, which does not.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A model is evaluated on a held-out set of 2,500 examples and gets 2,050 of them right.

Find the accuracy, its standard error, and how large the evaluation set would have to be for the standard error to fall below 0.005. Then say what would have to be true of the 2,500 examples for that standard error to be honest.

Hint

Accuracy is a proportion, so use the formula from section 7. For the third part, set the formula equal to 0.005 and solve for n. The last part is not arithmetic: look at which assumption step 3 of the derivation spent.

Show the worked solution
  1. Accuracy. 2,050 ÷ 2,500 = 0.82.
  2. The variance factor. p(1−p) = 0.82 × 0.18 = 0.1476.
  3. Standard error. √(0.1476 ÷ 2,500) = √0.00005904 = 0.00768, so about 0.8 percentage points.
  4. Solve for the target. 0.005 = √(0.1476/n) gives 0.000025 = 0.1476/n, so n = 5,904. About 5,900 examples, which is 2.4 times the current set for a 1.5-fold improvement.
  5. Name the assumption. The 2,500 examples must be independent. If the set contains near-duplicates, several crops of one image, or many turns from one conversation, the effective sample size is smaller than 2,500, the true standard error is larger than 0.00768, and the reported bar is too narrow.

Sanity check. The standard error must be well under the accuracy itself and must fall as n grows - 0.00768 against 0.82 is comfortable. As a second check, going from 2,500 to 5,904 is a factor of 2.36, and √2.36 = 1.54, which is exactly 0.00768 ÷ 0.005. If your n did not satisfy that, the square root went the wrong way.

Your teacher. The habit to build here is asking, of any number in a results table, "what would this be if the experiment were re-run". If the answer is "about the same", say why. If it is "I do not know", you have found the number that needs a standard error next to it. Ask an agent to take an accuracy and a test-set size and give you the standard error, then check its arithmetic against section 7 rather than trusting it.
