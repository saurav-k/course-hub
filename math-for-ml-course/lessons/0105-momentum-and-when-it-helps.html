Momentum, and when averaging past gradients actually helps

working 11 min Sutskever, Martens, Dahl & Hinton, ICML 2013. · On the importance of initialization and momentum

The one-minute version

  • Momentum keeps an exponential moving average of the gradient and steps along that instead of the latest one.
  • Against a steady gradient the velocity reaches η‖g‖/(1−α), so α = 0.9 is a ten-times multiplier and 0.99 is a hundred-times one. Think in 1/(1−α), never in α.
  • It works by cancelling the component that reverses and accumulating the one that agrees. It changes direction, not step size.
  • Measured: at κ = 2 momentum is ten times slower than plain descent, and at κ = 200 it is twelve times faster. It is a cure for a stretched bowl, not a general accelerant.

1. The update

vk+1 = α vk − η ∇J(θk)
θk+1 = θk + vk+1 v: the velocity, a vector the same shape as the parameters, starting at zero. α: the momentum coefficient, between 0 and 1, how much of the previous velocity survives into this step. η: the learning rate, as before. ∇J(θk): the gradient at the current parameters. The second line: the parameters move by the velocity, not by the gradient. At α = 0 the two lines collapse to plain gradient descent.

The velocity is a running memory. Each step it is discounted by α and then nudged by the current gradient. Nothing else changes.

2. What α buys, exactly

Suppose the gradient is the same vector g on every step, which is roughly true along the floor of a long valley. Unroll the velocity:

  1. First step. v1 = −ηg.
  2. Second. v2 = α(−ηg) − ηg = −ηg(1 + α).
  3. Third. v3 = −ηg(1 + α + α2).
  4. The pattern. vk = −ηg(1 + α + … + αk−1) = −ηg(1 − αk)/(1 − α).
  5. Take the limit. For α below 1, αk goes to zero, so the velocity settles at −ηg/(1 − α).

The whole content is the geometric sum, and the factor it produces is worth memorising as a table rather than a formula.

3. Why it kills the zigzag

Split the motion into two kinds of direction. Across the narrow axis of a valley the gradient reverses sign every step, because you overshoot each time. Along the valley floor it points the same way for many steps.

A running average treats these completely differently. Alternating contributions largely cancel each other, so the velocity across the valley stays near zero. Agreeing contributions add up, so the velocity along the floor grows towards its terminal value. Momentum does not take bigger steps, it takes better-aimed ones.

4. Nesterov: the same shape, one line different

Nesterov's accelerated gradient evaluates the gradient after the momentum jump rather than before it:

vk+1 = μ vk − η ∇J(θk + μvk) θk + μvk: where you would land if you took the momentum part of the step and nothing else. The gradient is measured there, not where you currently are. Everything else is identical to the previous update. The effect is a correction applied after the jump rather than before it, which is what makes it more stable when the jump overshoots.

On a convex β-smooth objective this improves the rate from O(1/t) to O(1/t2). This page states that result rather than proving it: the proof needs estimate-sequence machinery that would be a page of its own and buys the reader nothing here. What is worth carrying is the comparison to Lesson 102's rate, and the measurement below.

An optimizer that helps on every problem does not exist. On the credit dataset, where κ is only 26.5, momentum is behind plain gradient descent at two of three accuracy targets. That is not a broken run: a well-conditioned surface has little zigzag to cancel, and the extra effective step can overshoot. Nesterov is ahead of plain momentum at every κ measured here, which is a concrete reason to prefer it.

6. The honest limit: momentum and noise

Lesson 104 established that a stochastic gradient carries irreducible noise. That interacts with acceleration in a specific way. On a smooth convex objective:

SGD: O( L/T + σ/√T )      accelerated: O( L/T2 + σ/√T ) T: the number of iterations. L: the smoothness constant, so the first term is the part that depends on the shape of the surface. σ: the size of the gradient noise, so the second term is the part that depends on sampling. The difference between the two: only the first term improves. The second is identical in both.

So acceleration improves the curvature term and leaves the noise term alone. Early in training the curvature term dominates and momentum helps a great deal. Later the noise term dominates and the two methods are equally effective. That is why momentum was written off in the 1990s and rediscovered for deep networks: the earlier work measured the asymptotic phase, and deep training spends almost all its time in the transient one.

Answer from memory before revealing.

Momentum with α = 0.9 multiplies the terminal step size by
The geometric series 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 + … sums to 1/(1 − 0.9) = 10. The first reads α as the multiplier rather than as the decay applied to the memory. The second stops the series after two terms. The third is the off-by-one you get from summing from k = 1 instead of k = 0, and it is the most tempting wrong answer here.
When does momentum stop helping an SGD run?
Acceleration improves L/T to L/T² and leaves σ/√T untouched, so once the noise term dominates the two methods match. The second names a number with no mechanism behind it. The third is backwards: deep non-convex training is where momentum helps most. The fourth confuses a cause with a correlate, since a bigger batch lowers noise, which makes momentum more useful rather than less.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

With η = 0.1 and α = 0.9, run four momentum steps for two coordinates that receive these gradients:

coordinate one: +1, −1, +1, −1     coordinate two: +0.2, +0.2, +0.2, +0.2 Read as: the first coordinate's gradient reverses on every step, which is what happens across the narrow axis of a valley. The second's is steady and five times smaller, which is what happens along the floor.

Find both velocities after four steps, then say which is nearer its terminal value and why.

Hint

Run the two coordinates as two entirely separate scalar recursions. Momentum never mixes coordinates, which is what makes this doable by hand, and it is also the exact property the next lesson changes.

Show the worked solution
  1. Coordinate one, step 1. v = 0.9(0) − 0.1(+1) = −0.1000.
  2. Step 2. v = 0.9(−0.1) − 0.1(−1) = −0.09 + 0.1 = +0.0100.
  3. Steps 3 and 4. −0.0910, then +0.0181. It changes sign every step and stays within about 0.1 of zero.
  4. Coordinate two, step 1. v = 0.9(0) − 0.1(0.2) = −0.0200.
  5. Steps 2 to 4. −0.0380, −0.0542, −0.0688. Monotone, growing.
  6. Its terminal value. −ηg/(1−α) = −0.1(0.2)/0.1 = −0.2000, so after four steps it is about 34% of the way there.
  7. Answer the question. Only coordinate two has a terminal value to approach at all: coordinate one's driving gradient has no constant part, so there is nothing for the average to accumulate.
  8. Read the ratio. After four steps the useful coordinate has 3.8 times the velocity of the useless one, from gradients that started five times smaller. That reversal is the mechanism.

Sanity check. Coordinate one's velocity must change sign on every step and stay bounded by roughly the size of its first step. If it grows steadily without changing sign, you have used αv + ηg and lost the minus sign, which is the update for gradient ascent and would climb the objective rather than descend it.

Your teacher. If the cancellation argument is the part to keep, ask an agent: "average the sequence +1, -1, +1, -1 with an exponential moving average at 0.9, and do the same for 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, and show both running totals". Watching the two diverge from gradients of similar size is the whole idea. If the κ table surprised you, that is the intended reaction, and it is why this page measures rather than asserts.
