Integrals in machine learning: the area you report is a number you actually compute

working 12 min Nine pages on taking things apart. This one puts them back together.

The one-minute version

  • An integral is an accumulation: chop, add, shrink the pieces. The ∫ sign is notation for that limit and nothing more.
  • Differentiation and integration undo each other, which is why an accumulation can be evaluated without adding anything up.
  • The most-quoted number in applied classification, the area under the ROC curve, is one of these accumulations, computed by trapezoids because ties make sloped segments.
  • That same area is also the probability a random positive outranks a random negative. Two descriptions, one number, and on real data they agree to fourteen decimals.

1. Accumulation, before any notation

Take a curve and the strip of space under it. Chop that strip into rectangles, add up their areas, then make the rectangles narrower and add again. The totals settle on a number, and that number is the definite integral. Everything else on this page is bookkeeping around that sentence.

2. The fundamental theorem, and why it is worth a proof

What is assumed: f is continuous on a closed interval. The shape of the argument: define the running total as a function of where you stop, then show its derivative is the thing you were accumulating. The step that does the real work is the mean value theorem for integrals, because that is what turns an average over a shrinking window into a value at a point.

Theorem (the fundamental theorem of calculus). Let f be continuous on [a, b]. (i) F(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt is differentiable with F′(x) = f(x). (ii) If G is any antiderivative of f, then ∫ab f = G(b) − G(a).

  1. Write the difference quotient of the running total. For h ≠ 0, (F(x+h) − F(x))/h = (1/h) ∫xx+h f(t) dt. Everything before x cancels.
  2. Replace the small integral by one value. By the mean value theorem for integrals, which applies because f is continuous on the closed interval between x and x+h, there is a point ch in it with ∫xx+h f = f(ch) h. So the quotient is exactly f(ch). This is the step that does the work.
  3. Shrink the window. As h → 0, ch is squeezed onto x, and continuity gives f(ch) → f(x). Hence F′(x) = f(x), which is part (i).
  4. Pin down every other antiderivative. Two antiderivatives differ by a constant, because their difference has zero derivative on an interval and the mean value theorem then forces it to be constant. So G = F + C.
  5. Subtract. G(b) − G(a) = F(b) − F(a) = ∫ab f − 0, which is part (ii). QED

This is the reason the sigmoid area above needed no adding up: σ is the derivative of the softplus, so evaluating the softplus at the two ends is the whole calculation. It is also why the exponential tails Module 07 will meet collapse to a single term.

3. The rule that actually runs: trapezoids

∫ ≈ ∑i (xi+1 − xi)(yi + yi+1)/2 xi: the horizontal position of the i-th point, here the false-positive rate. yi: its height, here the true-positive rate. (yi + yi+1)/2: the average of the two end heights, which is what makes this a trapezoid rather than a rectangle. The sum runs over consecutive pairs of points, so n points give n−1 pieces.

A rectangle commits to one of the two end heights. A trapezoid averages them, and that is exactly right on a sloped segment, which is what a block of tied scores produces. On the fitted scores below every value is distinct, so left rectangles, right rectangles and trapezoids agree exactly and the comparison says nothing. Round those scores to two decimals to create ties and the honest picture appears: the trapezoid gives 0.8777700091, the rank sum agrees with it to the last digit, and the two rectangle rules bracket it at 0.8725317408 and 0.8830082774, low and high by 5.24 × 10−3 each.

4. The ROC curve, built by hand

Sort every held-out prediction by score, highest first. Start at the origin and sweep the threshold downwards. Each instance you admit moves you up if it was positive and right if it was negative, and tied scores are consumed as one block so the curve gains a sloped segment rather than a false corner. Plot the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate as you go.

5. Worked: the area, and then the same number a completely different way

  1. Fit and score. The committed table ships features and a label, not scores, so the program fits a logistic model by Newton's method in ten lines and scores all 20,000 rows.
  2. Count the classes. 3,401 positives and 16,599 negatives, so 3,401 × 16,599 = 56,453,199 positive-negative pairs.
  3. Sweep and accumulate. The sweep produces 20,001 curve points, and the trapezoid sum over them is 0.87805013494452.
  4. Now forget the curve. Rank every score, add up the ranks of the positives, and convert. The Mann-Whitney rank sum gives 0.87805013494452.
  5. Now forget both. Compare all 56,453,199 pairs one at a time and count how often the positive scores higher. That gives 0.87805013494452.
  6. Take the difference. The largest gap between the three routes is 0.000e+00.
The reframing An area under a curve and a probability about ranking are not two facts that happen to agree. They are the same sum, written twice. Three routes agreeing to fourteen decimals on real data is the strongest evidence this module produces, and it is evidence of an identity rather than of an approximation.

Theorem (the area is a ranking probability). For a scoring classifier with P positives and N negatives, the trapezoid area under the empirical ROC curve equals Pr(S+ > S−) + ½ Pr(S+ = S−), where S+ and S− are the scores of an independently, uniformly drawn positive and negative.

What is assumed: nothing but the sweep itself. The shape of the argument: look at one step of the threshold, write what the trapezoid over it contributes, clear the denominators, and notice you are counting pairs. The step that does the real work is step 2, because that is where an area turns into a count.

  1. Take one step of the sweep. Lower the threshold past one distinct score s, admitting p positives and q negatives tied at s. The curve moves right by q/N and up by p/P.
  2. Write the trapezoid over that step. Its area is (q/N) × [TPRbefore + (p/P)/2], where TPRbefore is the fraction of positives already admitted, that is those scoring strictly above s. This is the step that does the work.
  3. Clear the denominators. Multiply by P N and the step contributes q × #{positives scoring above s} + ½ pq.
  4. Sum over all distinct scores. The first term counts every ordered pair whose negative sits exactly at s and whose positive strictly beats it, so across the sweep every strictly-winning pair is counted exactly once. The second counts half of every tied pair, exactly once.
  5. Divide back. The total over P N is #{S+ > S−}/P N + ½#{S+ = S−}/P N, which is the stated probability because the pair is drawn uniformly from the P N pairs. QED

Sanity check. The area must land in [0, 1], and a classifier no better than guessing gives 0.5, so a value below 0.5 means the scores are inverted rather than uninformative. The two routes must also agree exactly rather than approximately: they are one sum rearranged, so any gap at all is a bug.

What changes if the class balance changes? Nothing. Both rates are ratios inside a single column of the confusion matrix, so neither depends on the mix. Fawcett makes the point directly, and notes that skews of 10² are common and skews up to 10⁶ have been observed. That insensitivity is why accuracy and precision, which read across both columns, move when prevalence moves and AUC does not.

In words. An AUC of 0.878 says that if you draw one failing machine and one healthy one at random, the model scores the failing one higher about 88 times in 100. It says nothing whatever about any particular threshold, which is the single most common way the number gets misused.

def auc_trapezoid(fpr: np.ndarray, tpr: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """The definite integral of the ROC curve, by the trapezoid rule.

    A trapezoid rather than a rectangle because a block of tied scores
    produces a sloped segment, and a rectangle would mis-measure it.
    """
    return float(np.sum(np.diff(fpr) * (tpr[:-1] + tpr[1:]) / 2.0))

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A classifier's AUC is 0.878. What does that number mean?
AUC is a statement about ranking a randomly drawn pair, which is what the second theorem on this page proves. The second option is accuracy, a different quantity that also moves when prevalence moves. The third invents a threshold, and the whole point of the measure is that it has none. The fourth misreads the geometry: the area is measured from the horizontal axis upward, not from the diagonal, so chance scores 0.5 rather than 0.
Why does the AUC algorithm sum trapezoids rather than rectangles?
A block of tied scores moves the curve right and up at once, and averaging the two end heights measures that slope correctly. The first is false: a trapezoid is exact only for a straight segment, which is all this curve is made of. The second is false and backwards, since a rectangle is the cheaper rule. The third is false, and were it true the choice of rule would not matter at all.

Sweep the threshold and list the ROC points, sum the trapezoids, then count the pairs in which the positive scores higher and divide by 100. Say what it means that the two agree.

Hint

Ten positives and ten negatives, so every step of the sweep moves the curve by exactly 0.1 in one direction. There are no ties in this table, so no step moves in both directions at once.

Hint for the counting route

Do not enumerate a hundred pairs. Take each positive in turn and ask only how many negatives it beats, which you can read off the sorted list at a glance, then add the ten numbers.

Show the worked solution
  1. Sweep. Starting at (0, 0) and lowering the threshold gives 21 points, among them (0, 0.2) after the first two positives, (0.1, 0.5) after the block ending at .54, and (0.5, 0.8) after .38.
  2. Sum the trapezoids. Every segment is horizontal or vertical, so each contributes width times height, and the total is 0.68.
  3. Count instead. Positive .90 beats 10 negatives, .80 beats 10, .60 beats 9, .55 beats 9, .54 beats 9, .51 beats 7, .40 beats 6, .38 beats 5, .34 beats 2, .30 beats 1.
  4. Add and divide. 10+10+9+9+9+7+6+5+2+1 = 68, and 68 ÷ 100 = 0.68.
  5. Say what it means. The two routes agree because they are the same sum, which is the Wilcoxon rank statistic. So an AUC is a claim about ordering and never about any threshold.

Sanity check. The pair count must be a whole number out of 100 before you divide, and 68 is. A half appearing before the division means a tie was mishandled, and this table has no ties, so there should be no halves anywhere.
