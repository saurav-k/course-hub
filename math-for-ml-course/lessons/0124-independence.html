Independence is an equation, not a story about the world

foundation 11 min One equation, two things it is confused with, and the assumption inside naive Bayes. · Hajek, ECE 313, section 2.4.1

The one-minute version

  • A and B are independent when P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). That is the whole definition, and it is a numerical test.
  • Mutually exclusive is the opposite of independent, not a version of it. Disjoint events with positive probability are maximally dependent.
  • Checking every pair is not enough. Three events can be independent in every pair and still fail as a triple.
  • Conditional independence is a different claim again, and it neither implies nor follows from independence.
  • Naive Bayes assumes conditional independence, that assumption is usually false, and the classifier is often useful anyway.

1. The definition

A and B are independent when   P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B) A ∩ B: both events happen. The definition is symmetric: it says nothing about which came first, and A is independent of B exactly when B is independent of A. Note it is stated as a product rather than as a conditional, which is deliberate - the product form still makes sense when P(A) is zero, and the conditional form does not.

When P(A) is greater than zero, dividing both sides gives the reading that carries the meaning: P(B | A) = P(B). Learning that A happened moves your probability for B not at all.

The reframing Independence is a numerical coincidence that you check, not a physical story that you tell. Two events involving different machines usually satisfy the equation, and two events on one machine occasionally satisfy it too. The equation is the authority.

2. What it looks like when it passes and when it fails

The module's log carries one pair generated independently and one pair generated dependent, so the same two-line test can be run twice with opposite results.

Neither gap is exactly zero, and the left one never will be on finite data. Deciding how close is close enough is a hypothesis test, and that is Module 09.

3. Mutually exclusive is the opposite of independent

This is the single most common confusion in the territory, and it survives because both phrases sound like "unrelated".

The proof, which is three lines

What is assumed: A and B share no outcome, and both have positive probability.

  1. Use disjointness. If A and B cannot both happen then A ∩ B is empty, so P(A ∩ B) = 0.
  2. Use positivity. P(A) × P(B) is a product of two positive numbers, so it is positive.
  3. Compare. Zero is not positive, so P(A ∩ B) is not P(A) × P(B), and the events are not independent.
The step that does the real work is noticing that both definitions constrain the same quantity, P(A and B), and force it to two different values. And the direction is worth stating: disjoint events are not merely dependent, they are as dependent as two events can be. P(B | A) = 0, so learning A tells you with certainty that B did not happen. That is the maximum information one event can carry about another.

4. Checking every pair is not enough

Here is a complete disproof, small enough to verify by hand. Flip two fair coins, so the sample space is four equally likely outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT.

  • A = the first coin is heads = {HH, HT}
  • B = the second coin is heads = {HH, TH}
  • C = the two faces match = {HH, TT}

So independence of several events means the product rule holds for every subset of size two or more, not merely for the pairs. For three events that is four equations; for n events it is one for each of the subsets.

5. Conditional independence, and the "naive" in naive Bayes

A and B are conditionally independent given C when   P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C) × P(B | C), for every value of C The same product rule, but inside a world where C is already known. For every value of C is load-bearing: it has to hold when C is true and when C is false, not on average.

Conditional independence neither implies plain independence nor follows from it. Two symptoms can be dependent overall and independent once you know the disease, and two variables can be independent overall and dependent once you condition on something they both influence.

Multinomial naive Bayes assumes the features are conditionally independent given the class. In text that assumption is nearly always false: "free", "money" and "prize" travel together. The cost is precise, and it is worth a number.

  1. Suppose each of three words carries a likelihood ratio of 4 in favour of spam, meaning each is four times as likely in spam as in ham.
  2. Naive Bayes multiplies them, because that is what conditional independence licenses: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.
  3. Suppose in truth the three always co-occur. Then they are one piece of evidence, seen three times, and the honest factor is 4.
  4. Read the damage. The classifier has overstated the evidence by a factor of 16.
That is why naive Bayes probabilities are famously overconfident, and also why the classifier often still works: a 64 and a 4 both beat a 1, so the argmax survives what the probability does not. Teach yourself both halves. A course that says only "it works anyway" produces engineers who trust naive Bayes probabilities, and those numbers are not trustworthy.

6. Worked: two verdicts from one test

  1. Take the first pair. P(cache_hit) = 0.24928 and P(verified) = 0.70076, so independence predicts a joint of 0.24928 × 0.70076 = 0.174685.
  2. Count the actual overlap. 4,361 rows are both, so the observed joint is 4,361 ÷ 25,000 = 0.174440.
  3. Compare as a share of the prediction. The gap is 0.000245, which is 0.14 percent of 0.174685. Consistent with independence.
  4. Take the second pair. P(flagged) = 0.003960, so independence predicts 0.003960 × 0.70076 = 0.002775.
  5. Count it. 19 rows are both flagged and verified, so 19 ÷ 25,000 = 0.000760.
  6. Compare. The gap is 0.002015, which is 72.6 percent of the prediction. Not independent, and not close.

Sanity check. A joint can never exceed either marginal. 0.174440 is below both 0.24928 and 0.70076, and 0.000760 is below both of its own. If a joint came out larger than a marginal, the counting was wrong.

What changes if you compare the gaps rather than the ratios. The absolute gaps are 0.000245 and 0.002015, so the second is only eight times the first - which sounds mild. As shares of what independence predicted they are 0.14 percent and 72.6 percent, a factor of five hundred. On rare events an absolute gap is unreadable, and only the ratio carries the verdict.

7. Both tests, at scale

code/0124-independence.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
def independence_gap(a, b):
    """Return the observed joint, the product of the marginals, and the gap."""
    observed = float((a & b).mean())
    product = float(a.mean() * b.mean())
    return observed, product, abs(observed - product)

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two events each have probability 0.4, and they cannot both happen. Are they independent?
P(A and B) is 0 because they are disjoint, while P(A)P(B) is 0.16, so the equation fails. They are maximally dependent: learning A happened tells you with certainty that B did not. The first two options are the misconception, and they are tempting because "cannot both happen" sounds like "unrelated". The fourth is wrong because disjointness already fixes P(A and B) at zero.
Three events are known to be pairwise independent. What more is needed before they count as independent?
Independence of three events needs four equations: the three pairs and the triple. The two-coin example has all three pairs and fails the triple. The second option is irrelevant - equal probabilities have nothing to do with it. The third names a genuinely different property that is neither implied by nor sufficient for this one. The fourth is the misconception, and it is exactly what the counterexample refutes.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

In the log, P(verified) = 0.70076 and P(retries = 1) = 0.8480, and 14,817 rows have both.

Test whether the two are independent, and state your conclusion as a comparison of two numbers rather than as a yes or a no.

Hint

You need the product of the two marginals and the observed joint. Compare them as a ratio, not only as a difference - section 6 shows why the difference alone misleads.

Show the worked solution
  1. Predict the joint under independence. 0.70076 × 0.8480 = 0.594244.
  2. Count the observed joint. 14,817 ÷ 25,000 = 0.592680.
  3. Take the gap. 0.594244 − 0.592680 = 0.001564.
  4. Express it as a share of the prediction. 0.001564 ÷ 0.594244 = 0.0026, that is 0.26 percent.
  5. Conclude. The observed joint is within a quarter of a percent of what independence predicts, so the data is consistent with independence - and indeed the two columns were generated independently.

Sanity check. The joint must be below both marginals, and it is. On 25,000 rows, sampling noise on a proportion runs to roughly 1 over the square root of 25,000, which is 0.006, so a gap of 0.0016 sits comfortably inside the noise. Notice what this means: independence on finite data never shows up as an exact zero, and a gap of precisely 0.000000 would be more suspicious than this one.

Your teacher. When someone says two things are independent, ask them for the two numbers: what does the product of the marginals predict, and what did you observe. If they cannot produce both, what they have is a story about the mechanism rather than a test. That is not worthless - physical separation is good evidence - but it is not the same thing, and the equation is what the mathematics uses. Ask an agent to build you a small table where two columns are dependent and the dependence is invisible unless you condition on a third.
