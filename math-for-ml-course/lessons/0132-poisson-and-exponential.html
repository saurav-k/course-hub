Two distributions that are one process read two ways

working 13 min Count the arrivals and you get a Poisson; measure the gaps and you get an exponential. · Hajek, ECE 313, sections 2.7 and 3.5

The one-minute version

  • Events landing independently at a constant rate form a Poisson process, and it has two faces.
  • Count the events in a window and the count is Poisson. Measure the gap between them and the gap is exponential.
  • Estimating the rate from the gaps and from the counts gives the same number, which is how you know it is one process.
  • A Poisson forces variance to equal mean. That ratio is the diagnostic that tells you when your count data is not Poisson.
  • The exponential is memoryless, so it is the no-wear-out model, and wrong for anything that ages.

1. The Poisson distribution

P(X = k) = λk × e−λ ÷ k!,   for k = 0, 1, 2, … k: how many events happened, any whole number from zero upward with no upper limit. λ (lambda): the one parameter, the average number of events in the window. k!: k factorial. Mean λ and variance λ - the same number twice, which is a strong and checkable claim.

2. Where it comes from: many trials, each rare

Claim. Fix λ. Take a binomial with n trials and success probability λ/n. As n grows, its PMF converges to the Poisson PMF at every fixed k.

What is assumed, in words: many trials, each individually rare, with the product of the two held fixed.

The shape: write the binomial out, split it into pieces, and show each piece converges separately.

  1. Write the binomial PMF with p = λ/n. It is C(n,k) × (λ/n)k × (1 − λ/n)n−k.
  2. Split the coefficient. C(n,k) is n(n−1)…(n−k+1) ÷ k!, so pair its k factors with the k copies of n in the denominator of (λ/n)k. That bracket is a product of k terms each tending to 1.
  3. Pull out the part that does not move. λk ÷ k! does not depend on n at all.
  4. Handle the leftover. (1 − λ/n)−k tends to 1, because k is fixed and λ/n tends to 0.
  5. The remaining piece is the whole theorem. (1 − λ/n)n tends to e−λ, which is the limit that defines the exponential function, from Module 01.
  6. Multiply the four limits. 1 × (λk/k!) × e−λ × 1, which is the Poisson PMF.
The step that does the real work is step 5, and it is where the e comes from. Everything else in the derivation tends to 1 and is bookkeeping. That is worth knowing, because it tells you the Poisson is not a new idea at all: it is what the binomial becomes when trials are numerous and successes are rare.

An honest boundary. This is convergence at each fixed k, which is what the approximation is used for. Uniformity over all k at once is a stronger statement and this course does not prove it.

The convergence is not theoretical. On this file's flag rate the binomial at n = 256 and the Poisson at λ = 1.0138 agree to four decimal places, as the figure shows.

3. The process, and the exponential gaps

A Poisson process with rate λ is a stream of arrivals with two properties: non-overlapping stretches of time are independent of each other, and the count in a stretch of length t is Poisson(λt). Note that λ is now a rate per unit time, and multiplying by the window length is what reconciles the two readings.

Why the gaps are exponential

Claim. If N is a Poisson process with rate λ, the time T until the first arrival is Exponential(λ).

  1. Translate the question. "The first arrival is later than t" says exactly the same thing as "no arrival happened by time t", which is the event that the count in [0, t] is zero.
  2. Use the definition of the process. That count is Poisson(λt), so its probability at zero is e−λt × (λt)0 ÷ 0!, which is e−λt.
  3. Read off the CDF. P(T > t) = e−λt, so P(T ≤ t) = 1 − e−λt, which is the exponential distribution.
The step that does the real work is step 1, the translation from a waiting time into a count. That one sentence is the entire duality, and everything else on this page follows from it. Hajek states the full equivalence in both directions - exponential independent gaps also produce a Poisson process - and this page proves the direction it uses and names the other.
f(t) = λ e−λt for t ≥ 0,   F(t) = 1 − e−λt,   P(T > t) = e−λt t: an elapsed time, never negative. λ: the same rate as the process. f: the density, which starts at height λ and decays. F: the CDF. The third form, the tail, is the one to reach for, exactly as with the geometric. Mean 1/λ.

4. The keystone figure: one stream, two readings

5. Memorylessness again, now in continuous time

P(T > s + t | T > s) = P(T > t). The proof is one line shorter than the geometric's: the containment collapses the numerator, and then e−λ(s+t) ÷ e−λs = e−λt, because the exponential turns a subtraction in the exponent into a division.

Memorylessness is equivalent to a constant failure rate, which is why the exponential is the no-wear-out model. That makes it a reasonable first model for a request gap and a poor one for anything that ages: a disk, a battery, a cache that warms up. If your hazard rises with age, the exponential will under-predict late failures no matter how well you fit λ.

6. Worked: an endpoint at 2.3 requests per second

  1. Estimate the rate from the gaps. The mean gap across 25,000 arrivals is 0.43538 seconds, so λ = 1 ÷ 0.43538 = 2.2969 per second.
  2. Estimate it again from the counts. 25,000 arrivals over 10,884 one-second windows is 2.2967 per second.
  3. Notice what just happened. A duration and a count produced the same parameter to three decimals. That agreement is the evidence that this is one process.
  4. Predict the empty second. P(N = 0) = e−2.2967 = 0.1006, and the file gives 0.1016 across its 10,884 windows.
  5. Predict a long gap. P(gap > 1 s) = e−2.2967 = 0.10059, and the file gives 0.09928.
  6. Run the diagnostic. The counts have mean 2.2967 and variance 2.3388, a ratio of 1.018. A Poisson demands exactly 1, so this is consistent.

Sanity check. The two rate estimates must agree, because they are the same parameter measured two ways. If they had not, the arrivals would not be a Poisson process, and that disagreement is itself a useful test.

What changes if you widen the window to five seconds. The count becomes Poisson(11.48), P(N = 0) collapses from 0.1006 to about 0.00001, and the distribution becomes nearly symmetric. The Poisson changes shape with the window and the exponential gaps do not change at all, because the gaps are a property of the process while the counts are a property of the process and your choice of window.

7. Where this bites: size for the tail, not the mean

At 2.3 arrivals per second, the mean says one replica at 200 ms per request is plenty: 2.3 × 0.2 = 0.46 replicas' worth of work. But P(N ≥ 5) in a one-second window is 0.0834 by theory and 0.0858 in the file, so about one second in twelve carries a burst that a single replica cannot clear inside the second. Queueing at p95 is guaranteed by the arithmetic, not by bad luck.

def poisson_pmf_by_recurrence(k, lam):
    """Start at e^-lambda and step up with P(j+1) = P(j) x lambda / (j+1)."""
    value = exp(-lam)
    for j in range(k):
        value *= lam / (j + 1)
    return value

The trap that costs a factor of five numpy.random.exponential and scipy.stats.expon are parametrised by the scale, which is 1/λ, not by the rate. Fit λ = 2.3 and pass 2.3 and your samples have a mean of 2.29 seconds instead of 0.435 - the program demonstrates exactly that, 5.27 times too large. Every number downstream is then wrong and nothing raises an error.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

You have fitted a rate of 2.3 arrivals per second and want to sample the gaps. What do you pass to numpy.random.exponential?
The argument is the scale, 1 divided by the rate, so 0.435 seconds. The first option is the trap and it fails silently: the samples come out 5.27 times too large and nothing errors. The third would work only if you divided rather than multiplied, so it has the direction backwards. The second invents a conversion that does not exist.
Your count data has a mean of 12 and a variance of 47. Is a Poisson a reasonable model?
A Poisson has variance equal to mean, and 47 is nearly four times 12, so the data is over-dispersed and something is clustering the events. The third option names a real requirement that is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. The fourth is tempting because the window does set lambda, but changing it scales mean and variance together and cannot repair a ratio of four.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A GPU node fails on average once every 90 days, modelled as a Poisson process.

Find the probability it survives 30 days, the probability of two or more failures in a 180-day period, and the probability it survives a further 30 days given it has already run for 60.

Hint

The first and third are exponential tail questions; the second is a Poisson count question. Decide which window you are in before you choose the distribution.

Show the worked solution
  1. Get the rate. One failure per 90 days means λ = 1/90 per day.
  2. Survive 30 days. P(T > 30) = e−30/90 = e−0.3333 = 0.7165.
  3. Switch to counts for the second part. Over 180 days the expected number is 180/90 = 2, so the count is Poisson(2).
  4. Two or more. P(N ≥ 2) = 1 − P(0) − P(1) = 1 − e−2(1 + 2) = 1 − 0.4060 = 0.5940.
  5. Survive 30 more, given 60 already. By memorylessness this is just P(T > 30) again: 0.7165.

Sanity check. The first and last answers must be identical, and if they are not you have used the elapsed 60 days somewhere - which is exactly what memorylessness forbids. As a second check, P(N ≥ 2) over 180 days should exceed one half, because the expected count over that window is 2.

Your teacher. Two habits. First, whenever you meet count data, compute the mean and the variance and look at their ratio before you reach for a Poisson; a ratio near 1 earns the model, and a ratio of 4 says something is clustering. Second, whenever you meet a rate parameter, check whether the library wants the rate or its reciprocal - and check it by sampling and taking the mean, which takes ten seconds and catches the error every time.
