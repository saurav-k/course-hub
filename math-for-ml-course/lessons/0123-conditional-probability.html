Conditioning does not change the world, it changes what counts as everything

foundation 10 min The definition, the chain rule, and why every supervised model computes one of these. · Hajek, ECE 313, section 2.3

The one-minute version

  • P(B | A) = P(A and B) ÷ P(A), read as "the probability of B, given A". The bar is not division and it is not "or".
  • Conditioning shrinks the sample space to the rows where A holds, then renormalises. Same numerator, smaller denominator.
  • It can push a probability up, down, or nowhere at all, and this page shows all three on one file.
  • Rearranged, the definition is the chain rule, which is literally what an autoregressive language model computes.
  • P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers. On this file they differ by a factor of 75.

1. The definition, and the guard clause

P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(A),   defined only when P(A) > 0 P(B | A): the probability of B, given A. Read the bar as "assuming A is already settled". A ∩ B: both happen. P(A): the probability of the thing you are assuming, and it sits in the denominator because A is the new "everything".

The guard clause is not pedantry. Hajek puts it in terms an engineer will recognise: if you write a routine to compute this and it is handed P(AB) = 0 and P(A) = 0, it "shouldn't simply return the value zero. Rather, your routine should generate an error message". Conditioning on something impossible is not a probability of zero, it is a question with no meaning.

2. The picture, before the algebra

You throw away every row where A is false, then ask the same question of what is left.

3. It can go up, down, or nowhere

People often expect conditioning to reduce a probability, because "more information narrows things down". It does not work like that. Three examples from the same file:

  1. Up. P(flagged) = 0.003960, but P(flagged | not verified) = 0.010694. Nearly three times higher.
  2. Down. P(flagged | verified) = 0.001085. Nearly four times lower.
  3. Nowhere. P(cached) = 0.249280 and P(cached | verified) = 0.248930. Essentially unmoved, and Lesson 124 gives that its own name.

The unconditional rate is always a weighted average of the conditional ones, which is why the first two must sit either side of it. That fact has a name and it arrives properly in Lesson 125, as the law of total probability.

4. The multiplication rule, and the chain rule

Multiply both sides of the definition by P(A) and the guard clause disappears:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A) Read left to right as a recipe: the chance both happen is the chance the first happens, times the chance the second happens once the first has. Nothing here assumes the two are unrelated.

Apply it repeatedly and you get the chain rule, which factorises any joint probability into a product of conditionals:

P(x1, x2, …, xn) = P(x1) × P(x2 | x1) × P(x3 | x1, x2) × … × P(xn | x1, …, xn-1) Each factor conditions on everything to its left. The subscripts are positions in a sequence, not different quantities. This is always true, for any joint distribution, and it assumes nothing at all.

Why it is always true

What is assumed: only that each conditioning event has positive probability, so every factor is defined.

The shape: it is the two-event multiplication rule applied n minus 1 times.

  1. Start with two. P(x1, x2) = P(x1) P(x2 | x1), which is the rule above.
  2. Now three, treating the first two as one event. P(x1, x2, x3) = P(x1, x2) × P(x3 | x1, x2).
  3. Substitute step 1 into step 2. P(x1) P(x2 | x1) P(x3 | x1, x2).
  4. Repeat. Each new variable multiplies in one more factor, conditioned on all the ones already placed.
The step that does the real work is treating x1, x2 as a single event in step 2. The two-event rule was never really about two events - it was about one event and one more. Note what is not assumed anywhere: nothing is independent, nothing is identically distributed. The factorisation holds for any joint distribution whatsoever. What independence buys later is the right to drop the conditions, which is a far stronger claim.

5. Where this bites: a language model is the chain rule

An autoregressive language model does not resemble the chain rule. It is the chain rule, evaluated left to right, with a neural network supplying each factor.

The same shape sits under a decision tree, where a leaf's class distribution is a conditional probability given the path taken to reach it, and under a logistic head, which outputs P(y = 1 | x) directly.

6. Worked: the 25,000 cancels

In the log, 7,481 requests come from an unverified user and 80 of those are flagged.

  1. Write the joint as a proportion of everything. P(unverified and flagged) = 80 ÷ 25,000 = 0.00320.
  2. Write the condition the same way. P(unverified) = 7,481 ÷ 25,000 = 0.29924.
  3. Divide. 0.00320 ÷ 0.29924 = 0.010694.
  4. Now do it in one step. 80 ÷ 7,481 = 0.010694. The 25,000 appeared in both numerator and denominator and cancelled.
  5. Read what that means. The second route never mentions the full file. Conditioning is not a new measurement - it is the old one, taken over a smaller population.

Sanity check. The answer must exceed the unconditional 0.003960, because unverified users are the risky group, and it must stay below 1. It does both.

What changes if you reverse the bar. P(unverified | flagged) is 80 ÷ 99 = 0.8081. The numerator is the same 80 in both directions, and the answers differ by a factor of 75, because one divides by 7,481 and the other by 99. P(A | B) and P(B | A) are not variations on one number; they are answers to different questions. Turning one into the other is Lesson 125.

7. Both routes, at scale

def conditional_from_the_definition(b, a):
    """P(B | A) = P(AB) / P(A), with both measured over the whole sample space."""
    if a.mean() == 0.0:
        raise ZeroDivisionError("conditioning on an event of probability zero")
    return float((a & b).mean() / a.mean())


def conditional_by_restricting(b, a):
    """The same number, by throwing away every row where A is false."""
    return float(b[a].mean())

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

On this file P(flagged | unverified) is 0.0107 and P(unverified | flagged) is 0.8081. What explains a gap that large?
Both are correct, and both have 80 on top: one divides by 7,481 unverified requests and the other by 99 flagged ones. The third option is a true statement about this file that answers a different question - the two would still differ under independence, because the denominators would still differ. The first assumes an error where there is none, and the fourth invents an obstacle: reversing a conditional is exactly what Lesson 125 does, and it works.
What should a routine return for P(B | A) when P(A) is exactly zero?
The definition divides by P(A), so at zero there is nothing to return. The first option is the dangerous one: returning zero silently propagates a meaningless number into whatever called you. The second confuses this with a vacuous logical truth. The third is what independence would give if the conditional existed, but it does not exist, and a zero-probability condition is a bug in the model rather than a case to handle.

Practice about 10 minutes, with paper

Practice 1foundation

In the log there are 6,232 cache hits and 17,519 verified users, and 4,361 requests are both.

Find P(cache_hit | verified_user) and P(verified_user | cache_hit). Then say why they differ, given that the numerator is the same number in both.

Hint

Write each as a count over a count. The top is 4,361 both times. Look only at what is underneath.

Show the worked solution
  1. Condition on verified. Throw away every unverified row, leaving 17,519. Of those, 4,361 were cached. 4,361 ÷ 17,519 = 0.24893.
  2. Condition on cached. Throw away every uncached row, leaving 6,232. Of those, 4,361 were verified. 4,361 ÷ 6,232 = 0.69978.
  3. Say why. The same 4,361 requests are a small slice of a large group and a large slice of a small one. The overlap did not change; the population it is being measured against did.

Sanity check. Both answers sit between 0 and 1, and each must be at least the joint proportion 0.17444, because conditioning divides by something no larger than 1. Note too that the first answer, 0.24893, is almost exactly the unconditional cache rate of 0.24928 - which is the "nowhere at all" case from section 3, and Lesson 124 gives it a name.

Your teacher. If the bar still reads as friction, say it aloud every single time: "the probability of B, given A". Then find the denominator before you find anything else - conditioning is a denominator change and nothing more. Ask an agent to give you a two-by-two table of counts and to ask you all four conditional probabilities in it; the two that share a numerator are the pair worth staring at.
