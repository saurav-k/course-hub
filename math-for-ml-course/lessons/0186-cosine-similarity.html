A cosine score means nothing until you know its baseline

frontier 13 min Ethayarajh, How Contextual are Contextualized Word Representations? (EMNLP 2019). · the paper

The one-minute version

  • Embeddings do not fill the space. They occupy a narrow cone, so two random vectors already score high.
  • On the data this page ships, two random rows average 0.6399. An isotropic space would average zero.
  • So a raw score of 0.59 is below chance, and reported as "59 per cent similar" it will read as a match.
  • Subtracting the mean vector moves the useful gap from 0.27 to 0.76. One subtraction.
  • And the classic word embedding turns out to be a factorised mutual-information matrix, which closes lesson 183's loop.

1. What cosine keeps and what it throws away

Cosine similarity divides the dot product by both lengths, so only the angle survives. That is either exactly what you want or exactly what ruins the answer, depending on whether magnitude means something in your representation.

Query (1, 1, 0). Document d1 is (10, 10, 0), which is the same document repeated ten times. Document d2 is (1, 1, 1), which is the query plus one more topic. Cosine gives d1 a perfect 1.0000 and d2 only 0.8165. Euclidean puts d2 at 1.0000 away and d1 at 12.7279. Opposite orders, and neither is wrong: they answer different questions about what a longer document means.

Lesson 185 gave the repair. Normalise every row to length one on the way in, and then |x̂ − ŷ|2 = 2 − 2cos, so the two metrics rank identically for every query. The program asserts that on all three thousand rows of the committed data. This is why a vector database normalises on ingest: not for speed, but so that the index's Euclidean pruning and the user's cosine score cannot disagree.

2. The space is not a ball

Everything above assumes the vectors are spread over all directions. Suppose instead every vector carried a large shared component pointing the same way. Then any two of them would already be pointing broadly the same way before you compared anything about their meaning.

That is what real embedding spaces do. Ethayarajh measured it across every layer of BERT, ELMo and GPT-2 and found the representations occupy a narrow cone rather than filling the space: for GPT-2, the average cosine between two uniformly random words is roughly 0.6 in layers 2 through 8, and in the final layer it is so extreme that, in the paper's words, "any two words have on average an almost perfect cosine similarity".

Why isotropy would give zero, since that is the number every intuition is calibrated against. Fix the first vector and call its direction north. A uniformly random second vector is as likely to point in any direction, and the sphere is unchanged by flipping every direction to its opposite. That flip turns the cosine into minus itself. A quantity whose distribution is unchanged by negation has mean zero, and the cosine is bounded so the mean exists. The step that does the real work is the reflection symmetry, and it is exactly the symmetry the right-hand picture does not have.

3. What a real score is worth

The committed embedding matrix is forty-eight dimensional, built from six latent topics with a deliberate shared component. Sample two hundred thousand random pairs and measure.

The numbers: baseline 0.6399, same-topic pairs 0.8677, different-topic pairs 0.5943. Subtract the baseline and the picture is honest. A same-topic pair is +0.2278 above chance. A different-topic pair is −0.0456, slightly below it. The raw 0.5943 was almost entirely the cone.

The reframing Ethayarajh makes the same point in the opposite direction, which is worth holding because it catches you out: if the baseline is 0.99, then a self-similarity of 0.95 means the representations are well contextualised, because they are more different from each other than two random words are. A score is only ever high or low relative to its own space.

4. One subtraction

The cone is, to first order, one shared direction. Remove it: subtract the mean vector from every row and renormalise.

On the committed data the mean vector has length 0.8000 out of a possible 1.0, which is the cone measured directly: eighty per cent of the average embedding is the part every embedding shares. After the subtraction the baseline falls to 0.0002, same-topic pairs sit at +0.6341 and different-topic pairs at −0.1267.

The practical consequence is a threshold. On the raw scores a cut at 0.75 keeps 18.1 per cent of all pairs, of which 92.3 per cent share a topic. The same cut on the centred scores keeps 0.9 per cent of pairs, of which 100 per cent share a topic. Nothing about the threshold changed. The space did.

Subtracting the mean is a first-order repair, not a theorem. It removes the single dominant shared direction and leaves everything else. The baseline of 0.0002 above is that good only because this dataset was built with exactly one cone; real spaces have more structure than that. The instruction that generalises is measure your own baseline, not assume centring fixed it.

5. What a word embedding turns out to be

One loop left to close. Lesson 183 defined pointwise mutual information: the log of how much more often a pair co-occurs than chance would allow. Module 04 defined low-rank factorisation. Levy and Goldberg showed those two things multiply out to word2vec.

The shift is not decoration. With k negative samples the cells are PMI − log k, so at the usual k = 5 that is 1.609 nats subtracted from every entry, which drives most cells negative and is why the sparse variant of the method keeps only the positive ones. Their measurements ran on English Wikipedia: 1.5 billion tokens, a 189,533-word vocabulary, a window of two tokens each side.

6. Worked: reading a score off a dashboard

  1. A retrieval system reports 0.72 between a query and a chunk, on a space like the one in section 3.
  2. Do not read it yet. Sample two hundred thousand random pairs from the same space and average them: 0.6399.
  3. Subtract. The excess over chance is 0.72 − 0.6399 = 0.0801.
  4. Get a scale for that. A genuinely same-topic pair in this space averages 0.8677, an excess of 0.2278.
  5. Now read it. 0.0801 / 0.2278 = 35 per cent of the way from chance to a real topical match. Not 72 per cent of the way to perfect.
  6. Sanity check. A baseline must lie strictly between −1 and 1, and if it comes out near zero your space is already close to isotropic and none of this is needed. A baseline above 0.9 means almost every reported score is the cone and the metric is telling you nothing.
  7. What changes if you centre first? The baseline drops to 0.0002 and the same query would have to be rescored on the centred vectors. The point is not that the number gets bigger or smaller: it is that after centring you can read it at face value, and before centring you cannot.
  8. What it means in production. A fixed rule such as "keep everything above 0.75" is not one rule. On a space with a 0.78 baseline it keeps nearly everything; on a space with a 0.11 baseline it keeps nearly nothing. Swap the embedding model and the retrieval behaviour changes without a line of code changing.
code/0186-cosine-similarity.py · NumPy and Pandas only · download
scores = (U[i] * U[j]).sum(axis=1)          # 200,000 random pairs, unit rows
print(scores.mean())                        # 0.6399  <- the chance level, not zero

centred = unit_rows(V - V.mean(axis=0))     # one subtraction
print((centred[i] * centred[j]).sum(axis=1).mean())   # 0.0002

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A retrieval system reports a cosine of 0.65. What do you need before calling that a good match?
A score is only meaningful against its own space's baseline, and 0.65 sits below the 0.6399 baseline measured here. The second option is irrelevant once vectors are normalised, which is exactly what cosine does. The third matters for whether a better chunk existed, a different question from whether this one is good. The fourth is a fact about the model that tells you nothing about the geometry of the space it produced, which is the surprise the anisotropy result delivers.
Levy and Goldberg showed skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises which matrix?
The cells are PMI minus log k, with k the number of negative samples. The first option is the matrix you start from, before the log and the normalisation that turn counts into PMI. The second and fourth are real matrices that real methods factorise, which is what makes them good distractors: latent semantic analysis and TF-IDF are close cousins, and the result is interesting precisely because word2vec turned out to belong to the same family.

Practice about 12 minutes, with paper

Practice 1frontier

You compare two sentence encoders on the same query-document pair. Encoder X reports a cosine of 0.81 and encoder Y reports 0.55. You sample ten thousand random pairs from each space: X's mean is 0.78 and Y's is 0.11.

Find the excess over baseline for each, say which encoder discriminates better and by how much, and name the two things that break if you set one shared similarity threshold across both.

Hint

Do not compare 0.81 with 0.55. Compare each number with its own space's baseline first, and only then compare the two results.

Show the worked solution
  1. Encoder X. 0.81 − 0.78 = 0.03 above chance.
  2. Encoder Y. 0.55 − 0.11 = 0.44 above chance.
  3. Compare. Y separates this pair from chance by roughly fifteen times as much as X does, despite reporting a much lower raw number.
  4. Say what X's 0.81 was. Almost all of it, 0.78 of 0.81, is the cone. Only 0.03 is anything about this particular query and this particular document.
  5. First thing that breaks. The absolute value of a cosine has no cross-model meaning, so "above 0.75" is not one rule applied twice. It is two different rules that happen to share a number.
  6. Second thing that breaks. Applied to both spaces it retrieves nearly everything in X's, where the baseline is already above the threshold, and nearly nothing in Y's, where a strong match only reaches 0.55. The same configuration is a no-op filter on one model and an over-aggressive one on the other.
  7. What to do instead. Measure each space's baseline, and set the threshold on the excess rather than on the raw score. Or centre both spaces, which puts both baselines near zero and makes one threshold defensible.

Sanity check. Both excesses must be smaller than their raw scores, and both must be below 1. If an excess comes out negative that is not an error: it means the pair scored below chance for that space, which is a real and reportable finding, and it is exactly what a different-topic pair does in section 3.

Your teacher. If you want this to stop being abstract, take whatever embedding model you already use, sample two thousand random pairs of your own documents, and print the mean cosine. That single number changes how you read every retrieval score your system has ever reported, and it takes about six lines.
