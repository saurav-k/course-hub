Module 06 · Identity and access · Lesson 0600

A workforce grant attaches to a role, to a scope, or to a sentence

working 11 min Capability area identity-workforce, nine rows of the matrix: iam-principals, iam-roles, iam-policy-language, permission-boundary, conditional-access, workforce-sso, privileged-access, managed-directory, os-login.

The one-minute version

  • All four clouds answer the same question - may this human do this thing here - and they disagree on what the permission is written on.
  • AWS, Azure and Google Cloud each give you a role object. OCI has no role at all: a policy statement names a group and a compartment, and that sentence is the grant.
  • The number of independent gates one call passes ranges from three to seven, and the count is why a permission that works on one cloud is refused on another.
  • A cap on what an administrator may grant exists as a real object only on AWS. Google Cloud approximates it and leaks by design; Azure and OCI ship no equivalent.
  • Only Azure decides at sign-in time with one engine. The other three decide per request, per identity domain, or through a separate zero-trust product.

1. One question, four grammars

Every workforce authorisation on every cloud is the same triple: a principal, a permission, and a place the permission applies. The clouds differ in what carries the middle term.

OCI is the outlier worth naming plainly, because the matrix marks it as a genuine absence rather than a different spelling. There is no role object: no assumable identity, and no reusable bundle of permissions to attach to somebody. Permission arrives only as policy text naming a group, so a design built around roles has to be re-modelled around groups and compartments before it can be carried across.

2. When two grants disagree

A grant that says yes and a guardrail that says no meet somewhere, and every cloud documents that meeting differently. This is the section that decides whether a design ports.

Two of those four orders are softer than they look, and the pages say so themselves. Google's policy-types page notes that IAM in fact evaluates every policy type at once and compiles the result, so the numbered stages are a teaching model rather than a runtime sequence. On Azure only the first four steps are documented platform behaviour; the ordering between RBAC, Azure Policy and resource locks is a teaching aid this course inherits from the research and not a sequence Microsoft publishes. Design against the guarantees, not against the numbering.

3. Capping what an administrator may grant

Delegation raises a second-order question: when you let a team lead hand out permissions, what stops them handing out everything? Only one cloud answers it with an object built for the purpose.

The two absences are recorded with their reasons rather than assumed. Azure has no first-class boundary object; its nearest tools are platform-created deny assignments, the Azure Policy DenyAction effect, and NotActions on a custom role, each narrower than a boundary. OCI has no cap on the maximum a principal can ever hold: an IAM deny subtracts, but anyone with policy-write authority can add or remove it, and governance rules cap a child tenancy from above rather than capping your own administrators.

4. Deciding at sign-in, or deciding per call

The clouds also disagree about when the decision happens, and that is a difference of order rather than of feature. A sequence makes it visible.

The other two sit between. Google Cloud has the capability but sells no single engine for it: it arrives as Access Context Manager access levels applied through IAM conditions and Identity-Aware Proxy, which is the matrix cell reading delivered elsewhere rather than no equivalent. OCI does ship a sign-in engine - sign-on policies with Adaptive Security, which allow, deny or step up per sign-in on group, network perimeter, client and a risk score - but it is scoped to one identity domain, so a tenancy running several domains configures the engine more than once.

5. Where the workforce comes from

Nobody keeps their staff list in the cloud twice. All four federate an existing corporate identity provider, and the difference is what the cloud does with the users afterwards.

6. Three rows about power, machines and old protocols

Three remaining rows of this area are short but decide real designs. Standing privilege is governed by IAM Access Analyzer on AWS, which reports external and unused access, by Privileged Identity Management on Azure, which activates a role just in time with approval, by Privileged Access Manager on Google Cloud, and by Oracle Access Governance on OCI. Only Azure and Google Cloud make the elevation itself time-boxed and approved.

Operating-system login is authorised by cloud identity on three clouds - EC2 Instance Connect, the Entra VM login extension and OS Login - and OCI has no IAM-controlled SSH authorisation: keys are supplied at launch and managed inside the guest, with the Bastion service brokering the session. A managed Active Directory domain, for the workloads that still need LDAP, Kerberos and domain join, exists on AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, and not on OCI, where identity domains are the directory and Active Directory arrives by federation or a domain controller you run yourself.

The sentence to keep A permission that ports between clouds is one you can express as this group, this verb, this subtree. Anything that depends on assuming a role, on a boundary intersecting, or on a decision taken once at sign-in is a permission that has to be redesigned rather than translated.

7. What this certification tests

This area carries weight on all four professional tracks, expressed as the domain it belongs to. The AWS Solutions Architect Professional guide gives 26 percent to Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity, the domain that holds federation chains, permission boundaries and cross-account roles. Azure's AZ-305 opens with Design identity, governance and monitoring solutions at 25 to 30 percent. The Google Professional Cloud Architect guide gives designing for security and compliance about 17.5 percent. OCI's Architect Professional blueprint makes Architect Security Solutions its heaviest domain at 25 percent.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A design assumes a role, does work, and drops the role. Which cloud has no object to port that pattern onto?
OCI records a genuine absence: there is no assumable role object and no permission bundle to attach, so access reaches a group through a policy statement instead. Google Cloud and Azure both have real role objects, so the second and third options name a true detail that answers a different question - how the role is attached, not whether one exists. The fourth is the cloud the pattern comes from.
On Azure, a team lead with User Access Administrator grants more than intended. What stops the excess?
Azure ships no boundary object, and its RBAC model is additive: overlapping assignments union and never subtract. The first option is the AWS answer. The second is close and still wrong, because only the platform creates deny assignments. The third confuses NotActions, which trims one definition, with an explicit deny statement, which Azure RBAC does not offer to customers.
Which statement about sign-in policy is accurate across the four clouds?
Conditional Access is the single sign-in policy plane; the other three reach the same decisions per request, per identity domain, or through Access Context Manager and Identity-Aware Proxy. The first is false, because all four enforce MFA. The third is close - OCI Adaptive Security really does score risk - but it excludes Google Cloud's access levels, so it overstates the split. The fourth is false for the same reason.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

An AWS design gives contractors a role they assume, capped by a permissions boundary, with a service control policy denying every region except one, and requires MFA at sign-in through IAM Identity Center.

Port it to OCI. Name what each of the four AWS elements becomes, and say which one has no equivalent and what you would do instead.

Hint

Work through the four elements in the order this page took them: the attachment point, the ceiling that subtracts, the guardrail from above, and the moment the sign-in decision is taken. Exactly one of the four is recorded in the matrix as a genuine absence.

Show the worked solution
  1. The assumed role becomes a group. Contractors go into a group inside their own identity domain, and a policy statement grants that group verbs in a compartment. Nobody assumes anything and the caller keeps their own identity throughout.
  2. The region restriction becomes a policy condition. OCI conditions read request.region, so the guardrail is written into the statement rather than layered above it. A parent tenancy can also lock allowed regions on a child through a governance rule.
  3. The boundary has no equivalent. OCI ships no control that caps the maximum a principal can ever be granted, so the cap has to move: withhold policy-write authority from anyone who should not be able to widen the grant, and treat that permission as the real ceiling.
  4. MFA at sign-in becomes a sign-on policy. An identity domain sign-on policy requires MFA, and Adaptive Security can step it up on a risk score - but it is configured per domain, so a second contractor domain needs its own copy.
  5. Say what changed in kind. Three of the four elements survive as different objects. The fourth is gone, and the design now rests on who may edit policy rather than on a ceiling the platform enforces.

Sanity check. If your answer has OCI assuming a role, or names an OCI permissions boundary, it has translated a name rather than a mechanism - neither object exists. Exactly one element should have moved from enforced by the platform to enforced by who holds an administrative permission.

Your teacher. If the evaluation orders blurred together, ask an agent to walk one concrete request through all four in turn - the same actor, the same action, the same denied outcome - and to name at each stage which document states that stage. Watching the same refusal arrive from a different layer on each cloud is what makes the four orders stop looking alike.
