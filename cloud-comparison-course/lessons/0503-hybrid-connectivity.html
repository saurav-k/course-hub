Module 05 · Networking, delivery and edge · Lesson 0503

The circuits are the same on all four clouds and everything around them is not

working 10 min The eight hybrid-connectivity rows of the capability matrix, compared across all four clouds.

The one-minute version

  • Three shapes carry everything here: an encrypted tunnel over the internet, a private circuit, and provider hardware placed outside a region.
  • The private circuit is the convergent one. All four top out at 400 Gbps, and the interesting difference is at the bottom of the range, not the top.
  • The tunnel is where the units stop matching: one cloud quotes a packet rate rather than a bit rate, and one charges nothing at all for the service.
  • Remote access for individual people is a real two-two split, and the two absences are documented by their own vendors.
  • On-premises hardware is the widest divergence in the module: three clouds ship you a rack and one projects your own servers into its control plane instead.

1. Three shapes, and nothing else

2. The circuit, where the four agree

The direct ports are AWS Direct Connect at 1, 10, 100 and 400 Gbps, ExpressRoute Direct at 10, 100 and 400, Google Cloud Dedicated Interconnect at 10, 100 and 400, and OCI FastConnect cross-connects at 1, 10, 100 and 400. The partner ranges are the AWS hosted connection from 50 Mbps to 25 Gbps, the ExpressRoute provider circuit from 50 Mbps to 10 Gbps, Partner Interconnect from 50 Mbps to 50 Gbps, and OCI partner virtual circuits commonly rate-limited between 1 and 10 Gbps.

Two details do differentiate the row. Redundancy is expressed differently: AWS names three resiliency tiers, with the top one requiring two circuits at two sites and carrying a 99.99 per cent commitment, while Google Cloud fixes the link type at creation so it cannot be changed later, and Azure includes dual connections to redundant edge routers in every circuit. And the money differs: OCI bills FastConnect by the port hour with no data-transfer charge on it.

3. The tunnel, where the units stop matching

All four sell managed IPSec, and all four build it from two tunnels per connection. AWS terminates the two in separate zones at 1.25 Gbps each, with 5 Gbps tunnels available on transit gateway and Cloud WAN attachments. Azure's throughput follows the gateway SKU, and its own guidance places the aggregate below 10 Gbps. OCI terminates both tunnels on the routing gateway and, alone among the four, charges nothing for the service itself.

The unit that catches people Google Cloud HA VPN publishes its per-tunnel limit as a packet rate, not a bit rate: 250,000 packets per second, ingress and egress combined, and traffic is throttled at that ceiling. Google's own note is that this works out at roughly 1 to 3 Gbps depending on average packet size. A workload of small packets hits that ceiling at a fraction of the bandwidth a bit-rate figure would have promised, so capacity here is planned against packets.

Remote access for individual people is a genuine two-two split rather than a difference in packaging. AWS Client VPN and Azure's point-to-site gateway with Bastion are managed remote-access services. Google Cloud ships none, and points at Identity-Aware Proxy forwarding for administrative sessions instead. Oracle's own tutorial states that OCI does not offer a native remote-access VPN, and names Bastion for brokered administrative access and marketplace appliances for the rest. Both absences are the vendors' own words, and both leave a real design decision on your side.

4. Hardware outside the region, the widest divergence in the module

Three clouds ship hardware. AWS Outposts puts racks running native services in your building. OCI Compute Cloud@Customer and the Private Cloud Appliance run the same control plane and the same APIs on your floor, with Roving Edge as the portable form. Google Distributed Cloud covers the edge and air-gapped cases. Azure Arc answers the same row from the other direction, by projecting servers, Kubernetes clusters and data services you already run into Azure's resource manager, with Azure Local as its validated on-premises cluster.

The hosted VMware row splits on administration rather than on hardware. Azure VMware Solution and Google Cloud VMware Engine are operator-managed private clouds, while Oracle Cloud VMware Solution hands you full administrative rights over the software-defined data centre, which is the difference Oracle itself draws.

Two cells in this area are marked absent with a caveat the audit wrote into the data, and this page repeats the caveat rather than smoothing it. AWS carries no first-party VMware stack in this snapshot, following the change of ownership of VMware, and the audit's own note asks that the current offer be checked before the cell is trusted. Azure carries no broadly available metro or carrier edge tier in this snapshot, with the same instruction to verify. Treat both as "not in the verified research" rather than as settled facts about the platform.

5. Reaching another cloud, where AWS is the one without a product

This row inverts the usual expectation. Google Cloud Cross-Cloud Interconnect sells a direct link into another provider's fabric. Oracle Interconnect pairs a FastConnect circuit with an Azure or a Google Cloud circuit, and Microsoft documents the Azure side of the same pairing. All of these work only where the two providers already colocate, so the paired-location list governs the design. AWS ships nothing first-party here: a cross-cloud link from AWS is Direct Connect plus a colocation or partner cross-connect that you arrange.

Attaching a branch network's own routers is the mirror image. AWS has Transit Gateway Connect, which peers over GRE and BGP, Azure hosts partner appliances inside the Virtual WAN hub, and Google Cloud attaches them as router appliance spokes. OCI has no attachment construct of its own: third-party appliances reach the routing gateway over IPSec or run as instances in a hub network.

6. What this certification tests

Oracle's blueprint carries the sharpest instruction here. The Architect Professional exam topics name five domains, and neither multicloud connectivity nor migration is one of them - migration has become its own certification. So this page's cross-cloud row is architecture rather than examination material on that track. On Azure the opposite holds: AZ-305's "Design infrastructure solutions" at 30 to 35 per cent names recommending connectivity to on-premises directly as an objective.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A Google Cloud HA VPN tunnel carries many small packets. Which limit is reached first, and what is it stated in?
Google publishes 250,000 packets per second combined per tunnel and throttles there, so small packets exhaust it long before any bit-rate figure suggests. The first and fourth options state the wrong unit, which is exactly the mistake the limit exists to catch, and the fourth also names a circuit rather than a tunnel. Route count is a real limit elsewhere and does not bind here.
Which cloud answers the on-premises extension row without shipping any hardware at all?
Azure Arc brings servers and clusters you already own into Azure's control plane, so the row is answered by management rather than by hardware. The other three options each describe a real product that does ship hardware, which is why they are the wrong answer to this question and not wrong statements.
Which of these is the only cloud with no first-party product for a direct link into another provider's fabric?
A cross-cloud link from AWS is built from Direct Connect plus a colocation or partner cross-connect that you organise, so there is no first-party product. Each of the other options correctly describes that cloud's own offering, which is what makes them the wrong answer here.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A manufacturer runs a factory with poor connectivity, a head office, and a database that must stay in the building for regulatory reasons. It wants one cloud's control plane over all of it, and a 5 Gbps private path from head office.

(a) Which kind of circuit meets 5 Gbps, and on which of the four clouds could a partner circuit alone deliver it?
(b) Which two clouds could put a managed rack in the factory, and which one could instead manage the factory's existing servers where they stand?
(c) If the head office link must be built this week rather than this quarter, what shape do you use instead, and what do you give up?

Hint

For (a), read the four partner bars in the chart against the 10 Gbps grid line rather than against the 400 Gbps end. For (c), the answer is one of the three shapes in section 1, and what you give up is named in that figure's own caption.

Show the worked solution
  1. Match 5 Gbps to a circuit. Every cloud's direct port clears it easily, but the question is whether a partner circuit alone can. AWS hosted connections run to 25 Gbps, Azure provider circuits to 10 Gbps and Google Cloud partner circuits to 50 Gbps, so all three cover 5 Gbps. OCI partner virtual circuits are commonly limited between 1 and 10 Gbps, which covers it as well - so the answer is all four, and the chart's left-hand ends matter only below 1 Gbps.
  2. Place the racks. AWS Outposts and OCI Compute Cloud@Customer are both managed racks in your building, and Google Distributed Cloud covers the same row for edge and air-gapped sites. Azure Arc is the one that manages servers already standing in the factory rather than replacing them, which suits a site whose hardware is not the problem.
  3. Build it this week. A site-to-site IPSec tunnel over the internet, which every cloud offers and which needs no colocation and no provider lead time. What you give up is the consistent latency of a private circuit - the tunnel rides the public internet - and, on three of the four, you start paying for the gateway.

Sanity check. Part (a) should end with all four clouds, not one - if you eliminated a cloud, check whether you compared 5 Gbps against its direct-port range instead of its partner range. And the answer to (c) must be a tunnel: a metro site would not help a head office that already has internet, and hardware in the building solves a different problem.
