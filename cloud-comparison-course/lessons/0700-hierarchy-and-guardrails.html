Module 07 · Governance, tenancy and telemetry · Lesson 0700

A parent's policy binds a child differently on every cloud

working 10 min Capability area org-tenancy, 10 rows of the capability matrix: org-hierarchy, org-guardrail-policy, landing-zone, quota-management, resource-tagging, cost-management, commitment-discounts, cost-advisory, service-catalog and cross-account-resource-sharing.

The one-minute version

  • All four clouds put your estate in a tree, and all four let a policy attached high up reach everything below it.
  • The word "inherited" then means four different strengths. An AWS guardrail is a ceiling nobody below can raise. A Google constraint is a default a child project can switch off.
  • Oracle is the only one of the four with two nested hierarchies: compartments inside a tenancy, and child tenancies under a parent tenancy.
  • Eight of the ten capabilities in this area are answered by a product on all four clouds. The two exceptions are the findings, and only one of them is a real absence of the capability.

1. One shape, four vocabularies

Every cloud in this comparison sells the same shape: a root container, grouping levels beneath it, and a billing-and-quota unit where resources actually live. Policy flows down the tree and cost rolls up it. The names differ, the number of levels differs, and one cloud has a second tree above the first.

The bottom box carries the real weight. An AWS account isolates security, billing, quota and region scope at once, which is why AWS estates grow by account count rather than by folder depth; the organisation nests organisational units five levels deep and admits up to fifty thousand member accounts (Organizations quotas). Azure splits that same job across two objects: the subscription is the quota and billing envelope, the resource group is the lifecycle container whose deletion deletes its members, and management groups nest up to six levels below the root to carry inherited policy. A Google Cloud project owns enabled APIs, quotas and the link to a billing account, and that billing link is deliberately outside the tree, so moving a folder moves inheritance and never moves the bill. An OCI compartment nests six levels deep inside one tenancy, and every resource lives in exactly one of them.

One number this page does not print is how deep Google Cloud folders may nest. The research this course rests on flagged that figure as not re-read against the current Resource Manager quota, so it is left out rather than asserted. Read the quota page before you design a deep folder tree; the other three depths above are quoted from the vendor pages linked beside them.

2. What a parent can actually force

Here is where the four designs stop agreeing. Each cloud has an inheritable policy object, each one is attached at a level and reaches everything below it, and each one answers the question "can the child get out of this?" differently.

AWS writes two ceilings, one per side of the request. A service control policy caps what principals your organisation manages may do, including the root user of a member account, and it grants nothing at all: an account gets only what every parent allows. Its mirror image, the resource control policy, caps what may be done to your resources regardless of who calls, which reaches callers outside your organisation that a service control policy never touches. Both have documented blind spots worth memorising: neither applies to the management account or to service-linked roles.

Azure runs one engine that both refuses and repairs. Azure Policy evaluates resource properties and actions and carries effects for denying a creation, auditing it, modifying it, and deploying the missing piece after the fact. That second half has no equivalent on the other three: a policy that fixes what it finds is an operational tool as much as a guardrail.

Google Cloud inherits a default rather than a ceiling. The Organization Policy Service sets constraints that flow down the tree, and the hierarchy rules are explicit that a resource holding its own policy supersedes the one its parents set; a child may also decline inheritance altogether (hierarchy evaluation). The brake that actually holds is therefore an identity decision, not a policy one: withhold the organisation-policy administrator role below the organisation node and nothing lower can loosen anything.

OCI splits the job across its two trees. Inside a tenancy, Security Zones attach preventive recipes to a compartment and block a non-compliant creation outright. Across tenancies, governance rules are created by the parent, attached to the child, and locked: allowed regions, quota policies and tag namespaces that the child's own administrators cannot modify until the parent unlocks them.

The reframing Carry the question, not the product name. Ask "who can undo this, and where do they have to stand to do it?" On AWS and OCI the answer is a level above the offender. On Azure it is the assignment's own scope. On Google Cloud it can be the offender themselves, if they hold one role.

3. The rest of the estate

The other seven capabilities in this area are the machinery that makes a tree usable: a starting estate, limits, labels and the bill. All four clouds ship a product for each of them, so the comparison is about shape rather than presence.

Two of those rows hide a real difference. Google Cloud is the only cloud here that splits its metadata in two: informal labels for cost breakdown, and governance-grade tags whose values can be referenced by access conditions and organisation policy. And OCI is the only one whose quota object caps consumption below the provider limit, per compartment, rather than only exposing the limit the provider set.

4. Where a product genuinely is not sold

Cross-account resource sharing is the sharpest. AWS sells Resource Access Manager, which shares a subnet or a transit gateway itself into another account without copying it. The other three ship no general sharing service, and each solves the problem another way: Azure places the resource in a shared subscription and grants access at the right scope, Google Cloud shares networks through Shared VPC and everything else by granting access on the owning project, and OCI writes cross-tenancy policy statements. Read that as a packaging difference, not as a wall.

The service catalog row is the one real absence in this area. AWS, Azure and Google Cloud each sell a curated catalogue that end users self-provision from inside guardrails. OCI does not, and the research records the nearest path rather than a peer product: Resource Manager stacks and private Marketplace listings. If your design depends on a self-service catalogue on OCI, you are building it.

What this certification tests

This area is weighted heavily on two of the four professional exams. The AWS Solutions Architect Professional guide gives its Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity domain twenty-six percent of scored content, and multi-account design is what that domain is about (exam guide). Azure's architect exam puts Design identity, governance, and monitoring solutions at twenty-five to thirty percent, and names management-group, subscription and tagging structure among its objectives (skills measured). Oracle's architect professional blueprint carries governance inside its heaviest domain, Architect Security Solutions at twenty-five percent, whose objectives name Organizations Management by name (exam topics).

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

An organisation policy at the Google Cloud organisation node forbids external IP addresses. A project below it needs one. Who can make that happen?
Google documents that a resource with its own organisation policy supersedes its parents, so the child sets its own policy and wins - which makes the real control the permission to write that policy. The first option describes the AWS service control policy model, not this one. The third confuses the billing link, which sits outside the hierarchy, with policy. The fourth is the permission to use the resource, not to change the constraint over it.
A partner account outside your AWS organisation is calling one of your buckets. Which guardrail can cap that call?
Service control policies constrain only principals your organisation manages, so an outside caller is untouched by either of the first two - and the second is wrong twice over, since they do not apply to the management account at all. A permission boundary shapes one of your own principals. The resource control policy is the side of the pair that caps what may be done to your resources regardless of who calls.

Practice about 15 minutes, with paper

Practice 1working

A regulator tells you that a subsidiary's workloads must never run outside two named regions, and that the subsidiary's own administrators must not be able to lift the restriction themselves.

Design the control on each of the four clouds, name the object you would attach and the level you would attach it at, and say plainly which of the four leaves a gap you would have to close with something else.

Hint

Ask the same question of each cloud: after the object is attached, what would the subsidiary's own administrator have to be able to do in order to undo it? Then ask who can grant them that.

Show the worked solution
  1. AWS. A service control policy on the organisational unit holding the subsidiary's accounts, denying every region but the two. It grants nothing and caps every principal the organisation manages, including a member account's root user, so a local administrator cannot lift it. Note the documented blind spots: the management account and service-linked roles.
  2. OCI. A governance rule from the parent tenancy restricting which regions the child tenancy may subscribe to. Oracle states the enforcement is locked against the child, and only the parent can unlock it - the closest match to the requirement as written.
  3. Azure. A policy assignment with a deny effect on the management group above the subsidiary's subscriptions. The remaining question is who holds rights at that management group, since the control lives at the scope, not above it.
  4. Google Cloud. A resource-location constraint at the organisation node - and then the real work, which is withholding the organisation policy administrator role below that node, because a project holding its own policy supersedes its parent's.
  5. Name the gap. Google Cloud is the one that does not meet the requirement with the policy object alone. The restriction holds only for as long as nobody below has the role that lets them replace it, so the control is an identity control wearing a policy's clothes.

Sanity check. Two of your four answers should be a single attached object, one should be an object plus a question about who holds rights at its scope, and one should be an object plus a permission you must withhold. If all four came out identical, the page did not land.

Your teacher. The fastest way to test whether you have this is to say out loud, for one cloud at a time, the sentence "a child can escape this guardrail by ...". If the sentence has no ending, you have a ceiling. If it ends with a role name, you have a default. Ask an agent to give you a guardrail requirement and to argue the case that your chosen object does not actually hold.
