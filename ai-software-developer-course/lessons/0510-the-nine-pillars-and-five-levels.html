Module 05 · Agent-ready codebases · Lesson 0510

Readiness is a ladder with named rungs, so "make it better" becomes a target

working 12 min Read from the published Agent Readiness Model, which is the only vendor readiness rubric with its levels and pillars in public documentation. · Factory: Agent Readiness

The one-minute version

  • Five levels - Functional, Documented, Standardized, Optimized, Autonomous - and you unlock the next one by passing 80% of the criteria at the one below.
  • Nine pillars cross all five levels. They are the dimensions; the levels are how far along each dimension you have gone.
  • A monorepo is scored at two scopes: once for the repository, and once per independently deployable application inside it.
  • An organisation's score is the floor of the mean of its repositories, which is a deliberately pessimistic management choice rather than arithmetic necessity.

1. Five levels, and the gate between them

The Agent Readiness Model is Factory's published rubric for exactly the property lesson 0500 defined, and it is worth reading whether or not you use their product, because it is the only one of its kind with the levels and their example criteria in public documentation. Five levels, each described as a qualitative shift rather than a score band.

The gate is the interesting part. You do not advance by scoring well overall; you advance by passing 80% of the criteria at your current level, and until you do, the level above is locked and its criteria do not count for you at all. That has a consequence worth feeling rather than reading: on a level with a handful of criteria, one failure holds the whole repository back.

2. Nine pillars, which are the other axis

The levels say how far. The pillars say along what, and they are the part worth stealing outright even if you never run the tool. Nine of them, and the table below adds the column the vendor's own documentation does not: which ones a single engineer can move this week, without a platform team and without a budget.

Six of the nine are things one engineer can put in a repository on a Thursday afternoon, and the two that are not are the two that describe a running production system rather than a codebase. That asymmetry is the practical reading of the model: most of what makes a repository legible to an agent is inside the repository.

3. Two scopes, because a monorepo is not one thing

Criteria are evaluated at two scopes and the distinction is load-bearing. Repository scope is evaluated once for the whole repository: a CODEOWNERS file exists, branch protection is on. Application scope is evaluated once per independently deployable application inside it, so a monorepo's linter criterion comes back as 3 / 4 rather than pass or fail.

That is the right shape for a reason the next lessons keep hitting: a single root command that is correct for one of nine packages is not a command surface, it is a trap. A score that could not express "correct in three of four applications" would call that repository ready.

4. The floor of the mean, and what it throws away

An organisation's readiness is the average of its repositories' levels, rounded down. The documentation's own worked example is three repositories at levels 3, 2 and 3, giving an organisation level of 2.

Reported honestly, the aggregation is pessimistic on purpose: it makes a single weak repository visible instead of letting eight strong ones hide it. Reported as a productivity metric, it is a number nobody can move in a quarter, which is a different thing entirely. Lesson 0550 is where this course takes its own position on scores as instruments.

What is not published. The five levels, the nine pillars, the 80% gate, the two scopes and the aggregation formula are all documented and checkable. The list of criteria is not: the documentation names examples per level and per pillar and never enumerates the set, so "80% of the criteria" cannot be independently applied by anybody outside the vendor. Nor is there any published study relating a readiness level to an agent's actual success rate. Treat the model as a well-organised checklist with an unproven predictive claim, which is exactly how much weight it can carry.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Your repository has tracing and metrics but no instruction file. What does the model say?
Levels are gated, not averaged: a level is locked until 80% of the one below passes, and criteria above a locked gate do not count. The second and fourth options both describe averaging, which is the mental model the gate exists to replace. The third confuses the two axes, since a pillar says which dimension a criterion is on and never which level it sits at.
A monorepo has nine packages and one root test command that works for two of them. Why does the model notice?
Application-scope criteria are evaluated once per independently deployable application, so the result reads two of nine rather than pass. The first option inverts the two scopes, since repository scope is the one evaluated exactly once. The second is a category error and the fourth invents a ranking between pillars that the model does not have.

Practice about 15 minutes, with the repository open

Practice 1working

Place your capstone repository on the ladder, using only the example criteria quoted in the figure above.

Work out two things. First: for each of level 1 and level 2, how many of its four example criteria your repository passes. Second: which level the 80% gate actually puts you at, given that four criteria means you may fail at most 0.8 of one.

Hint

80% of four is 3.2, and you cannot pass a fifth of a criterion. Work out what that means for a level with exactly four criteria before you count anything.

Show the arithmetic
  1. Take the gate at level 1. Four example criteria, and 0.8 × 4 = 3.2. You cannot pass 3.2 of four, so you need all four.
  2. Apply it. A repository with a README, a linter and unit tests but no type checker is at level 1, gated, with three of four. It is one artefact away and none of its level 2 work counts yet.
  3. Take the gate at level 2. The same arithmetic. An AGENTS.md and pre-commit hooks with no branch protection and no devcontainer is two of four, which is 50%.
  4. Read the result the useful way. The output is not the number. It is the shortest list of named artefacts that moves you one rung, which for most repositories at this point is a type checker or a devcontainer.

Sanity check. If you scored yourself above level 2 without owning a devcontainer or branch protection, you have scored yourself against the pillars rather than against the gate. The pillars have no gate; the levels do.

Your teacher. Ask your agent to walk your repository and report which of the twelve example criteria in the level 1, 2 and 3 bands above it can find evidence for, with a file path per claim. Then check three of its answers yourself. The ones it got wrong are usually the ones where the artefact exists but is not where a reader would look.

Primary source to go deeper

The model itself, including the level table, the pillar table and the scoring rules quoted here: Agent Readiness. Read the pillar table and ask, for each row, what file in your own repository is the evidence. The rows where you cannot name one are your list.
