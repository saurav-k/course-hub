A machine reviewer reaches the defect it can state on one line

working 9 min Numbers recomputed from the committed evaluation JSON of the only open head-to-head review benchmark. · review-droid-benchmark

The one-minute version

  • A machine-reachable finding has three parts and no more: a line, an input that reaches it, and an observable wrong result. If you cannot write all three, the reviewer cannot either.
  • In the open data, the defects a reviewer actually caught are overwhelmingly logic bugs and runtime errors - 77 of 94 validated catches - rather than security or performance.
  • Precision is settable. Across six reviewers on the same fifty pull requests it ran from 55.3% to 96.8%, a 41-point spread that is a product decision rather than a limit of the technology.
  • The second thing a machine reaches is the standard you wrote down. That is not a smaller job than defect-finding; at Google's scale it was the larger one.

1. The data, before the interpretation

One open dataset lets you compare AI reviewers against each other on the same work. Factory publishes a benchmark of 50 pull requests - ten each from Sentry, Grafana, Keycloak, Discourse and Cal.com, across Python, Go, Java, Ruby and TypeScript - against a golden set of 167 manually validated bugs with file and line locations. Six reviewers have public runs in it, and every run's raw evaluation is committed as JSON.

It is run and published by a vendor who competes in it, and lesson 0620 takes that apart properly. This page uses only the parts a sceptic can recompute, and every number below was read out of results/<run>/*_eval.json rather than off the marketing page.

2. A finding has three parts

Read a hundred accepted review comments and the accepted ones share a shape. There is a site - a file and a line the change touched. There is a trigger - an input or a state that reaches that line. And there is an observable consequence - something a person could see go wrong. A comment missing any of the three is an opinion, and it is the kind of comment authors learn to skip.

3. Which kinds actually arrive

The benchmark's ground-truth validation directory classifies every one of one reviewer's comments by hand, with a verdict, a severity and a bug_type. Ninety-eight comments were validated; 94 held up as real. Their distribution is the most concrete answer available to "what does an AI reviewer catch".

That distribution is a warning as much as a description. A vendor that markets an AI reviewer on security findings is selling you 6% of its output, and the deterministic tools that own the other 94% of the security question are module 7's three scanners rather than this module's subject.

The reframing An AI reviewer is a logic-bug instrument that also happens to read your standards file. Buy it for the first, configure it for the second, and do not let it be your security programme.

4. Precision is a dial somebody already turned

Precision is the share of a reviewer's comments that are real defects. It is the number you feel every day, because it is the share of comments that were worth reading. Across six reviewers on identical work it ranged from 55.3% to 96.8%.

That is why the adjacent lesson exists. A vendor can raise precision to nearly a hundred by saying less, and the number that would tell you what it cost is the one that gets published least: in this same benchmark, no reviewer found more than 61.7% of the 167 validated bugs. Lesson 0620 is that half, and reading this page without it is reading a sales sheet.

5. The second catch: what you wrote down

The other job a machine reaches is the written standard, and at scale it is the bigger one. Google's AutoCommenter, running across C++, Java, Python and Go codebases each "exceeding 100 million lines", produced comments for 330 distinct best-practice URLs and covers 68% of historical human comments that cited one. Its usefulness ratio moved from about 54% to 66% to a target of 80% - and both moves came from suppressing outdated practices and improving summaries rather than from a better model.

Every rule you enforce by hand today is a comment you will write again. Lesson 0640 is where it becomes a file, and the scorecard below is how you decide which of your comments qualify.

Of 94 hand-validated true-positive review comments, which kinds dominated?
Logic bugs were 54 and runtime errors 23. Security was 6, which is why the first option is the trap: it is what reviewers are marketed on and it is 6% of the output. Performance was 3 and race conditions 6, so both of those are the long tail rather than the body of the distribution.
A vendor advertises 96% precision. What does that number alone tell you?
Precision is the share of comments that were right, so a high one is reached by staying quiet - and the 96.8% reviewer emitted 93 comments while a 57.9% one emitted 178 and found more bugs. The first option confuses precision with recall, which lesson 0620 separates. The second assumes a single quality axis. The third describes a real methodological problem in this benchmark, but it is not what a precision figure reports.

Practice about 25 minutes, with a recent pull request

Practice 1working

Take the last real bug your team shipped to production, and find the pull request that introduced it.

Write the finding a reviewer would have had to produce, in the three parts from the figure above: the site, the trigger, and the observable consequence. Use the diff as it was at review time, not the code as it is now.

Then score it on the widget above, and say whether the defect was reachable at all from the artefacts a reviewer could open.

Hint

The honest failures are the ones where you cannot write the trigger without knowing something that was in a ticket, a Slack thread or somebody's head. Write that down rather than working around it - it is the useful half of the exercise.

Show what the three parts usually reveal
  1. Write the site first. If the defect's site is not in the diff, no reviewer that sees only the diff could have caught it, and lesson 0630 is about which architectures see more than the diff.
  2. Write the trigger as a value. "A tenant with no billing address" is a trigger. "Edge cases" is not. If yours needs a fact from a ticket, the finding was never machine-reachable and no configuration would have changed that.
  3. Write the consequence as something observable. If the wrong result is "confusing for users", the comment would have been an opinion, and an author would have skipped it.
  4. Score it. Three parts present and a rule written down puts you in the top band. Anything below half means this defect was going to reach production whatever you install.

Sanity check. Most teams find that one in three recent production bugs was machine-reachable in this sense, which matches the recall range you are about to meet in 0620. If every bug you check scores in the top band, you picked easy bugs - go back and take the one that took longest to diagnose.

Your teacher. If the three parts feel arbitrary, hand your agent five review comments from your own repository and ask it to split each into site, trigger and consequence, marking any part it has to invent. The comments where it invents the trigger are the ones your team argues about, and they are usually the ones nobody acts on.
