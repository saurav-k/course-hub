Module 06 · Agentic code review · Lesson 0670

One company bet that understanding a change is scarcer than writing one

frontier 12 min Built only from Cognition's own public documentation and engineering posts, and read as a claim you can argue with. · Devin Review documentation

The one-minute version

  • Three products, one thesis: Devin Review for the diff, DeepWiki for the repository, Codemaps for the task. None of them writes the feature - all three manufacture understanding.
  • The design decision worth stealing is the six-band severity taxonomy: three finding classes, two bands each, with an administrator toggle per band deciding what reaches GitHub at all.
  • The decision worth noticing is that Devin Review reads nine instruction filenames including its competitors'. A reviewer that reads .cursorrules has conceded that the standard belongs to the repository.
  • The thing to hold at arm's length is every number: Cognition publishes no precision or recall for Devin Review, and it is not one of the six tools in the open benchmark this module has been reading.

1. The thesis, in their words

Cognition's argument is that as agents write more code, the constraint moves: "code review - not code generation - is now the bottleneck to shipping great products". Their engineering post puts the same thing as an observation about their own work - "as we ship more code with agents, the bottleneck shifted from writing code to reviewing it" - alongside the figure that they merged 659 agent pull requests in a week against 154 in their best week the year before.

Take the mechanism and leave the measurement. The mechanism is that review capacity does not scale with generation capacity, which is arithmetic. The measurement is one company's account of its own codebase with no comparison group, which is a story.

2. Six severity bands, and why not three

Devin Review sorts findings into three classes: bugs, described as "high-confidence issues that require immediate attention" plus a lower-severity tier; flags, split into ones that "warrant further investigation" and informational annotations; and security, split into critical vulnerabilities and warnings, with a CWE classification attached.

Six bands is more than most tools ship, and the reason is triage cost rather than precision. A reader deciding what to look at first needs the classes to sort into different actions, and three classes with one band each cannot express "this is a security finding but you can read it on Thursday". Each band also carries its own administrator toggle for whether it is posted to GitHub at all, which makes the taxonomy a volume control as well as a label.

Three vocabularies for one job, and they are not interchangeable: Factory's P0 is defined as a "critical issue blocking release or operations", which mixes urgency with consequence, while the benchmark's severities are consequence alone. Map whichever one you adopt onto your own triage before you switch it on, or the labels will sort into no action at all.

3. Four ways in, each removing a different barrier

Devin Review can be reached four ways: a web application at app.devin.ai/review, the comment command /devin review on any pull request, swapping github.com for devinreview.com in a pull-request URL, and npx devin-review {pr-url} from a local clone. That is more entry points than the feature strictly needs, and the reason is adoption rather than capability.

4. The reviewer that reads its rivals' configuration

This is the beat to take away. Devin Review's documentation lists the instruction files it respects: REVIEW.md, AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, CONTRIBUTING.md, .cursorrules, .windsurfrules, .cursor/rules, .coderabbit.yaml and greptile.json. Four of those are competitors' formats.

Read as strategy it is switching-cost removal: a team that already wrote rules for another tool can try this one with no work. Read as a claim about the field it is stronger, and it is the claim 0640 is built on - the standard belongs to the repository rather than to the vendor, and a product that argued otherwise would be arguing for its own lock-in. Whatever happens to these companies, that direction is the one to build your own repository for.

The reframing When a vendor makes its product read its competitors' configuration files, it has told you where it thinks the durable artefact lives. Write your rules once, in your repository, and let the reviewers compete for the privilege of reading them.

5. The comprehension layer, as a real file

DeepWiki is the repo-aware layer from 0630 made concrete: an indexed wiki of a repository with architecture diagrams, source links and summaries. It is steered by an optional checked-in file, and its cap is documented - a maximum of 30 pages for standard organisations and 80 for enterprise ones. A free version works against public GitHub repositories, so you can point it at something you already know and judge the output yourself.

Two things about the format are worth stealing whatever tool you use. repo_notes is a place to write down the facts a reader cannot derive from the code - which is the same job as AGENTS.md from module 4, aimed at a different consumer. And when you supply pages, the documentation says only those pages are generated, "no more, no less", which turns a generated wiki from a firehose into a document with an editor.

Windsurf Codemaps is the third surface: task-scoped generated maps of a codebase, opened with a keyboard shortcut, referenced into the agent with @{codemap}, and running a fast path on SWE-1.5 or a smart path on Claude Sonnet 4.5. The supporting evidence Cognition offers is third-party estimates of ramp time - three to nine months for a new engineer - rather than a study of Codemaps itself. Coding Harness Engineering, lesson 0180 takes Windsurf apart at source level; this page stays on the comprehension story.

6. The numbers, and what is missing from them

MARKETING, and the absence matters more than the numbers. Cognition publishes no precision figure and no recall figure for Devin Review, and Devin Review is not one of the six tools in the open head-to-head benchmark 0620 reads. So there is no way to place it on the same axes as the reviewers this module measured, and any comparison you see between Devin Review and those six was invented by whoever wrote it. What would make these figures evidence: a stated denominator, a comparison group, and the miss rate. Ask for the third one first.

7. What survives if the products do not

Three things, and none of them needs this company to exist. Understanding is a product surface: a repository that is hard to understand is expensive for humans and agents alike, which is module 5's whole argument arriving from a different direction. A severity taxonomy is a triage tool, so pick the number of bands your team will actually sort into, not the number a vendor ships. And the configuration belongs to the repository, which is the one thing every serious reviewer in this module now agrees on.

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

What does a reviewer reading .cursorrules and .coderabbit.yaml tell you?
Reading a rival's configuration is a concession that the durable artefact is the repository's file, not the vendor's format - which is exactly why 0640 tells you to write it once. The first describes CodeRabbit running Semgrep inside a review, a different thing entirely. The second is untrue: nine filenames with different syntaxes is the opposite of a standard. The third is an inference nothing in the documentation supports.
Why can Devin Review not be placed on the same axes as the six benchmarked tools?
Cognition publishes neither number and the product is not in the open benchmark, so any point plotted for it would be invented. The first is a real difference and a mappable one - the benchmark scores against its own severities regardless. The third is false: it reviews pull requests. The fourth describes one of three documented trigger modes and would not stop anyone measuring it.

Practice the module assignment - several sessions, on your capstone repository

Module assignmentfrontier

Goal. Stand up an AI reviewer on a real repository, constrain it with a rules file you wrote, and measure it honestly enough to decide whether to keep it.

Do all seven.

  1. Add the review workflow from 0650 to a repository you control, scoped with paths: to one directory, and confirm it runs on a pull request that is not a draft.
  2. Write a rules file with at least three rules and at least one "do not flag" entry. Every rule must come from a comment you or a colleague has actually left more than once.
  3. Open five pull requests, or replay five recent ones on a branch. Record every comment the reviewer makes, with file and line.
  4. Classify each comment yourself into wrong, correct and acted on, or correct and ignored. Compute precision and acceptance from the same table, as in 0660.
  5. Do the cheap recall check from 0620: take the last five defects your team shipped, replay the diffs that introduced them, and report the fraction the reviewer catches - saying plainly that five is not a measurement.
  6. Configure the gates: required checks that include your verify job and exclude the AI review, code-owner review on the directory you scoped, and dismissal of stale approvals.
  7. Write one paragraph deciding whether to keep the reviewer, citing your own numbers rather than any vendor's.

Hand in. The workflow file, the rules file, the classified comment table with your precision and acceptance figures, the five-defect recall check, the ruleset configuration, and the decision paragraph.

Feeds the capstone. The workflow, the rules file and the gate configuration are the capstone's review stage, and the classified comment table is the baseline the later modules measure against.

Hint

Do step 2 before step 1 if you can. A reviewer switched on with no rules file makes its first impression with default behaviour, and a team that mutes it in week one will not un-mute it in week three because you improved the configuration.

Show what a complete hand-in looks like
  1. The workflow, scoped. One paths: list, drafts excluded, contents: read, and a tool list that contains no write tool. If your workflow can push a commit, step 6 cannot save you.
  2. The rules file, earned. Three rules each traceable to a real comment, plus a "do not flag" section written from the checks your CI already runs. Say which rule you would move to a scanner and why.
  3. The comment table. One row per comment: pull request, file, line, your verdict, and a sentence for the ignored ones saying why nobody acted. That last column is the part that changes your configuration.
  4. The two ratios. Precision is correct over all; acceptance is acted-on over all; acceptance is always the smaller. Report both as fractions with the raw counts beside them.
  5. The recall check, hedged correctly. "Two of five, and five is a sample rather than a measurement" is the whole sentence. Add which of the three finding parts each miss was missing.
  6. The ruleset. A screenshot or an export showing verify required and the review job absent from the required list, plus one line saying why, so the next administrator does not "fix" it.
  7. The decision. One paragraph, your numbers, and a named scope. "Keep it on src/auth/**" is a decision. "It seems useful" is not.

Self-check. You have done this correctly if your measured precision differs from every published figure and you can say why, and if your ruleset makes the AI review advisory rather than blocking. If your numbers match a vendor's exactly, you have almost certainly copied their classification rules rather than applied your own judgement.

Your teacher. Point DeepWiki at a public repository you know well and read the pages it generates for the parts you understand best. Where it is wrong is where a reviewer relying on that index would also be wrong, and that comparison is the cheapest evaluation of a repo-aware architecture you will ever run.

Primary source to go deeper

The most complete public specification of a production AI review product, and the source of nearly everything on this page: Devin Review. Read the instruction-file section first, then the finding classes, then ask yourself which parts of it you would want in a reviewer you built.
