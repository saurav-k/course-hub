Module 04 · Customizing your agent · Lesson 0470

The team that ships the agent publishes what it learned, and it is nearly all one constraint

working 9 min Built from Anthropic's own public documentation, engineering posts and blog, and from nothing else. · Claude Code best practices

The one-minute version

  • The best-practices page names its own premise in the second sentence: "Most best practices are based on one constraint: Claude's context window fills up fast, and performance degrades as it fills."
  • Everything this module built - the file, the budget, the hierarchy, the hooks, the subagents - is downstream of that sentence.
  • Five named failure patterns are published with their fixes, and four of the five are ways a window fills with the wrong thing.
  • The vendor's own numbers split cleanly into measured on a named system and self-reported with no method, and telling them apart is the skill this page is really teaching.

1. One constraint, and everything else is downstream

Read the best-practices page's opening as an admission and the whole document changes shape. It says its recommendations rest on a single fact: the window fills, and performance degrades as it does. That is why the instruction file is a budget, why skills load on demand, why subagents are worth fifteen times the tokens, and why /clear between unrelated tasks is offered as a serious technique rather than as housekeeping.

Two of this module's pages are that constraint applied directly, and this page does not re-argue either: lesson 0410 is the file as a budget, and lesson 0460 is what a second context costs. What is here instead is the part of the published practice that has no home elsewhere in the module.

2. The five named failure patterns

The page lists five, each with a one-line fix, and naming them is most of the value: three of them are things every reader has done this week without having a word for it.

PatternWhat it looks likeThe published fix
The kitchen sink sessionOne task, then something unrelated, then back again/clear between unrelated tasks
Correcting over and overTwo corrections in and it is still wrongAfter two failures, clear and write a better initial prompt
The over-specified fileRules get lost in the noise of other rulesPrune, or convert the rule to a hook
The trust-then-verify gapA plausible implementation that misses the edge casesAlways provide verification, and do not ship what you cannot verify
The infinite exploration"Investigate this" with no scope, and hundreds of files readScope it narrowly, or delegate it to a subagent

Two of those fixes are this module's artefacts wearing the vendor's words. "Convert the rule to a hook" is lesson 0430, and "delegate it to a subagent so the exploration does not consume your main context" is lesson 0460. The other three are habits rather than files, which is why they need naming rather than building.

3. Two sessions, and why the second one is worth its cost

Alongside the subagent pattern, the same page publishes a plainer one that needs no configuration at all: run two sessions, one writing and one reviewing. Its stated reason is the same isolation argument from lesson 0460, in one line: "A fresh context improves code review since Claude won't be biased toward code it just wrote."

4. Reading the vendor's own numbers

Anthropic publishes a blog post describing how eight of its own teams use Claude Code - infrastructure data scientists, product engineering, product design, security engineering, inference, data infrastructure, growth marketing and legal - and the recurring shape across them is the one this module has been building towards: "They give Claude abstract problems, let it work autonomously, then review solutions before final refinements." Autonomy with a human checkpoint, which is exactly what a Stop hook and a reviewer are for.

The same post carries numbers, and they are a different kind of thing from the numbers in the engineering posts. Sorting them is the useful exercise.

The three on the right are worth naming so you recognise them in the wild: "Problems that typically take 10-15 minutes of manual scanning now resolve 3x as quickly", "saving them 20 minutes of valuable time during a system outage", and "an 80% reduction in research time". Each is a real report of a team's own experience and none is a measurement anybody outside the company can check. Quote them as what a team said about its own work. What would move them into the left-hand bar is small and specific: the baseline task set, how many people, and how the before and after were timed.

5. What actually transfers

Strip out the product and one recommendation survives every source on this page, and it is the one that would still be true if a different company shipped the agent: give it something it can run, then read the evidence rather than the claim. The best-practices page puts it as showing evidence rather than asserting success - the test output, the command and its result, a screenshot - because reviewing evidence is faster than re-running the verification yourself, "and it works for sessions you weren't watching."

That sentence is where module 4 hands over to the rest of the course. For how this particular harness is built underneath the configuration, rather than how it is configured, see Coding Harness Engineering, lesson 0130, which reads it as one architecture among fifteen.

One thing about these sources is worth knowing before you cite them. The material this page calls the best-practices page was published as an engineering post and is now a documentation page at a different address, and a documentation page changes without a changelog. Cite it by its current URL and by the section name rather than as a dated artefact, and re-read the section before you quote it in something of your own.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Which of the five published failure patterns is not a context problem?
The trust-then-verify gap is about the absence of a check rather than about a full window, and its fix is verification rather than clearing or pruning. The other three each describe a window filling with something that should not be in it - unrelated work, unscoped file reads, and rules that drown one another - and all three fixes are about what is in the window.
A colleague cites "an 80% reduction in research time" from Anthropic's blog. What is the honest reply?
The claim is real and is precisely unverifiable from outside: no baseline, no sample size and no timing method are published, so it is a report rather than a measurement. The first option overclaims in the other direction and is not a reading of the evidence. The second treats publication as method. The third is a good idea and is not what the question asked - reproducing it would produce your own number, which is a different claim from theirs.

Practice about 30 minutes, and it closes the module

Practice 1working

The module assignment. Make one rule in your repository true whatever the model decides, and prove it twice.

  1. Open your instruction file, or write one from lesson 0410's builder. Count its lines.
  2. Take the three rules you care about most and classify each as a fact, a procedure, a rule for one subtree, or a rule that must hold.
  3. Move at least one procedure to .claude/skills/ and at least one subtree rule to .claude/rules/ with a paths: list. Count the lines again and record the delta.
  4. Take one rule that must hold and write it as a hook: PreToolUse to stop an action, Stop to stop the turn.
  5. chmod +x the script. Confirm it is registered with /hooks, then trigger it deliberately and keep the transcript.
  6. Break the condition on purpose and keep the transcript that shows the agent being stopped with your own reason text visible.
  7. Add the matching permissions.deny or permissions.ask entry to .claude/settings.json, so the rule holds through two independent mechanisms.
  8. Write one subagent - planner, implementer or reviewer - and run it once against a real diff.

Hand in the before and after line counts, the skill and rule you extracted, the hook script and its settings block, both transcripts, and the subagent file with its output on a real diff.

Hint

Do step 7 even though step 4 already works, because it is the step that makes the point. A hook is a program that can crash, and a deny rule is enforced by the client whatever your script does. Two mechanisms for one rule is not belt and braces here, it is the difference between a gate and a gate that also holds when you break your own script.

Show the self-check for a finished submission
  1. /context lists your instruction file under Memory files. If it does not, nothing else in this list matters - see lesson 0400.
  2. /hooks lists your hook, under the event and matcher you intended. A hook that is not in that list is not installed.
  3. The deliberate failure is blocked, and your own reason text appears word for word in the transcript. Somebody else's block is not yours.
  4. The same action is still refused with the hook disabled, because the permission rule catches it. Two mechanisms, one rule, and this is the step that proves it.
  5. The line count fell and no content was lost, because it moved rather than being deleted.

Sanity check. Five artefacts on disk - an instruction file, a skill, a path-scoped rule, a hook script and a settings file - plus one subagent, and two transcripts that show the same rule holding by two different routes. That is the module's whole output, and it is the surface the capstone page for this module starts from once it is written.

Your teacher. Give your agent the five failure patterns and the last hour of your own session, and ask which of the five it thinks you were in and what evidence it is reading. Then ask it for the one change to your repository that would have made that pattern harder to fall into - the answer should be a file, not a habit.
