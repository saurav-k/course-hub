Module 10 · The software factory · Lesson 1060

Four habits, one repository, and a factory you run once end to end

frontier 14 min What the evidence says a person actually does with an agent, and the assignment that closes the capstone. · A study of 400,000 sessions

The one-minute version

  • Ten modules reduce to four habits: make the repository readable, make the gate explicit, make the correction durable, make the cost visible. Each is a file, and you can add all four this week.
  • Across roughly 400,000 sessions, people made about 70% of the planning decisions and about 20% of the execution decisions. The judgement stayed and the typing left.
  • Expertise shows up as a measurable gap: novices abandon 19% of troubled sessions against 5-7% for everyone else, and verify success far less often.
  • The assignment is one full turn of your own factory, and the deliverable is a document called docs/factory.md that says where your gate is and why.

1. Four habits, and the module each one came from

Everything in ten modules is one of four moves. Each is small, each is a file, and each has a module behind it if you need the argument again.

The habitWhat it is, concretelyTaught inThe artefact
Make it readableone command sets up, one runs the tests, one proves the changemodules 5 and 6a readiness score, taken twice

The order is not arbitrary. A repository nothing can read is a repository where every later habit is guesswork; a gate that is a norm collapses the first time something other than a person opens a pull request; and a cost you cannot see is a cost that gets discovered by a finance team rather than by you.

2. What a person actually does with an agent, measured

A study of roughly 400,000 interactive sessions from about 235,000 people gives the clearest published picture of how the work divides, and it is not the picture either the enthusiasts or the sceptics describe.

Over the seven months the study covers, the mix of what people did with the agent moved as well, and it moved away from the thing everybody expected it to be for.

3. What experience actually buys

The same study separates sessions by how the person drove them, and the difference is not subtle. An expert session runs action chains more than twice as long - about twelve agent actions against five - carrying five times the output, around 3,200 words against 600.

Two numbers matter more than those, because they are about outcomes rather than volume.

Read those bars as a correlation and nothing more. The study observes sessions; it does not assign people to conditions, so it cannot separate "experience causes better outcomes" from "people working on tractable problems look experienced". The useful reading is narrower and safe: experienced sessions do not abandon. Whatever produced that, the behaviour it describes - staying with a troubled session and finishing it - is the one worth copying.

4. Three things that would change what this course says

A course that could not be wrong is not teaching anything checkable. These are the three claims this module rests on, and the evidence that would overturn each.

  • The gate belongs at merge. Overturned by a study showing that teams who moved the gate downstream - to a deploy check or a canary - shipped more without more incidents. Nobody has published one, and it is a measurement somebody could actually run.
  • Delivery has not kept up with writing. Overturned by a replication of the release measurement, on a different population, finding the attenuation gone. The published elasticity of 0.25 is the number to watch.
  • Prompting is not the lever, the repository is. Overturned by a controlled comparison in which prompt technique beat repository readiness on the same tasks and the same codebase. The pieces to run it exist.

5. Write the one document that survives the tools

The assignment ends in a file, and the file is the point: docs/factory.md, describing your own loop, is the thing you will hand to the next person and the thing that will still be true after you have changed every product named in this course.

The reframing You came in believing the lever was the sentence you typed. It was never the sentence. It is a file the agent reads, a rule that refuses, a permission that bounds, a check that must pass, and a log that says what happened - and every one of them is something you commit.

6. The exit is somebody else's repository

The capstone's last page is where this module's assignment lands, and it ends somewhere deliberately uncomfortable: your unattended pipeline runs against a repository that is not yours.

That is where module 7 stops being theoretical, because now the untrusted text is somebody else's issue tracker and the permissions are somebody else's project's. It is also where a rule you have not met yet applies: many open-source projects now publish a position on AI-assisted contributions, and those positions differ from one another. Read the project's policy before you open the pull request, and honour the one it actually has rather than the one you assume it has.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Across about 400,000 sessions, which decisions did people mostly keep?
The measured split is about seventy percent of planning decisions and about twenty percent of execution decisions, in the same sessions. The second option reverses the two, which would describe a person typing while an agent decides what to build. The third and fourth both describe a division nobody measured, and the third would leave the agent making almost nothing.
Which is the safest reading of the abandonment gap between novice and experienced sessions?
The study observes sessions rather than assigning people to conditions, so the causal reading in the first option is not available from it. The second is a possible confound and the data does not establish it either. The fourth is contradicted by the size of the gap - nineteen percent against five to seven. What the numbers do describe is a behaviour, and behaviour is the part you can copy.

Practice the module assignment, about three hours across a few days

Assignmentfrontier

Goal. Run one full turn of the factory on your own repository, with the gate where you put it and the evidence that it held.

  1. Add two workflows. One that answers a mention, one that reviews every non-draft pull request. Commit them.
  2. Write .agents/policy.yaml for exactly two agents: a reviewer that cannot write, and a fixer that can push only to one branch prefix. Authenticate at least one of them with no long-lived secret.
  3. Open a real issue in your repository - small, genuine, and something you would otherwise have fixed yourself. Let the fixer produce the pull request. Do not fix it yourself.
  4. Let the reviewer review it. Read both the review and the diff. Then merge it yourself, or reject it and write down why.
  5. Capture the run. Turn on telemetry and record three numbers: the tool decisions, the cost, and the wall-clock time from issue to merge.
  6. Break it on purpose. Revoke the fixer's credential and confirm the reviewer still runs. Then flip one kill switch and confirm both stop.
  7. Write docs/factory.md using the four sections above: your loop, where your gate is and why, the three numbers from step 5, and the one thing that surprised you in step 6.

What you hand in: the two workflows, the policy file, the merged or rejected pull request, the captured telemetry, and docs/factory.md.

Hint

Do step 6 before you write step 7, and do not plan what step 6 will show. The surprise is the deliverable; a factory document written from what you expected to happen is the failure mode this assignment exists to prevent.

Show how to check your own work
  1. Step 6 is the whole check, and it is self-marking. If revoking one credential stops both agents, the two identities were never separate and every claim in your policy file about the reviewer is really a claim about the fixer.
  2. If a kill switch leaves something running, your switch list is wrong. That is a finding rather than a failure, and correcting the list is a better outcome than having guessed right.
  3. Compare the wall-clock split from step 5 against the machine minutes. The gap is your gate, measured, and it is the single number that tells you whether the factory is model-limited or people-limited.
  4. Then compare against your baseline from lesson 0000 - the reads before the first write, whether anything stopped and asked, and whether the agent could tell it had finished correctly. All three should have moved, and the third is the one that moves most.

Sanity check. A completed assignment has at least one thing in it that you did not expect, and it is almost always in step 6. If every step went exactly as planned, check whether step 3 used a genuinely real issue - a manufactured one lets the whole pipeline succeed and teaches you nothing about any of it.

Your teacher. When docs/factory.md is written, hand it to your agent with one instruction: find every sentence that describes an intention rather than a control, and for each one name the file or setting that would have to exist for it to be true. What comes back is your next week of work, and it will be shorter than you expect.
