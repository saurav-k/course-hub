Module 09 · Building an AI-native team · Lesson 0960

Everything you can measure is activity, and activity is not the thing

frontier 10 min Built on the published analytics and telemetry references, and on three independent measurements of adoption. · NBER working paper 35275

The one-minute version

  • Six metric sources exist and every one of them measures activity: sessions, accepted lines, spend, pull requests carrying assisted code.
  • Attribution is deliberately conservative. Code a developer rewrote by more than 20% is not attributed, so the dashboard is an underestimate by design.
  • Adoption is not in doubt: 90% of DORA's respondents use AI at work and 84% of Stack Overflow's use or plan to use it.
  • Trust is: 30% report little or no trust in AI-generated code, and 46% actively distrust its accuracy.
  • The measurement that breaks the story: commits rose 180% under autonomous agents, projects 50%, releases 30%. Instrument the release step.

1. Six sources, one kind of number

Start with what is actually available, because the argument is usually lost by reaching for a number nobody has.

Read the middle column again. Every row is a count of something happening, and the last row is the only one measuring an outcome, which is also the only row nothing in the tooling fills in for you. That asymmetry is the subject of this page.

2. Attribution is conservative on purpose

Before quoting a contribution figure, know how it was produced, because the method decides which direction it is wrong in. When a pull request merges, added lines are extracted from the diff and matched against agent sessions that edited the same files inside a window running 21 days before to 2 days after the merge. Lines are normalised first: whitespace trimmed, runs of spaces collapsed, quotes standardised, text lowercased. Only "effective lines" count, meaning more than three characters after normalisation, with empty lines and bracket-only lines excluded. Lock files, generated code, build directories, test fixtures and lines over 1,000 characters are dropped. And code a developer substantially rewrote, with more than a 20% difference, is not attributed at all.

The documentation states the consequence itself: these metrics "are deliberately conservative and represent an underestimate", because only lines and pull requests with high confidence are counted. That is the right bias for a dashboard and it makes one common argument invalid. If somebody says the number is inflated, the method says otherwise. If somebody says the number proves the tool is working, the method says only that the tool was present.

The 20% rewrite rule has a perverse reading that is worth naming out loud. The code an agent drafted and a human then improved substantially - which is arguably the best outcome in the whole pipeline - is the code the dashboard does not attribute. So the metric is lowest exactly where the collaboration worked best, and highest where the human accepted the draft as written. Never use assisted-line share as a quality signal in either direction.

3. Streaming it yourself, and the cardinality bill

The only source that works on every deployment is OpenTelemetry, and it is the one to reach for if your fleet runs through a cloud provider, since that usage never reaches the vendor dashboards at all. Eight metrics are exported: claude_code.session.count, claude_code.lines_of_code.count, claude_code.pull_request.count, claude_code.commit.count, claude_code.cost.usage in USD, claude_code.token.usage in tokens, claude_code.code_edit_tool.decision, and claude_code.active_time.total in seconds.

Every one carries the same standard attributes, and two of them decide what your observability bill looks like. session.id is included by default and is unbounded: one new series per session, for ever. user.email and user.id are always included when available, which is what makes per-developer attribution possible and also what makes the data personal. The switches are documented, and OTEL_METRICS_INCLUDE_SESSION_ID=false is the first one to consider if your metrics store charges by series.

4. Three measurements, honestly labelled

Adoption itself is settled. DORA's 2025 report, drawn from "survey responses from nearly 5,000 technology professionals from around the world", finds that "90% of survey respondents report using AI at work" and that "more than 80% believe it has increased their productivity". Stack Overflow's 2025 survey puts 84% using or planning to use AI tools, up from 76% the year before.

Trust is not settled, and it moved the wrong way. DORA records that "30% report little or no trust in the code generated by AI". Stack Overflow finds 46% actively distrusting accuracy against 33% who trust it and 3% who highly trust it, with positive sentiment falling from over 70% in 2023 and 2024 to 60%. Its top frustration, at 66%, is "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite".

Vendor evidence about a vendor's own engineering is real and citable, and it is not an industry benchmark. Anthropic reports that Claude authors approximately 80% of the code merged into its codebase, and that the share of pull requests receiving substantive review comments grew from 16% to 54%. Both are self-reported, about a company whose product this is, with no independent replication. Quote them as what one organisation says about itself and never as what your organisation should expect.

5. The number nobody can move

The strongest measurement in this area is also the least comfortable. An NBER working paper by Demirer, Musolff and Yang, drawn from more than 100,000 GitHub developers combined with their AI usage telemetry, measures the effect at each level of the production hierarchy. Commits rise by 40% under autocomplete, 140% under interactive coding agents and 180% under autonomous ones. New projects rise by 50%. Releases rise by 30%. The estimated elasticity of substitution between AI and human effort is 0.25, which the authors read as strong complementarity rather than replacement.

DORA's own reading of its survey points the same way from a different direction: "AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there", and acceleration "can expose weaknesses downstream" without strong automated testing, mature version control and fast feedback loops. Two independent methods, one conclusion: the constraint moved downstream and nobody's dashboard is pointed at it.

So the metric contract worth writing for your own team is short. Three numbers, one definition each, and a named reader. One activity number, because you need to know the tool is being used at all. One cost number, because somebody will ask. And one delivery number you were already measuring before any of this - lead time to release, or change failure rate - reported unchanged in definition, so that the comparison across the rollout is honest.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

A developer heavily rewrites code an agent drafted, then merges it. How is it attributed?
Code substantially rewritten, with more than a 20% difference, is not attributed at all. The second option ignores the matching step entirely: touching a file is not enough. The third describes a proportional scheme that would be reasonable and is not what the documented method does. The fourth invents a session boundary the 21-day window does not have.
Commits rose 180% and releases rose 30%. What does the 0.25 elasticity add to that?
An elasticity of substitution well below one means the two inputs are complements: adding more of one does not replace the other, so the human-gated stages remain binding. The first option is a forecast the paper does not make. The second misreads an elasticity as a confidence interval. The fourth invents a time-allocation finding from a substitution parameter.

Practice about 25 minutes, with your own delivery data

Practice 1frontier

Write the metric contract before anybody asks you for a number.

Choose three numbers for your team: one activity number, one cost number, and one delivery number you were already measuring. Write one sentence each defining exactly what is counted, including the window and what is excluded.

Then find the honest caveat. For each number, write the sentence a sceptical colleague would say to dismiss it, and decide whether they would be right. Keep the ones where they would not be.

Hint

The delivery number is the hard one, and it is hard for the right reason: it has to be defined the same way before and after the rollout. If you find yourself changing its definition to make it move, you have found the reason this exercise exists.

Show a worked contract
  1. Activity. "Distinct people with at least one agent session on a working day, from claude_code.session.count, excluding CI service accounts." The sceptic says it counts anyone who opened a terminal, and they are right, which is why it is the number you watch for a dip rather than quote for value.
  2. Cost. "Model spend per active developer per month, from the gateway, at contracted rates." The sceptic says it ignores seat licences and the gateway's own hosting, and they are right, so the definition names what it excludes.
  3. Delivery. "Median hours from first commit on a branch to that change being released, unchanged in definition since before the rollout." The sceptic says the mix of work changed, and they may be right, so report the distribution rather than the median alone.
  4. Name a reader for each. A number nobody reads on a schedule is a number nobody will notice going wrong.

Sanity check. If all three of your numbers would move together in the same direction on a good week, at least two of them are measuring the same thing. A real contract has one number that can rise while another falls, because that disagreement is where the information is.

Your teacher. If the attribution method still feels like a detail, ask your agent to work out which of your own recent pull requests would and would not have been attributed, and why. If the NBER cascade feels like an argument against agents, ask it to state the strongest reading of the paper that is still consistent with a 180% commit effect.

Primary source to go deeper

The paper that makes this page uncomfortable, and the only one here with a hundred thousand developers behind it: Demirer, Musolff and Yang, NBER working paper 35275. Read the analytics reference beside it for the attribution method, which is the part that decides what your own dashboard is entitled to claim.
