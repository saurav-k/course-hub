Acceptance rate is the only number that survives contact with your own codebase

working 9 min Four published production results, read for method as well as for magnitude. · MetaMateCR

The one-minute version

  • Precision hides recall. Recall needs a golden set you are never going to build. Acceptance rate - the share of comments the author acted on - you can compute from week one.
  • The published production figures agree far more than the marketing does: roughly 20% to 45% of comments get acted on, across four independent deployments.
  • That is much lower than any precision headline, and the gap between "the comment was correct" and "somebody did something about it" is the whole subject of this page.
  • The two things that moved these numbers in production were discoverability and suppression, not a better model. Meta even found that showing patches to reviewers made reviews over 5% slower.

1. Three metrics, and what each one hides

MetricWhat it measuresWhat it hidesCan you compute it
Precisionthe share of comments that were righteverything it never mentionedyes, by classifying comments
Recallthe share of real bugs it foundhow much noise it made finding themno - needs a golden set
Acceptance ratethe share of comments acted oncorrect comments nobody had time foryes, from the first week
Comments per pull requestvolumeeverything that mattersyes, and it is a trap

Acceptance rate wins on availability rather than on merit. It is a blunter instrument than precision - a correct comment that the author was too busy to act on counts as a miss - but it is the only one of the three you can compute on your repository without building the thing you do not have time to build.

2. What the field actually publishes

Four production deployments have published a number of this shape, and the interesting thing is that they agree.

Set that beside the precision chart in 0610, where the numbers ran to 96.8%. There is no contradiction. Precision asks whether the comment was correct; acceptance asks whether anybody did anything. The distance between them is the cost of being right in a way nobody has time for.

The reframing Every improvement in these deployments that can be attributed came from suppression or from placement. Google's usefulness ratio went from about 54% to 66% by suppressing outdated practices, and reached its 80% target by improving summaries. Neither was a model change.

3. Read every published figure as a question about method

Greptile publishes acceptance rising from 30% to 43% between two versions, along with addressed comments per pull request going from 0.92 to 1.60. Its own post states how "addressed" was determined: "'Addressed' is determined by an LLM". That is not a disqualification - it is what everybody does, because string matching cannot tell whether a change resolved a comment - but it means the number is an estimate produced by the same class of system being measured.

MARKETING, and the absence is the finding. Every vendor number in this module is precision-flavoured or acceptance-flavoured. Not one vendor publishes a false-negative rate for its own product, and the only recall figures in this module come from a benchmark where each vendor's tool was run by somebody else. When you are shown a review benchmark, ask for the denominator, the judge and the miss rate. If the miss rate is not there, you have been shown the half of the trade the seller likes.
A decision worth copying. Meta ran a safety trial and found "reviewers taking over 5% longer to conduct reviews" when AI patches were shown to both reviewers and authors. The regression disappeared once the patches were shown only to authors. That is the same ordering 0600's sequence figure draws, and it was found by measuring rather than by reasoning.

4. The measurement you can actually run

It costs one afternoon and it settles the argument for your team. Take fifty pull requests, take every comment your reviewer emitted, and classify each one by hand into three buckets. There is no fourth bucket and no partial credit.

5. The trap, and it is the easiest metric to collect

Comments per pull request is the number that appears in every dashboard because it needs no judgement at all. It is also the one metric that gets worse when you optimise it: a reviewer told to comment more will comment more, and every extra comment is another thing your team reads. The reviewer with the highest volume in 0600's chart was also the one with the second-lowest precision.

Track it, because a sudden change in volume is a real signal that something moved. Never target it. And when you decide whether to keep the reviewer, cite your own acceptance rate rather than anybody's benchmark - a number from your codebase, with a method you can describe, beats a number from a vendor's fifty pull requests every time. The Staff AI Engineer, lesson 0004 is the hub's argument for why most reported improvements sit inside the noise, and it applies here whole.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Why can you compute acceptance rate but not recall on your own repository?
Recall divides by the bugs actually present, so it needs a golden set somebody built by hand - which is exactly what the benchmark in 0620 spent its effort on. The first confuses size with availability. The second describes how matching is done once you have a golden set, not why you lack one. The fourth is the trap: a vendor dashboard reports its own view of acceptance, and the point of this page is to compute yours.
Google doubled the share of comments addressed. What changed?
The preview rate moved from about 20% in beta to about 40% at launch, and the fraction of comments addressed rose by a factor of two - a placement change, not a capability one. The second is the assumption this page exists to break. The third inverts a real number: the model was calibrated for a target precision of 50%. The fourth is Meta's trial, and it made reviews slower rather than better.

Practice about 90 minutes, with fifty pull requests

Practice 1working

Compute your own acceptance rate, and write the sentence that decides whether the reviewer stays.

Export every comment your reviewer left on the last fifty pull requests, with the pull-request number, the file and the line. gh api will give you the review comments per pull request.

Classify each into the three buckets from the flowchart: wrong, correct and acted on, correct and ignored. Then compute precision and acceptance from the same table, and read the third bucket properly.

Hint

"Acted on" means a subsequent commit changed the code at or near that line in a way that answers the comment. A thumbs-up reaction is not acting on it, and neither is a reply agreeing to fix it later - both of those belong in the ignored bucket, which is the bucket you want to be honest about.

Show the arithmetic and the sentence it produces
  1. Count the three buckets. Say 200 comments total: 74 wrong, 61 correct and acted on, 65 correct and ignored.
  2. Precision. Correct over all: (61 + 65) ÷ 200 = 63%. That is squarely inside the published range from 0610, which is a sign your sample is not weird.
  3. Acceptance. Acted on over all: 61 ÷ 200 = 30.5%. Also inside the published range, and roughly half your precision figure - which is the gap this page is about.
  4. Read the ignored bucket. Sixty-five correct comments nobody acted on is a bigger finding than either ratio. Cluster them: if a third are one rule, delete that rule rather than tuning the reviewer.
  5. Write the sentence. "On our repository, 30% of this reviewer's comments changed the code, at a cost of X per month and about Y minutes of reading a week. We are keeping it, scoped to these paths." Cite your numbers, not the vendor's.

Sanity check. Acceptance must be lower than precision, always, because acting on a comment requires it to be correct first. If yours comes out higher, you have counted a comment as acted on that you did not also count as correct, and the classification needs redoing.

Your teacher. Hand your agent the exported comment table and ask it to do a first pass at the three buckets by looking at whether a later commit touched the same lines, then check fifty of its judgements yourself. The disagreements are instructive: they are exactly the cases where an LLM-as-judge acceptance figure - the kind vendors publish - would have been wrong.
