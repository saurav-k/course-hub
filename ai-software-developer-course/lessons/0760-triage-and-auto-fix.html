The model's job is the verdict, not the discovery

working 9 min Built on two vendors' published documentation for model-assisted triage and fixing. · Responsible use of Copilot Autofix

The one-minute version

  • Detection is solved enough. Triage is the bottleneck, and it is where the hours go.
  • Autotriage hands the model the finding, the code and your own past decisions, and asks for ignore or act.
  • Autofix hands it the alert's dataflow, the code around both ends, and the query's help text, and asks for a diff.
  • Both are model calls, so the same alert can produce a different suggestion, or none - the vendor says so in its own documentation.
  • That non-determinism is exactly why the human merge gate stays, and why the last step of the loop is your own rule re-run against the fix.

1. Where the hours actually go

The economics are the argument for this page, so start with them rather than with a product. Scanning is cheap and getting cheaper: a diff-aware run costs a couple of minutes of a machine's time. Deciding what a finding means costs a person who understands the codebase, and that person is the scarce resource.

Put your own numbers in. The arithmetic is linear and unromantic.

The third slider is the honest one, and it is a slider rather than a number because nobody has published what it is for your repository. The rest of this page is about what a vendor does publish, what it does not, and what that means for where you put the human.

2. Autotriage: the model reads the finding and your history

Model-assisted triage hands the model three things - the finding, the surrounding code, and the team's own record of what it has ignored before - and asks for a recommendation. Semgrep's assistant names the pieces distinctly, and the distinction matters when you are deciding what to trust: autotriage proposes that a finding can safely be ignored, remediation guidance writes prose about how to fix it, suggested fix explains a fix without a diff, and autofix generates a code change and opens the pull request. Only the last of those changes a file.

Memories are the mechanism that stops the loop repeating itself: a team writes custom instructions once, and the next finding of that class is judged against them instead of against the vendor's defaults. That is the same move as writing a rule in lesson 0710, one layer up - the standard goes into the repository rather than into a reviewer's memory.

Marketing, not evidence Semgrep publishes one number for that step: "Multimodal is over 95% accurate in categorizing Semgrep Code findings as false positives." No method, sample, or evaluation set appears on the page. It may well be true, and it is not evidence about your repository: an accuracy figure without a corpus cannot be compared with anything, cannot be reproduced, and cannot fail. What would make it evidence: the corpus, how ground truth was decided, the language mix, and the false-negative rate - because a triage step that is 95% right about which findings to close is a different thing depending on which 5% it is wrong about.

3. Autofix: what is actually in the prompt

GitHub documents the inputs its auto-fix assembles, which is unusually useful because it lets you predict where the mechanism will be weak. For a code-scanning alert it collects the alert data in SARIF format, code around each source location and sink location, the first ten or so lines of every file involved, and the help text of the CodeQL query that produced the alert.

Then read the sentence that follows those inputs, because it is the most useful thing on the page: "Even with the same alert and code, it might fail to produce a viable suggestion, or the suggestion might vary across attempts." The published limitations are equally direct: suggestions may be syntactically invalid, land in the wrong place, change the program's meaning, fix nothing, or introduce a new vulnerability.

4. What is published, and what is not

Both vendors publish something about quality, and neither publishes the thing you would need to plan with. That gap is worth drawing, because it is the difference between "this is useful" - which it is - and "this can be trusted unsupervised", which nobody has shown.

5. The loop, and where the human stands

Put the pieces in order and the design question answers itself. Two of the steps are model calls and neither is repeatable, so the gate goes after both, and the last step is not a model at all - it is your own rule from lesson 0710, re-run against the fix.

The habit worth taking Never accept a security fix without re-running the check that found it. A model-authored diff that silences a scanner by moving the sink out of the rule's reach looks exactly like a fix, passes review by a tired human, and leaves the vulnerability where it was. The strongest version of this habit is a test that would have caught the original defect, which is the same instinct the harness world applies to permissions: prove the boundary, do not assert it.

Why does a vendor's published "over 95% accurate" figure not tell you what your team's review load will be?
A percentage with no corpus, no ground-truth procedure and no language mix cannot be compared with anything, including your repository - which is exactly why it belongs in a warning rather than in a plan. The first option asserts something nobody has measured either way. The second invents a scope limit the page does not state. The third confuses two products: the figure is about categorising findings as false positives, not about fixes.
An auto-fix suggestion arrives, looks reasonable, and the scanner goes green. What is the minimum you owe the change?
Green proves the rule no longer matches, which a fix and an evasion both achieve, so you need a person asking whether the behaviour survived and the deterministic check re-run against the result. The first option treats a scanner as an oracle. The second is the trap this page is built on: the call is documented as non-repeatable, so agreement between two runs is evidence of nothing. The fourth is good provenance hygiene and no substitute for reading the diff.

Practice about 25 minutes, with your own findings

Practice 1working

Measure the middle slider on your own repository, because it is the only number on this page you can actually establish.

Take ten real findings from the gate you built in lesson 0750. Before looking at any tool's opinion, write your own verdict on each - act, ignore, or cannot tell without more context - and time yourself.

Then compare against whatever assistant you have available, and record two counts: how many it agreed with, and how many of its disagreements were in the direction of closing a finding you would have acted on.

Hint

The third verdict is the important one. Count how many findings you could not decide without opening two more files - that number is the real cost of triage, and it is what a model with a four-item prompt is also missing.

Show what the ten usually teach
  1. Your own time per finding is higher than you guessed, and it is dominated by two or three that needed real reading. The median is not the cost; the tail is.
  2. Agreement is high on the boring ones. A test fixture, an unreachable advisory and a duplicate are exactly where an assistant does well, and those are also the ones that cost you the least time - so the agreement rate overstates the value.
  3. The disagreements that matter are one-directional. A finding wrongly left open costs a minute of your attention. A finding wrongly closed costs whatever the vulnerability costs, and nobody reviews a closed finding.
  4. Write the asymmetry down as a policy. Let the assistant close nothing on its own in the code paths you care most about, and let it close freely in test fixtures. That policy is a memory or a rule, not a preference.

Sanity check. Ten findings should take you between fifteen and forty-five minutes. Much faster and you were pattern-matching on the rule name rather than reading the code, which is the same shortcut you are about to criticise a model for taking.

Your teacher. Give your agent one finding and ask it for a fix, twice, in two fresh sessions. Compare the diffs. Then ask it to write the test that would fail before the fix and pass after - the second request is the one that turns a suggestion into something you can merge.
