Score it twice, because a score used once is an opinion

frontier 8 min The third of four capstone stops. It spends modules 5 and 6 on the contract you committed at stop two. · Agent Readiness

The one-minute version

  • You have just finished module 6, so you can score a repository and you know what an AI reviewer can and cannot reach. This stop spends both on your own repository.
  • The artefact is a before and an after. One score is an opinion about a repository; two scores with a diff between them is a measurement of a change.
  • Choose what to close by hours, not by importance. The loop only turns if the first pass through it takes an afternoon.
  • Put a reviewer on your own pull requests, advisory rather than blocking, and measure it on comments you classified yourself. Every published precision figure was measured on somebody else's code.

1. The artefact is the difference, not the number

Score your capstone repository against the ten dimensions under the evidence rule: every point is a file path or a command you ran and watched, and a dimension where you cannot name the evidence is a zero even when you are certain the thing is fine.

Then take every dimension below 2 and rank them by hours to close rather than by importance. This is the step people skip and it is why readiness work stalls: the most important dimension is usually observability, which is a quarter of a platform team's year, while the cheapest is usually one paragraph in one file. Close the cheapest one, re-score that dimension with new evidence, and record both.

The published model this course's rubric derives from is explicit about the same idea at a coarser grain. Factory's five readiness levels - Functional, Documented, Standardized, Optimized and Autonomous - are gated: "To unlock a level, you must pass 80% of the criteria from the previous level." A level is not something you argue your way into, and neither is a dimension.

2. Put a reviewer on your own pull requests

Module 6's argument is that what a reviewer may look at is the ceiling and the prompt is only the ladder, so the setup that matters is the trigger, the scope and the authority rather than the wording. The documented review workflow makes all three visible in about twenty lines.

.github/workflows/claude-code-review.yml · code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions · Anthropic documentation · reproduced verbatim with attribution
name: Code Review
on:
  pull_request:
    types: [opened, synchronize, ready_for_review, reopened]
jobs:
  review:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    permissions:
      contents: read
      pull-requests: read
      issues: read
      id-token: write
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v6
        with:
          fetch-depth: 1
      - uses: anthropics/claude-code-action@v1
        with:
          anthropic_api_key: ${{ secrets.ANTHROPIC_API_KEY }}
          plugin_marketplaces: "https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code.git"
          plugins: "code-review@claude-code-plugins"
          prompt: "/code-review:code-review --comment ${{ github.repository }}/pull/${{ github.event.pull_request.number }}"
          claude_args: '--allowedTools "mcp__github_inline_comment__create_inline_comment"'

Read the permissions block first, because it is the ceiling: contents: read and pull-requests: read mean this reviewer can look and comment and cannot push. The documentation is also explicit that on a public repository "GitHub withholds secrets from runs triggered by fork pull requests, so the review runs only on pull requests from branches in the same repository", which is a real limit to write into your record rather than to discover during your first outside contribution.

Then constrain it with rules you wrote. Every rule must come from a comment you or a colleague has actually left more than once; a rule invented at the keyboard produces comments nobody asked for, and at least one entry should be a do not flag.

.github/review-rules.md · ours, written to the shape module 6 argues for · hub content, CC BY 4.0 · reproduce freely
# Review rules

Comment only on these. Everything else is out of scope for this reviewer.

## Flag

- A new network call with no timeout. We have shipped this outage twice.
- A migration that is not reversible, unless the pull request body says why.
- A test that asserts on a log line rather than on a return value or a state change.

## Do not flag

- Formatting, naming or import order. `make verify` owns those and it is a required check.
- Missing tests on files under `scripts/`, which are operator tools and are not covered.
- Anything in `vendor/`, which is generated.

3. Measure it on your own comments

Open five pull requests, or replay five recent ones on a branch, and classify every comment the reviewer makes yourself into three piles: a real defect, correct but not worth acting on, and wrong. Precision is the first pile over all three, and it is the only number that survives contact with your own codebase.

Do recall the cheap way and say plainly that it is cheap: take the last five bugs your team actually shipped, reconstruct the diffs that introduced them, and check how many the reviewer catches. Five is not a measurement and you should write that sentence in your own record. The published head-to-head data, which module 6 works through, is what tells you whether your number is surprising.

Then set the gates deliberately. Required checks should include your own verify job and exclude the AI review, so the reviewer is advisory; code-owner review should cover the directory you scoped; and stale approvals should be dismissed on a new push. A reviewer you cannot merge past is a reviewer whose first false positive becomes an outage in your delivery, not in your product.

Agent-readiness and AI-reviewability are different properties, and the score you just took does not measure the second one. In the one open sample module 5 works through, the most agent-ready repository was where AI reviewers did worst on recall, and the least agent-ready one was where several scored their best precision. There is no published study relating a readiness level of any kind to an agent's success rate. So do not report the two together as one direction of travel. Your record should carry them as two independent findings about the same repository, and if closing three readiness gaps left your reviewer's precision exactly where it was, that is the expected result rather than a failure of the exercise.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Three dimensions score below 2 and one of them is observability. Which do you close?
Ranking by cost is what keeps the loop turning, and under an eighty-per-cent gate two cheap criteria are worth more than one expensive one. The first option picks the quarter of platform work and stalls. The second is intuitive and routinely selects a dimension nobody can finish this month. The fourth destroys the before-and-after comparison, which is the only thing that turns the score into a measurement.
Your reviewer leaves ten true comments and your team acts on two of them. What is that?
Precision is real defects over all comments, and a true comment nobody acts on counts against it, which is what makes it the blunt and useful number. The second option confuses recall, which is about bugs the reviewer missed, with volume. The third treats truth as the bar, and it is the failure this metric exists to catch. The fourth is half right - the fix is usually a do not flag rule - but the number moves either way.

Practice about two hours, spread over a week of real pull requests

Practice 1frontier

Produce the before-and-after and the classified comment table for your capstone repository.

Score the ten dimensions under the evidence rule and record the table with a path or a command in every row. Close the cheapest dimension below 2 and re-score that one row with new evidence. Add the review workflow, scoped to one directory, with a rules file of at least three rules and one do not flag.

Hand in, in docs/agent-readiness.md: both scores, the diff that closed the gap, the classified comment table with your precision figure, and one paragraph deciding whether to keep the reviewer, citing your numbers rather than a vendor's.

Hint

Score dimension 2 last and score it by running each command from a clean checkout rather than by reading the file you wrote at stop two. In most repositories at least one command has quietly stopped working since it was written down, and finding that out is worth more than the point it costs.

Show what the two records look like, and the four traps
  1. The readiness diff. One row, twice: 7 | CI legibility | 1 | logs only, no annotations, no summary step becomes 7 | CI legibility | 2 | .github/workflows/ci.yml step "summary" writes check, outcome, fix command. The evidence changed, so the score is allowed to change.
  2. The reviewer decision. "Keep, advisory. Precision 0.42 over 19 comments on 5 pull requests; 6 real defects, 5 correct-but-unwanted, 8 wrong. Two of the five unwanted are now do not flag rules. Recall unmeasured: 2 of the last 5 shipped bugs, which is not a measurement." A paragraph like that is defensible; a paragraph quoting a vendor's figure is not.
  3. Trap one: re-scoring the whole rubric. Only the dimension you changed may move. If three other rows moved too, you scored the first pass carelessly and the diff means nothing.
  4. Trap two: making the review a required check. The first false positive then blocks a release, somebody adds an override, and the override becomes the process.
  5. Trap three: classifying your own reviewer's comments generously. If your precision is above every published figure, re-read the middle pile: "correct but not worth acting on" is the pile people quietly promote.
  6. Trap four: reporting the two findings as one story. The readiness score and the reviewer's precision are independent, and the warning above is the evidence for that.

Sanity check. Your measured precision should differ from every published figure, and you should be able to say why in one sentence - your codebase, your rules, your five pull requests. If it matches a vendor's headline exactly, you have probably classified against their definition rather than counting what your team acted on.

Your teacher. Give your agent the ten dimensions and ask it to score your repository with a path per point, then score it yourself without looking. The rows where you disagree are almost always rows where the artefact exists but is not where a first-time reader would look, and that is itself a finding for the record.
