Review does four jobs, and a machine can reach two of them

working 10 min Framed against the only open head-to-head review benchmark and one vendor's own argument for why review is the bottleneck. · review-droid-benchmark

The one-minute version

  • Review is not one activity. It is four jobs that happen to share a text box: catch defects, enforce a written standard, transfer knowledge, and decide whether the change should exist.
  • Two of them need only the code and a rule. Two of them need a person who was in the room where the requirement was set, and no reviewer that arrives at the diff has been.
  • So the question is never "is the AI reviewer good". It is which two jobs are you handing it, and who still owns the other two.
  • The failure the tools are aimed at is lazy LGTM: approving a change because reading it costs more than the risk feels like it is worth. An automated first pass moves that threshold. It does not remove it.

1. Four jobs sharing one text box

Every code review you have ever done was doing more than one thing at once, which is why arguments about review are so hard to settle: two people defending "review" are often defending different jobs.

Separate them and the automation question becomes answerable. A defect is a claim about the code: given this input, this line does the wrong thing. A standard is a claim about the repository: we do it this way here. Knowledge transfer is a claim about the people: after this review, one more person understands this subsystem. Intent is a claim about the product: this change should exist, in this shape, now.

The AutoCommenter row is the interesting one, because it is the job people assume a machine cannot do and it is the job a machine does best. Google reports that of the fifty most frequently predicted best-practice violations, 33 - two thirds - are "beyond the scope of traditional static analysis", and that the system produced comments for 330 distinct best-practice URLs. A standard becomes machine-enforceable the moment somebody writes it down, and 0640 is that sentence turned into a file.

The reframing You are not buying a reviewer. You are moving two of review's four jobs onto a machine so that the two it cannot do get the attention they never had.

2. Lazy LGTM is the failure being aimed at

Cognition, which sells a review product, names the human failure mode directly: when pull requests are small they are easy to read and argue about, and "this approach deteriorates rapidly during large reviews". They call it the Lazy LGTM problem - approving a change because the cost of reading it has passed what the risk feels like it is worth.

That is a real thing you have done. It is also a vendor's framing of a market, and the same post argues that "code review - not code generation - is now the bottleneck to shipping great products" without publishing a measurement of it. Keep the mechanism and label the claim.

MARKETING. "Review is the bottleneck" is an argument, not a measurement. Cognition's post supplies no numbers at all; its companion engineering post supplies the closest thing, that they "merged 659 Devin PRs into our own codebase, up from 154 in our best week in 2025" and that "the bottleneck shifted from writing code to reviewing it". That is one company's own experience, self-reported, with no comparison group. For it to be evidence you would want review latency and defect-escape rate before and after, from somebody who is not selling the reviewer. The Staff AI Engineer, lesson 0000 is the hub's method for reading a claim like this one, and this module leans on it three times.

3. The first pass changes the volume, not the reading

An automated first pass does not remove the moment where a human decides. It moves it, and it adds a second queue: the comments themselves now have to be read.

How much reading that is depends almost entirely on which reviewer you install, and the spread is larger than anyone expects. Across the same fifty pull requests, six reviewers emitted between 54 and 178 comments - a factor of 3.3 - while catching between 39 and 103 real bugs.

That volume is the constraint the rest of this module keeps running into. A reviewer nobody reads has a catch rate of zero whatever a benchmark measured, so before you tune anything, work out how many comments a week your team will actually absorb.

4. What you actually decide

Everything after this page is a consequence of one choice: which of the four jobs you are handing over. Hand over defects and you need to know the catch rate, which is 0610 and 0620. Hand over the standard and you need to write it down, which is 0640. Keep intent and you need a gate that makes a human look, which is 0650.

Practice about 20 minutes, with your team's pull requests

Practice 1working

Open the last ten merged pull requests in the repository you picked in lesson 0000, and read every human review comment on them.

Classify each comment into exactly one of the four jobs: defect, standard, knowledge transfer, or intent. A comment that does two things gets split into two.

Then work out what share of the comments fall inside the boundary in the figure above, and what share fall outside it. That share is the ceiling on what an automated reviewer could ever have written for you.

Hint

The hard calls are the ones phrased as questions. "Why does this need a cache?" is intent. "Does this handle the empty list?" is a defect stated politely. Sort by what the reviewer would have had to know, not by the wording.

Show what the classification usually turns up
  1. Count the defect comments. These are the ones an automated reviewer would compete with directly, and lesson 0610 tells you what share of them a machine reaches at all.
  2. Count the standard comments. Every one of these is a rule somebody is repeating by hand. Each is a candidate line in the rules file you write in 0640, and the test is whether you have left it more than once.
  3. Count the intent comments. These stay with a human whatever you install. If there are none in ten pull requests, that is not a sign your reviews are efficient - it is the lazy LGTM this page names, and the automated pass will make it easier rather than harder.
  4. Take the ratio. Inside the boundary divided by all comments is the largest fraction of your review load that could move.

Sanity check. A team whose comments are almost all defects and standards is one whose reviews were already mechanical, and it should expect a large gain and should worry about the intent job nobody is doing. A team with a third of its comments on intent should expect the automated pass to change its week very little, and that is the correct expectation rather than a disappointing one.

Your teacher. If the four jobs still feel like one, hand your agent a real pull request from your own repository along with its linked issue, and ask it to write the review twice: once using only the diff, then once using the issue as well. The comments that appear only in the second pass are the intent job, and they are the ones you are keeping.
