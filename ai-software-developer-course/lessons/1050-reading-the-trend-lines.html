Capability and delivery are measured separately, and they disagree

frontier 10 min Five measurements, each read for the stage of the chain it actually looked at. · METR task horizons · NBER w35275 · Developer survey

The one-minute version

  • Nearly every argument about where this is going is two people quoting measurements of different stages of the same chain and assuming they contradict.
  • The capability measurements look at writing: a benchmark of real issues, and the length of task a model finishes half the time. Both rose steeply.
  • The delivery measurement looks at shipping: commits, projects and releases across more than 100,000 developers. It rose far less, and the gap is the finding.
  • Two published horizon numbers disagree about the same model and about the doubling period. This page prints both, names both sources, and averages neither.

1. Four measurements, four different stages

Code is written, integrated, released, and used. Every measurement below looks at exactly one of those, and the disagreements dissolve the moment you pin each one to its stage.

2. The benchmark, and what saturation costs you

SWE-bench asked whether a model could resolve real GitHub issues: 2,294 instances drawn from 12 popular Python repositories, where resolving one "frequently requires understanding and coordinating changes across multiple functions, classes, and even files". The best model in the original paper resolved 1.96% of them.

That number is now historical, and the way it stopped being useful is the point. A benchmark saturates when the leaders resolve nearly all of it: the scores keep going up, the differences between models stop meaning anything, and a metric that cannot separate two models can no longer tell you which is better at your work.

This page prints the 1.96% starting figure and no trajectory, and the omission is deliberate. A current leaderboard is what a trajectory needs, and the public leaderboard did not return its table to the author's fetch, so every later point available here is vendor-reported on a benchmark the vendor also selects the harness for. A curve drawn from one measured point and a series of self-reported ones is not a measurement, however honest each point is. If you need the trajectory, fetch a leaderboard yourself and check what harness each row used before you compare two rows.

3. The metric that outlived it, and the number it disagrees about

The metric that survived saturation asks a different question: not what share of a fixed set a model resolves, but how long a task has to be before the model only finishes it half the time. That is the 50% time horizon, measured in human minutes, and it keeps discriminating after a pass rate has stopped.

METR's paper measures it over three suites - RE-Bench, HCAST, and 66 novel shorter tasks - and reports a horizon of around 50 minutes for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, with the horizon doubling approximately every seven months since 2019, adding that "the trend may have accelerated in 2024".

Anthropic, writing about its own models, reports a different series: about four minutes for Claude Opus 3, about 90 minutes for Claude Sonnet 3.7, and 12 hours for Claude Opus 4.6, doubling "roughly every four months".

Put the two accounts beside each other and they disagree twice: about the same model, and about the rate. Both numbers are published, both are attributed, and averaging them would produce a figure nobody measured.

The longest figure in the vendor's account carries its own limit, and it is worth quoting rather than paraphrasing: a preview model could work for "at least" 16 hours, which the source itself says is "at the upper end of what [METR] can measure without new tasks". That is a statement about the measuring instrument, not about the model. A horizon reported at the ceiling of the ruler is a lower bound with an unknown top, and quoting it as a capability level is the single easiest mistake to make with this evidence.

4. The measurement that points the other way

Against all of that sits one study of what happened to shipped software. Demirer, Musolff and Yang matched more than 100,000 GitHub developers with their AI usage telemetry and followed each generation of tool down the production chain.

Coding activity rose in steps - 40% under autocomplete, 140% under interactive agents, 180% under autonomous ones. Then the same 180% effect fell to 50% for the number of projects and 30% for actual releases, with an estimated elasticity of substitution of 0.25 between AI and human effort, which the authors read as strong complementarity rather than substitution. A separate check across four app marketplaces found a moderate rise in new apps and no rise in total usage. Lesson 1000 draws that cascade under the loop, because the narrow end of it lines up exactly with the gate.

Nothing here contradicts the capability measurements. A model that finishes longer tasks and a pipeline that ships slightly more releases are both true at once, and the weak-link reading is the one the authors themselves give: the gains are capped by the least-improved stage.

5. What the people using it report

The fifth measurement is not on the chain at all. It asks practitioners what they think, which is evidence about adoption and about trust rather than about output.

6. How to read the next one

You will be handed a number about this every week. Five questions settle nearly all of them, and they are worth running in order.

Check yourself

Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before revealing.

Two published sources give a different task horizon for the same model. What should a page do with that?
The two figures come from different measurements, so the midpoint is a number nobody measured and cannot be traced to a method. The first option is a defensible reading and still discards evidence rather than labelling it. The second discards the source that published its method for the source that did not. Printing both with their provenance is what lets a reader do the discarding themselves.
Somebody says a benchmark result and a productivity study contradict each other. What is usually true instead?
A benchmark measures whether code gets written correctly and a productivity study measures what reached users, so the two can both rise at very different rates with no contradiction at all. Saturation is a real problem but it makes a benchmark uninformative rather than contradictory. The survey is the source that measures belief - the economics paper measured commits, projects and releases.

Practice about 25 minutes, with one claim you have been sent

Practice 1frontier

Find one claim about AI and software productivity that somebody has actually sent you - a slide, a post, a vendor page, a line in a planning document.

Run the five questions against it and write one line for each. Where a question has no answer in the source, write "not published" rather than guessing.

Then place it on the chain: written, integrated, released, or used. Finally, name the one further measurement that would change your reading, and say whether anybody could run it.

Hint

The fastest tell is the baseline. A claim phrased as a multiple - "three times faster", "five times the output" - with no stated before is usually a survey of impressions rather than a measurement, and the source often says so in a sentence nobody quotes.

Show the five questions run against one claim
  1. Take a self-reported multiplier as the worked example. One vendor polled 130 of its own research staff and reports a median estimate of about four times the output, then adds that the true uplift was "somewhat lower".
  2. Who. The vendor, about its own tool, and it says so plainly. Not disqualifying, and worth writing down.
  3. Population. 130 people, all inside one organisation, all users of the tool. That is a population and it is not a general one.
  4. Baseline. None. It is a median of estimates rather than a measurement against a before, which is exactly why the source caveats it itself.
  5. Which stage. Written, and self-assessed. It says nothing about integration or release.
  6. What would overturn it. The same population's actual output measured over the same period. That is runnable, and it is not published.

Sanity check. Your five lines should include at least one "not published". A claim that answers all five with published detail is rare enough that finding one is itself worth noting - and it usually turns out to be a paper rather than a post.

Your teacher. Hand your agent the claim you analysed and ask it to argue the opposite reading using only the source's own words. If it can, the claim was weaker than it looked. If it cannot, ask it what one measurement would settle the disagreement - that answer is usually more useful than the analysis.
